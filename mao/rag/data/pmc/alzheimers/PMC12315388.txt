Introduction Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common form of dementia, accounting for 60–70% of cases worldwide. The number of people living with dementia is expected to reach 139 million by 2050 [ 1 ]. Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is often considered an early stage of AD and a transitional phase from normal aging to Alzheimer’s disease [ 2 ]. Early diagnosis and intervention at the MCI stage can delay the progression to AD [ 3 ]. Clinically, structural magnetic resonance imaging (sMRI) plays a vital role in diagnosing AD, as it provides key measurements that help identify structural abnormalities and monitor brain atrophy over time [ 4 – 6 ]. Despite the critical importance of accurate and timely diagnosis of AD and MCI, traditional diagnostic methods heavily rely on manual assessment by physicians, which is both time-intensive and costly [ 7 ]. The rise of deep learning in computer vision has transformed the medical field, leading to substantial efforts to develop automated diagnostic systems that can efficiently and accurately process medical images for disease detection [ 8 – 11 ]. Earlier studies often relied on 2D slice-based approaches, primarily due to their lower computational demands and the widespread availability of 2D convolutional neural networks at the time [ 12 , 13 ]. These methods helped demonstrate the feasibility of deep learning in brain imaging analysis. However, such approaches inherently suffer from loss of spatial continuity, as they treat each slice independently and fail to capture inter-slice relationships—limiting their capacity to model the full 3D brain structure. To enhance localization and reduce redundancy, some methods adopt region-of-interest (ROI)-based strategies, focusing on specific brain areas known to be associated with AD [ 14 ]. While this can improve sensitivity to established biomarkers, it also introduces strong reliance on prior anatomical knowledge and risks overlooking diffuse or atypical lesion patterns. Other approaches leverage patch-based modeling, which processes small 3D sub-volumes to extract fine-grained local features [ 15 ]. These models maintain partial 3D context, but they often struggle to aggregate patch-level information into a globally consistent representation, reducing their effectiveness for whole-brain classification. Meanwhile, full 3D volume-based methods have emerged as a promising direction, as they retain the complete spatial context of brain anatomy [ 16 ]. However, these models typically require substantial computational resources and are more susceptible to training instability in data-limited scenarios. Motivated by these limitations, we introduce the TA-SSM NET model, designed to explore potential improvements in the accuracy and efficiency of MRI-based AD and MCI diagnosis. Unlike traditional methods that rely on 2D slices or domain-specific regional segmentation, our model captures the comprehensive spatial complexity of 3D MRI scans without neglecting potential lesion sites. The TA-SSM NET model computes feature dependencies in three orientations-forward, backward, and vertical-enabling a more holistic representation of spatial and contextual information. To better integrate directional information, we incorporate an attention-based fusion mechanism that adaptively weights features, potentially offering a more coherent representation than basic summation. Leveraging Mamba’s SSM, the proposed model enhances processing speed and classification accuracy while handling the complexities of medical imaging. We validated the TA-SSM NET model using MRI data from ADNI database, which includes 300 AD subjects, 400 MCI subjects, and 400 cognitively normal controls. The model achieved 90.2% accuracy in detecting MCI and 95.8% accuracy in identifying AD, significantly outperforming traditional methods. These results suggest that the model holds promise for assisting in the early detection of AD and MCI and may contribute to future clinical decision-making support. Related work In this section, we review key approaches in computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) of AD and MCI using MRI data. Existing approaches can be broadly categorized into four directions: 2D slice-based methods, region-of-interest (ROI)-based modeling, patch-based 3D methods, and full-volume 3D approaches. Each class of methods presents specific advantages and limitations, which we critically analyze below. 2D slice methods In the early stages of computer-aided diagnosis for Alzheimer’s disease and mild cognitive impairment, many methods focused on extracting features from three-dimensional MRI scans by dividing them into two-dimensional slices. Lyu introduced a cross-domain transfer learning technique utilizing a pre-trained Vision Transformer (ViT) model to transfer knowledge from natural images to brain imaging data. The method applies the ViT architecture to structural MRI scans, enabling effective feature learning even under limited data availability conditions [ 13 ]. However, the method relies on pretraining in a non-medical domain, and its ability to generalize across diverse neuroimaging datasets remains uncertain. Wang et al. proposed a joint learning framework that combines cross-modal synthesis with diagnostic classification for Alzheimer’s disease. Their model is designed to extract shared modality information across imaging domains, effectively modeling both intra-slice and inter-slice relationships within MRI data [ 17 ]. Nonetheless, the framework requires access to multiple imaging modalities, which may limit its applicability in clinical scenarios where only single-modality data is available. Lozupone et al. introduced the AXIAL model, which applies 2D convolutional neural networks directly to 3D MRI data. The model incorporates attention mechanisms to improve interpretability and explicitly models both intra-slice and inter-slice dependencies for localized Alzheimer’s disease diagnosis [ 18 ]. Despite this, the use of 2D CNNs may constrain the model’s ability to fully exploit the 3D spatial continuity of brain structures. Consequently, 2D methods are increasingly being replaced by volumetric models capable of retaining anatomical coherence Regional feature extraction methods To reduce redundancy and focus on biologically relevant regions, regions of interest (ROIs) based methods extract features from anatomically defined structures such as the hippocampus, known to correlate with AD pathology. As a result, some methods focus on extracting features from pre-identified ROIs based on prior biological knowledge [ 19 ]. Although computationally efficient, the approach relies on handcrafted feature extraction and lacks the adaptive representation learning capabilities of deep learning models. 3D patch methods To address the limitations of 2D slice and ROI-based approaches, some researchers have investigated patch-based 3D modeling, in which MRI scans are divided into smaller local regions that serve as individual inputs to learning algorithms. This strategy aims to capture fine-grained anatomical variations across different brain regions. Ahmed et al. proposed an ensemble learning framework for Alzheimer’s disease classification using patch-based classifiers. In their method, structural MRI scans are segmented into multiple patches, each processed by an individual classifier. The outputs of these classifiers are then aggregated to generate the final prediction [ 23 ]. While the ensemble strategy improves robustness, the independent processing of patches may hinder the model’s ability to capture long-range dependencies and global anatomical context. Ren et al. applied Mamba-based state space models to 3D MRI Alzheimer’s disease classification, effectively capturing long-range spatial dependencies among 3D MRI voxels through state space modeling. Their method achieved 87.18% accuracy on the CN vs AD classification task using single-modal MRI, demonstrating that the Mamba architecture offers superior computational efficiency and stronger sequential modeling capabilities compared to traditional CNNs when processing high-dimensional medical images [ 24 ]. Zhu et al. developed a Dual Attention Multi-Instance Deep Learning (DA-MIDL) framework for structural MRI-based Alzheimer’s diagnosis. Their approach utilizes a T-test to select discriminative patch locations, which are then passed through a dual attention mechanism incorporating both spatial and channel-wise attention. The method applies a multi-instance learning scheme to associate local features with overall diagnostic outcomes, without requiring detailed voxel-level annotations [ 25 ]. However, the model’s reliance on predefined statistical selection of patch locations may limit adaptability across datasets with different anatomical distributions or noise characteristics. Patch-based models preserve local 3D context and improve resolution, but they often struggle to capture global anatomical relationships. Moreover, some models rely on statistical preselection of patch locations, which may not generalize across populations or scanners. Effective integration of local patch features into a coherent whole-brain representation remains an open challenge. Advantages and challenges of 3D models Three-dimensional models have been developed to process entire brain volumes in MRI data, with the aim of capturing fine-grained structural changes and modeling spatial dependencies across the full anatomical context. These methods provide an alternative to slice-based or region-focused approaches by preserving spatial continuity throughout the brain volume. One study integrated attention mechanisms with parallel dilated convolutions to improve feature extraction from brain MRI scans. The model was evaluated on MCI classification tasks and demonstrated improved detection accuracy compared to baseline configurations [ 26 ]. However, the increased architectural complexity introduced by attention and dilation may result in higher computational overhead and longer training times. Another work proposed an interpretable 3D residual self-attention neural network for sMRI-based Alzheimer’s diagnosis. The model includes a mechanism designed to identify the contribution of specific brain regions to the final prediction, enabling localized atrophy analysis [ 1 ]. Despite its interpretability, the approach may still be constrained by the limited availability of labeled data and the inherent difficulty of validating voxel-level importance in clinical settings. With increased computational capabilities, full-volume 3D models have gained popularity due to their ability to preserve global spatial continuity. However, the benefits come at a cost: full 3D models are computationally intensive, prone to overfitting in small datasets, and often suffer from inefficiencies in capturing long-range dependencies. Methodology One of the primary challenges in AD and MCI detection is the comprehensive capture and utilization of key information from MRI images. Traditional methods often rely on segmenting 3D MRI scans into 2D slices, which leads to the loss of spatial information and limits the model’s ability to fully capture the 3D structure of the brain. The TA-SSM NET model addresses this issue by introducing tri-directional feature extraction, capturing rich spatial information from forward, backward, and vertical directions in 3D MRI images. By processing data from multiple directions, the model ensures that the full spatial context of the brain’s structural changes is maintained, effectively mitigating the problem of spatial information loss. The integration of an attention mechanism ensures the dynamic fusion of these multi-directional features, which helps address the challenges of insufficient feature integration present in previous methods. This results in a more effective representation of the spatial relationships and enables the model to capture subtle brain changes, significantly improving both diagnostic accuracy and efficiency. Furthermore, the model incorporates Mamba’s Structured State-Space Model, which preserves whole-brain spatial correlations while capturing both spatial and contextual information from MRI images. The SSM enhances the model’s ability to handle complex 3D MRI data by preserving spatial relationships across the entire brain, which traditional methods often fail to do due to their reliance on 2D slices. This enhancement improves the model’s efficiency in processing 3D data, enabling more accurate and reliable results in the diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease and mild cognitive impairment. Dataset construction In this study, data from the ADNI database ( http://adni.loni.usc.edu ) were used, consisting of baseline MRI scans from the ADNI-1, ADNI-2, and ADNI-3 datasets. Based on clinical standards including the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) scale, the subject in the ADNI dataset were categorized into three groups: AD, MCI, and cognitively normal (CN). The ADNI dataset utilized in this study consists of 300 AD subjects, 400 MCI subjects, and 400 CN subjects. Limitations and future works While TA-SSM NET demonstrates strong performance in 3D MRI-based classification tasks, it also has several inherent limitations that warrant further attention. First, the model still requires substantial computational resources during training, especially due to the tri-directional encoding and the relatively large embedding size and depth. This may limit its scalability in low-resource environments. Second, although the tri-directional design improves spatial representation, it treats each directional encoding independently before fusion, which may lead to redundancy or incomplete cross-directional interaction. Additionally, the model currently lacks built-in interpretability mechanisms, making it difficult to visualize which brain regions most influence the diagnostic decision. To address these limitations, future work will focus on several directions. One goal is to develop a lightweight variant of TA-SSM NET with fewer parameters, enabling faster inference and broader deployment in clinical settings. Another goal is to explore more effective cross-directional attention schemes that can capture shared and complementary information between directional representations more holistically. Furthermore, we aim to extend the model to support multimodal data fusion, including PET imaging, genetic biomarkers, and clinical assessments. This would provide a more holistic view of disease progression, particularly for early-stage Alzheimer’s detection. Finally, enhancing model interpretability remains a critical direction, such as incorporating saliency-based methods or attention map visualizations to better support clinical trust and decision-making. Conclusion In this paper, we present the TA-SSM NET model, designed to improve the accuracy and efficiency of AD and MCI diagnosis using MRI scans. By utilizing a tri-directional attention mechanism and a structured state-space model, our method effectively captures spatial dependencies in 3D MRI data, overcoming the limitations of traditional 2D slice-based and ROI-based methods. The model’s attention-based fusion mechanism ensures the selective integration of the most relevant features from different orientations, enhancing classification performance. The experimental results demonstrate the superior performance of TA-SSM NET in comparison to existing methods. The model achieved an accuracy of 95.83% for AD detection and 90.24% for MCI detection, surpassing existing approaches. Notably, the tri-directional attention mechanism played a crucial role in these results, enabling the model to focus on subtle structural changes, particularly in the more challenging MCI classification task. Furthermore, the integration of SSM improved computational efficiency, making the model suitable for large-scale datasets while maintaining high accuracy. TA-SSM NET provides a robust and efficient solution for MRI-based diagnosis of AD and MCI, demonstrating strong potential for real-world clinical applications. Future work will focus on extending the model to incorporate multimodal data fusion and improving interpretability for enhanced clinical integration.

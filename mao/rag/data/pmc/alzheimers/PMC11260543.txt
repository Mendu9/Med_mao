Introduction The global trend towards increased longevity is evident, with life expectancy more than doubling since 1900 due to significant advancements in healthcare, nutrition, and living conditions. As a result, older individuals now make up a larger share of the world’s population ( Roberts, 2024 ). As people age, natural changes in the brain can impact cognitive function and memory, raising the risk of conditions like dementia. Detecting early dementia stages, such as mild cognitive impairment (MCI), is crucial as most forms of dementia progress irreversibly. MCI could be a pivotal focus for interventions, both pharmacologic and non-pharmacologic, aimed at delaying dementia onset ( Farlow, 2009 ). MCI can bridge age-related cognitive decline and Alzheimer’s disease (AD), possibly progressing to AD. In 2024, about 6.9 million Americans aged 65 and above have Alzheimer’s, 73% of them aged 75 or older ( Rajan et al., 2021 ). Despite extensive research efforts, AD remains without a cure, underscoring the complexity and challenges associated with treating this prevalent form of dementia ( Korczyn and Grinberg, 2024 ). Maintaining cognitive health through lifestyle choices is crucial for preserving mental acuity and overall well-being throughout life. Understanding normal brain ageing is crucial for healthy ageing initiatives and early detection of conditions like dementia ( MacDonald and Pike, 2021 ). While MCI and AD have been extensively studied, clarity is lacking on factors for effective dementia prevention in older individuals. Some maintain cognitive function due to genetics, cognitive engagement, and lifestyle choices, emphasizing both innate resilience and proactive maintenance. Moreover, over time, changes to our genes caused by the environment can raise the risk of dementia, but these changes by themselves are mostly inactive. If enough of these changes build up, they can disrupt gene function enough to cause dementia ( Maloney and Lahiri, 2016 ). Exploring genetics, environment, and lifestyle can provide insights into dementia prevention ( Lahiri and Maloney, 2010 ). Gaps exist in understanding lifestyle impacts on brain regions, especially concerning dementia. Further research is needed on age-related changes and disease pathology’s interaction in dementia-affected brain regions. We are conducting a longitudinal study focused on the elderly population of Northwest Texas, aiming to recruit both healthy participants and individuals with MCI and AD. Our study involves two visits spaced six months apart, during which participants will undergo psychosocial surveys, comprehensive blood profiling, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans, and anthropometric measurements to assess various aspects of their health and cognitive function over time. MRI has become indispensable in the follow-up, diagnosis, and assessment of treatment effectiveness in dementia, particularly with the advent of 3T MRI and its advanced techniques ( Živanović et al., 2023 ). Studying brain shrinkage with MRI in AD patients is vital for tracking cognitive decline and understanding neurodegenerative processes to assess therapeutic interventions’ effectiveness. Our objective is to investigate age and disease-related brain atrophies and correlate lifestyle factors like sleep, exercise, nutrition, and stress with dementia-affected brain regions. We hypothesize that these factors positively influence brain structure and function, promoting healthy brain ageing and reducing cognitive decline in the elderly. Material and Methods: Study Design: Our original research study is a longitudinal one that spans six months ( Basu et al., 2023 ). The participants are recruited with an initial telephonic interview. Once the eligibility of participants is ensured, they are invited for visit 1. In study visit 1, the participants explained the study design, they signed informed consent, and healthy individuals were subjected to the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) test ( Nasreddine et al., 2005 ). Individuals with MCI/AD are not administered any cognitive test as they become part of the study with an established diagnosis. Anthropometric measurements are taken of the participants, blood is drawn for basic blood profile and for genetic testing that would be in the advanced stages of our study. Psychosocial and demographic surveys that include the Functional Autonomy Survey (SMAF) questionnaire ( Hébert et al., 1988 ), The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) ( Marteau and Bekker, 1992 ), The Sleep Disturbance Questionnaire (SDQ) ( Douglass et al., 1994 ), The Spirituality Scale ( Davis et al., 2009 ), The Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly (PASE) ( Washburn et al., 1993 ), The Fruit and Vegetable Consumption Questionnaire (FVCQ) ( Bogers et al., 2004 ), and The Personal Wellbeing Index (PWI) ( Tomyn et al., 2013 ) are administered to gain insight into the lifestyles of the participants. The participants are requested to undergo Magnetic Resonance Imaging as the last task of visit 1. Everything mentioned above is repeated in study visit 2, which is six months apart from visit 1, except for the MoCA assessment, refer to Figure 1 . Recruitment of Participants: Our recruitment strategy aims for 4000 healthy adults and 500 with MCI/AD in West Texas, using diverse channels like local newspapers, community magazines, and Latino publications. Social media, notably Facebook ads, target the tech-savvy ageing population, while a postcard campaign emphasizes study benefits( Brownell et al., 2024 ). Partnerships with organizations such as the Alzheimer’s Association and city programs boost recruitment. A refer-a-friend initiative and database development for Latino service providers support the plan. We present findings on 15 cognitively healthy individuals and 5 with MCI/AD in this ongoing study. The inclusion criteria for this study target individuals aged between 60 and 90 years who primarily reside in West Texas. Prospective participants must demonstrate proficiency in either English or Spanish to answer oral questions. The healthy group comprises individuals without specific diagnoses, including but not limited to AD, Parkinson’s disease, brain tumors, Huntington’s disease, uncontrolled thyroid disease, intellectual or developmental disability, and human immunodeficiency virus. Conversely, the control groups consist of individuals diagnosed with MCI and/or AD. The inclusion criteria for this study target individuals aged between 60 and 90 years who primarily reside in the West Texas region, particularly in and around Lubbock. Prospective participants must demonstrate proficiency in either English or Spanish to answer oral questions. The healthy group comprises individuals without specific diagnoses, including but not limited to Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, brain tumors, Huntington’s disease, and others as outlined. Conversely, the control groups consist of individuals diagnosed with MCI and/or Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and related dementia (ADRD). These inclusion criteria serve to delineate the target population for the study while also ensuring a suitable control group for comparison and analysis. Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA): A concise and sensitive cognitive screening tool is essential for navigating the ambiguous boundary between normal ageing, MCI, and mild dementia. The MoCA ( Nasreddine et al., 2005 ) stands out as an innovative international tool designed for the detection of MCI and mild AD, making it potentially suitable for this purpose ( Freitas et al., 2013 ). The MoCA assesses general cognitive function, with a normal range of 26-30 points, established from a study of 90 healthy Canadian controls, average age of 72.84 years, mean education of 13.33 years, with a one-point education correction for individuals with 12 years or less of education( Nasreddine et al., 2005 ). According to Dr. Nasreddine’s official website, cognitive impairment severity is graded as follows: 18-25 for mild, 10-17 for moderate, and less than 10 for severe impairment (refer to Table 1 ). In our study, only cognitively healthy individuals take the MoCA test. Those who fail are referred to a geriatrician/neurologist for diagnosis. The MoCA and MMSE are widely used in epidemiologic studies to identify MCI, with the MoCA favored due to its efficacy, especially in healthy subjects over 60, aligning better with MCI screening criteria than the MMSE ( Ciesielska et al., 2016 ; Jia et al., 2021 ; Siqueira et al., 2019 ). Anthropometric Measurements: The ageing population, prone to chronic illness and disability, brings a significant demographic shift. Anthropometric measures, simple, portable, non-intrusive, and cost-effective, provide valuable tools in geriatric healthcare. They can guide preventive strategies and medical treatments for older adults ( Padilla et al., 2021 ). In our study, the anthropometric measurements, including weight, height, body mass index, fat percentage, fat mass, fat-free mass, total body water, and basic metabolic rate, are taken for each participant. Blood Sample Collection: Blood samples collected from each participant facilitate the assessment of vital health markers, encompassing glucose levels, hemoglobin A1C, cholesterol profile, thyroid function, and micronutrient status. Additionally, these samples serve as a valuable resource for genetic studies, DNA damage analysis, and future investigations into cell-free biomarkers, augmenting the depth and scope of research endeavors. Psychosocial and demographic Surveys: Participants undergo a series of psychosocial and demographic surveys, including SMAF( Hébert et al., 1988 ), STAI( Marteau and Bekker, 1992 ), SDQ ( Douglass et al., 1994 ), Spirituality Scale ( Davis et al., 2009 ), PASE( Washburn et al., 1993 ), FVCQ ( Bogers et al., 2004 ), and PWI ( Tomyn et al., 2013 ), providing insights into the lifestyles of study participants. However, this study focuses on the STAI, SDQ, PASE, and FVCQ to examine the correlation between sleep, stress, physical activity, and nutrition with brain shrinkage in both healthy individuals and those with MCI/AD. Responses are recorded in Qualtrics and analyzed for data interpretation. Brain Scans: All MRIs were conducted using a 3 Tesla magnet and a 48-channel head coil scanner located at the Radiology Department of the University Medical Center in Lubbock, TX. MRIs of both coronal and axial planes for the 3D T1 GRE series were uploaded to the NeuroQuant server, a cloud-based AI software. NeuroQuant ® is an FDA-cleared medical software that quantitatively analyzes brain MRI scans by comparing brain volume to normative data adjusted for age, sex, and intracranial volume ( Louis et al., 2020 ),( Bash and Lawrence Tanenbaum, 2021 ). The NeuroQuant server generated age-related brain atrophy reports for all participants and the reports segmented, quantified, and compared brain structures for conditions like dementia. It provided volumetric data on various brain regions, including the Hippocampi, Entorhinal Cortex, Ventricles, Temporal Cortex, Parietal Cortex, Frontal Cortex, Occipital Cortex, Anterior Cingulate Cortex, Posterior Cingulate Cortex, and Hippocampal Occupancy Score (HOC) for assessing age and disease-related atrophy. The HOC is a calculated measure utilized to estimate mesial temporal lobe atrophy, factoring in hippocampal volume loss and the resultant ex-vacuo dilatation of the inferior lateral ventricle (ILV). It is computed as the ratio of hippocampal volume to the combined volumes of the hippocampus and ILV in each hemisphere ( Heister et al., 2011 ), with subsequent averaging. Statistical Analysis: Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM’s SPSS Statistical software. For healthy participants, a correlation matrix was constructed to assess the relationships among MoCA score, age, whole brain volume, hippocampal occupancy score, volumes of various brain regions (listed in Table 2 ), and additionally, lifestyle factor scores obtained from Qualtrics, including sleep score, fruit and vegetable consumption score, stress score, and physical activity score, was included in the analysis. Another correlation matrix with the same variable except the MoCA score was constructed for individuals with MCI/AD. Pearson’s correlation was performed using these scores to assess the relationship between lifestyle factors and brain atrophy. Both whole brain volume and volumes of all the brain regions quantified by NeuroQuant server ( Heo et al., 2022 ) were utilized for the correlations. Results: Our study aimed to explore the relationship between brain atrophy and lifestyle factors in twenty participants, comprising seven males and thirteen females aged 62-87, with a mean age of 68, refer to Figure 3 for the structure and function of the brain regions under study. Five participants had MCI/AD, while fifteen were cognitively healthy based on MoCA scores. The MCI/AD group consisted of four Whites and one Hispanic, while the healthy group included fourteen Whites and one Hispanic. Anthropometric measurements and blood profile data analysis revealed no significant findings regarding ageing and cognitive decline in MCI/AD, highlighting the complexity of these processes. However, both MCI/AD and healthy populations exhibited a negative correlation between age and hippocampal volume, indicating hippocampal shrinkage with age leading to ventricle enlargement. Moreover, both superior and inferior lateral ventricles showed a negative relationship with age in healthy individuals. Discussion: The goal of our study was to understand mechanistic links between brain atrophy and key lifestyle factors, such as sleep deprivation, exercise, diet, and stress in rural elderly. Our correlative analysis in subjects with MCI/AD revealed that significantly reduced hippocampal volume reduced total brain levels and parietal cortex volume. Additionally, widespread atrophy across cortical regions, including the entorhinal cortex, temporal cortex, frontal cortex, and anterior cingulate cortex, underscores the multi-faceted nature of cognitive impairment in dementia. Conversely, increased lateral ventricle volumes correlate with greater atrophy in key brain regions associated with dementia pathology. In cognitively healthy participants, ventricle enlargement, particularly superior lateral ventricles, is linked to lower cognitive scores, indicating a potential association between ventricular size and cognitive decline. In a study by Nestor and colleagues, ventricular enlargement, as measured objectively, appears to be a sensitive indicator of neuropathological changes linked to MCI and AD. Over six months, subjects with AD and MCI exhibited greater absolute ventricular volumes and enlargement compared to age-matched controls. Ventricular enlargement also showed sensitivity in distinguishing between stable MCI subjects and those progressing to AD ( Nestor et al., 2008 ). In our study, age correlates negatively with hippocampal volume but positively with ventricle volumes, suggesting age-related volumetric changes in memory-related regions. Moreover, healthy subjects’ hippocampal volume shows positive correlations with parietal cortex volume, indicating an interplay between memory-processing structures. Whole brain volume correlates positively with multiple cortical regions, including the entorhinal cortex, temporal cortex, parietal cortex, frontal cortex, occipital cortex, and anterior/posterior cingulate cortex, highlighting widespread cortical involvement in age-related atrophy. These findings underscore the complex structural changes occurring in various brain regions during healthy ageing and their potential impact on cognitive function. In our study, we focused on four major lifestyle factors, Sleep, Diet, Exercise, and Stress in MCI/AD and healthy subjects. Sleep and MCI/AD: Reduced sleep is directly correlated with reduced hippocampal volume, posterior cingulate cortex, and total brain weight. These findings suggest a potential link between sleep disturbances and brain structure alterations in MCI/AD. These observations offer insights into interventions targeting sleep quality to mitigate cognitive decline. A similar finding was presented in a longitudinal study on cognitively normal elderly individuals that revealed a significant increase in tissue volume loss rates in two brain regions associated with cognitive decline and dementia, namely the hippocampus and posterior cingulate cortex, due to poor sleep quality. The results highlighted the heightened vulnerability of the hippocampus and posterior cingulate to the detrimental effects of poor sleep in older adults without cognitive impairment ( Liu et al., 2022 ). Such findings enhance our comprehension of the intricate relationship between sleep and cognition. They show that maintaining a regular sleep pattern of at least 7 hours per night enhances working memory and response inhibition in healthy adults ( Zimmerman et al., 2024 ). Sleep in both healthy individuals and those with MCI or Alzheimer’s is controlled by the brain. Melatonin, a natural hormone produced mainly by the pineal gland in the brain, helps manage the sleep-wake cycle and circadian rhythm ( Arendt and Aulinas, 2019 ). Released during the dark phase, melatonin regulates sleep by synchronizing neural and endocrine processes with daily light-dark changes, although its production decreases with age. Oral melatonin effectively treats jet lag and certain circadian rhythm sleep disorders and is believed to influence aging ( Lahiri, 1999 ; Song and Lahiri, 1997 ). Considering this, it is important to study and understand the brain changes that occur in individuals with normal aging or those with MCI/AD and how these changes affect the sleep cycle. Stress and MCI/AD: Stress is directly linked to lack of sleep and brain atrophy in MCI/AD patients. Sleep disturbances are strongly associated with increased stress levels, ultimately leading to atrophy in different regions of the brain. In individuals with MCI, higher levels of depressive symptoms, multimorbidity, and a sedentary lifestyle correlate with an increased likelihood of experiencing sleep disturbances. A study by Dan and colleagues underscores the elevated prevalence of sleep disturbances in MCI compared to cognitively healthy individuals, emphasizing the need for assessment and management of sleep in older adults with MCI ( Song et al., 2020 ). Diet and MCI/AD: Our study indicates that fruit and vegetable intake show a negative correlation with entorhinal cortex volume, suggesting potential benefits for brain health with a diet rich in these foods. Adopting a diet abundant in fruits and vegetables, managing sleep quality and stress levels, and engaging in regular physical activity may contribute to preserving brain structure and function. Recent epidemiological and clinical research indicates that lifestyle modifications, such as dietary adjustments and increased physical activity, present a promising approach to slowing and preventing cognitive decline and dementia. Age, the primary risk factor for dementia, correlates with diminished cellular bioenergetics and metabolic functions, underscoring the importance of a nutrient-rich diet for optimal brain health ( Chu et al., 2022 ). Foods commonly found in Mediterranean ( Panagiotakos et al., 2006 ), MIND( Morris et al., 2015 ), and DASH ( Chen et al., 2024 ) diets, such as fruits, leafy greens( Morris et al., 2018 ), fish( Kokubun et al., 2020 ), nuts( Morris et al., 2018 ), and olive oil( Morris et al., 2018 ), may play a role in preventing or delaying cognitive decline ( Chu et al., 2022 ; He et al., 2024 ). Exercise and MCI/AD: Lack of exercise is associated with reduced brain volume and atrophy in the hippocampus and other regions of the brain, indicating that higher physical exercise may improve cognitive function. Higher physical activity levels are associated with lower volumes of inferior lateral ventricles, implying a potential protective effect against brain atrophy. Gavin and colleagues’ study investigates the impact of physical exercise on hippocampal atrophy in MCI and AD. The primary analysis shows a positive effect of physical activity (PA) on total hippocampal volume (HV). However, subgroup analyses revealed that HV preservation is significant only in individuals with MCI, not in those with AD. Subgroup analyses also identify significant heterogeneity only within the MCI group for total and left HV. PA demonstrates a moderate, significant effect in preserving HV among MCI individuals, indicating therapeutic benefits, particularly in earlier disease stages ( Kress et al., 2024 ). Emerging evidence indicates that hypothalamic-pituitary adrenal-axis (HPAA) dysfunction may play a role in AD pathology. Lifestyle interventions like exercise, diet, sleep, and mindfulness practices show promise in modifying HPAA function and promoting cognitive health ( Watermeyer et al., 2021 ). Overall, our findings underscore the significance of lifestyle factors in maintaining brain health among healthy individuals. Limitations: The study has limitations that warrant consideration. Firstly, the dataset is small, reflecting an ongoing study aiming to recruit 4000 healthy individuals and 500 individuals with MCI/AD over five years. This limitation can be addressed in the future as recruitment progresses. Secondly, due to the small sample size, there was unequal representation from different ethnic groups, which can be rectified with increased enrollment efforts. Thirdly, the study is currently confined to West Texas, limiting the generalizability of findings. Future extensions of the study to encompass other regions of Texas would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the effects of lifestyle factors on elderly Texans’ health. Lastly, genetic testing and the analysis of blood-based biomarkers were not included in this study. Incorporating these wet lab studies in the future could elucidate the molecular and genetic impacts of adopting certain lifestyle factors. Plans are in place to integrate these aspects into future research endeavors. Conclusion: In conclusion, the findings from this study highlight the intricate interplay between lifestyle factors and brain health across different populations. In individuals with MCI/AD dietary habits, sleep quality, and physical activity are associated with brain atrophy, suggesting potential avenues for intervention to mitigate cognitive decline. Our data also suggest a positive association between sleep quality and brain health, as indicated by lower stress levels correlating with better sleep and potentially reduced risk of brain atrophy. Conversely, sleep disorders appear to be linked with increased stress levels, highlighting the importance of addressing sleep disturbances for maintaining optimal brain function. Moreover, higher fruit and vegetable intake, along with better sleep quality, and increased physical activity levels appear to be linked to preserved brain structure and function. Similarly, in healthy individuals, these lifestyle factors are associated with brain health, indicating their importance in maintaining cognitive function and preventing age-related brain changes. Overall, these findings underscore the importance of lifestyle interventions in promoting brain health across the lifespan. Further research is warranted to better understand the underlying mechanisms and to develop targeted strategies for optimizing brain health and preventing neurodegenerative diseases.

Introduction Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disorder biologically characterized by the extracellular deposition of amyloid-β (Aβ) and intracellular accumulation of phosphorylated tau (p-tau) neurofibrillary tangles in the brain [ 1 , 2 ]. These pathological changes are well-captured by cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers and, more recently, can also be reliably detected in plasma [ 3 ]. In CSF, a decreased Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio together with increased p-tau181 levels, compared to healthy subjects, are known to happen decades before the first symptoms appear [ 2 ]. These biomarkers in preclinical stages provide a crucial window for early disease detection and may, in the future, enable more effective therapeutic interventions at early stages. Although amyloid and tau biomarkers remain central to the biological definition of AD, they capture only part of its complex pathophysiology. The most recent criteria proposed by the Alzheimer’s Association in 2024 reinforce a biologically based framework for AD and explicitly acknowledge the importance of complementary pathways, including neuroinflammation and glial activation, across the disease continuum [ 4 ]. There is increasing evidence that neuroinflammation plays a central role in the pathogenesis of AD [ 5 ]. Gliosis is a nonspecific phenomenon that occurs as a response to any injury in the central nervous system and involves the activation and proliferation of glial cells [ 6 ]. When Aβ deposits in the brain, these plaques are surrounded by reactive astrocytes and activated microglia. While initially protective, their chronic reactivity drives sustained inflammation that may accelerate disease progression [ 5 , 6 ]. In the presence of AD pathology, astrocytes become reactive, undergoing profound molecular, functional, and morphological changes [ 6 ]. This reactivity can trigger inflammatory processes that could link both soluble and insoluble species of amyloid, as well as amyloid and tau pathology [ 5 – 8 ]. The development of biomarkers to assess astrocytosis, most notably glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP)-an intermediate filament protein predominantly expressed in astrocytes-has enabled the in vivo characterization of reactive astrocytes and their contribution to AD [ 9 ]. GFAP has been shown to predict amyloid PET positivity and to distinguish different stages of the disease [ 9 , 10 ], and the most recent AD diagnostic criteria acknowledge it as a nonspecific but biologically relevant marker of AD pathogenesis [ 4 ]. Notably, CSF GFAP levels are reliably replicated in blood, and plasma GFAP has consistently shown superior performance over CSF measures in detecting Aβ pathology in cognitively unimpaired (CU) individuals [ 9 ]. Building on this evidence, several recent studies have specifically highlighted plasma GFAP as a promising biomarker for detecting astrocyte activation in the preclinical stages of AD. Elevated plasma GFAP levels have been associated with Aβ burden in CU individuals [ 11 ], and longitudinal data indicate that such increases in plasma GFAP may precede cognitive decline by up to a decade, reinforcing its potential for early risk stratification [ 12 , 13 ]. However, most studies rely on ultrasensitive research assays, with limited data available from automated clinical platforms. Establishing the clinical value of astrocytic biomarkers such as GFAP is crucial for advancing both diagnosis and prognosis in AD, and may ultimately inform therapeutic strategies [ 9 ]. The recent approval of disease-modifying treatments has further increased the need for biomarker assays that are simple, reliable and readily applicable in clinical practice. In this context, we aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of plasma GFAP in CU individuals using the fully automated Lumipulse platform, which is widely available in many hospital laboratories worldwide. We further sought to examine its concordance with a highly sensitive research-based immunoassay, Single Molecule Array (SIMOA). Materials and methods Study participants This research was performed with 204 volunteers from the ‘Valdecilla Cohort for the Study of Memory and Brain Aging’ at the Memory Unit of the Marqués de Valdecilla University Hospital (Santander, Spain), and it was approved by the hospital´s ethics committee (Internal code: 2018.111). The cohort consists of Caucasian CU subjects age 55 years or older who have provided written informed consent for the collection and storage of biological samples. Exclusion criteria include the presence of any degree of cognitive impairment defined by a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) score > 0 [ 14 ]; severe sensory impairments that hinder the performance of neuropsychological tests; major psychiatric or systemic diseases; and any contraindication to performing complementary tests (e.g. claustrophobia or anticoagulation therapy). Regarding the detailed participant selection process, 328 subjects responded to an open call in the media in our community. Of those 328 individuals, 63 participants are still awaiting their first evaluation. Among the remaining 265, a total of 26 were excluded due to unavailability (schedule conflicts and changes in residence), 15 were excluded because they were unable to undergo MRI or LP, 9 were excluded due to systemic or psychiatric conditions, 3 because of imaging findings (such as space-occupying lesions or major stroke), and 8 were excluded because they had a score > 0 on the CDR scale. In total, 204 subjects were included for analysis of plasma GFAP. Detailed selection process is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. ApoE status determination Apolipoprotein E ( ApoE ) genotype was determined using the TaqMan single nucleotide polymorphism genotyping assay (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, United States). Subjects carrying at least one copy of the ε4 allele were classified as ε4+, and those without were placed as ε4-. Sample collection and pre-analysis Our institution belongs to the Alzheimer’s Association Quality Control program, so we comply with the international recommendations for sample collection and storage [ 15 , 16 ]. CSF and plasma samples were obtained on the same day in all participants, between 9 and 10 AM, with less than a 30 min interval, and with the subjects fasting. The CSF was collected into 15 mL polypropylene tubes and the centrifuged at room temperature (2000 g for 10 min). The supernatant was aliquoted in 500 µl volumes into 1 mL tubes and frozen at -80 °C until analysis in the immunology laboratory of our hospital. Plasma samples are obtained and handled according to the standardized operating procedures detailed in previous work to minimize pre-analytical and analytical variability [ 17 ]. Blood was stored in 10 mL ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes and kept cold until processed, within the following three hours. Samples were then centrifuged under strictly controlled conditions of temperature and duration (10 min at 1800 g). The supernatant was stored in 500 µl aliquots in polypropylene tubes and frozen at -80 °C until laboratory analysis. All samples underwent a single freeze-thaw cycle prior to analysis. CSF and plasma biomarkers CSF Aβ40, Aβ42, p-tau181, total tau (t-tau) and Neurofilament Light Chain (NfL) levels were measured in all participants using the fully automated immunoassay analyzer Lumipulse G600 II platform (Fujirebio Diagnostics, Malvern, PA, United States) [ 18 ]. The following kits were used: Lumipulse G β-Amyloid 1–40, Lumipulse G β-Amyloid 1–42, Lumipulse G p-tau181, Lumipulse G t-tau, and Lumipulse G NfL. Aβ42 and Aβ40 were used to calculate the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio. To establish the CSF cut-off points of Aβ42/Aβ42 ratio and p-tau181, we used an unbiased Gaussian mixture modelling approach based on a cohort of 578 subjects, comprising both CU and cognitively impaired subjects [ 19 ]. Based on this model, we considered Aβ-positive (A+) when CSF Aβ42/40 ratio < 0.067, and tau-positive (T+) when CSF p-tau181 > 55.0 pg/mL. According to their CSF biomarker profile, participants were classified into four groups: A − T−, A + T−, A + T+, and A − T+. Based on this classification, all individuals with abnormal Aβ values (A + T−, A + T+) were considered to be within the Alzheimer´s continuum . In addition, participants were further categorized into A + and A− groups. According to the revised criteria for diagnosing and staging of AD of the Alzheimer´s Association, we did not consider t-tau as biomarker of “ N ” in terms of ATN classification due to its high correlation with p‐tau, particularly in preclinical stages [ 4 ]. However, since t‐tau has been widely used as a marker of neurodegeneration in the literature, we retained it in this role in correlation analysis along with NfL. Plasma GFAP levels were measured in all participants using the Lumipulse G GFAP assay on the Lumipulse G600II automated platform. The limits of detection and quantification were 1.8 pg/mL and 6.0 pg/mL, respectively. Intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were < 2.5% and < 1.7%, respectively [ 20 ]. All samples were above lower limit of detection. In a subset of 143 participants, plasma GFAP was also measured using an in-house ultrasensitive SIMOA assay on an HD‐X Analyzer (Quanterix, Billerica, MA, USA) at the Clinical Neurochemistry Laboratory, University of Gothenburg, Sweden. Cognitive assessment The neuropsychological assessment consisted of a comprehensive battery of tests addressing all cognitive domains. The details have been described in previous work [ 21 ]. The Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) [ 22 ] was used for global cognitive assessment, and global CDR score for measuring both functionality and cognition. Scores of cognitive tests were adjusted for age and educational level according to data from the NEURONORMA project since it provides normative data in Spanish population [ 23 ]. Detailed information on the cognitive domains studied and the tests used can be found in Supplementary Material 1. Physiological variables and comorbidities We studied the influence of several variables on plasma GFAP concentrations, including renal filtration rate, body mass index (BMI), and previous diagnosis of diabetes mellitus (DM) and arterial hypertension (HT). Both estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and BMI were considered as continuous variables. eGFR was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration formula and expressed in mL/min/1.73m 2 [ 24 ]. Previous works suggest that it is not isolated values of blood pressure or glycemia that influence plasma biomarker concentrations, but rather their chronic effect on the blood-brain barrier [ 25 ]. Therefore, participants were dichotomized according to their medical records into those with a history of HT and DM and those without. Statistical analysis We used Shapiro test to assess the distribution of variables. Data were described as mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate. Non-normally distributed CSF and plasma biomarkers were log-10 transformed. Agreement between Lumipulse and SIMOA measurements was evaluated using several approaches. First, we calculated the linear relationship using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. We then applied Passing–Bablok regression to assess constant and proportional bias. Finally, we constructed a Bland–Altman plot to visualize method differences as a function of measurement magnitude and to estimate the limits of agreement, thus providing an assessment of the absolute agreement between the two methods. Associations between plasma GFAP and CSF Aβ42, p-tau181, t-tau and NfL were tested with Pearson´s correlation in the overall sample and after stratification by A + and A- groups. For correlations involving Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio, since it is considered a state biomarker and it has a non-linear relationship with the rest of biomarkers, we used Spearman’s Rho. Next, group comparisons of plasma GFAP were performed using Student’s t test (A − vs. A + and T − vs. T+) and one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test (AT groups). The differences between AT groups were also analyzed adjusting for age, sex, and ApoE ε4 status using ANCOVA. Effect sizes were calculated with Cohen’s d. We also performed logistic regression models taking A+/- status as dependent variable and plasma GFAP, age, sex and ApoE ε4 status as independent variables. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC) values were analyzed to assess the potential of plasma GFAP as amyloid predictor. To compare the different AUCs we used the DeLong test. Optimal cut-off points were estimated with the Youden index. To examine the influence of physiological variables (glomerular filtration rate, BMI) and comorbidities (HT, DM) on plasma GFAP levels, linear regression models were fitted including each factor individually, adjusted for age, sex, and ApoE ε 4. P-values were further corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate method. Results were considered as statistically significant when p-value < 0.05. Missing values have been handled by omission. Statistical analyses were performed with R studio software version 4.2.22 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Discussion Plasma GFAP has emerged as a promising biomarker of astrocytic reactivity, contributing to the biological characterization of AD within the current conceptual framework. Nevertheless, its value when measured with automated techniques, especially in preclinical AD —the stage at which intervention is most relevant— is not yet well defined. To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the diagnostic performance of plasma GFAP in preclinical AD using the Lumipulse platform, and the first to directly compare plasma GFAP measurements obtained with this fully automated platform against those from SIMOA. In our cohort of CU subjects, we found a significant correlation between plasma GFAP measured by Lumipulse and CSF Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio (Rho = -0.36) and p-tau181 levels ( r = 0.25). Our results are consistent with previous studies demonstrating a strong relationship between GFAP and Aβ, using both CSF Aβ42/Aβ40 and amyloid PET as references of brain amyloidosis [ 8 , 26 ]. When we stratified by amyloid status, the correlation between GFAP and CSF Aβ42/Aβ40 remained in the A+ individuals, but the association with p-tau181 ceased to be significant. Our results align with recent evidence indicating that plasma GFAP is linked to Aβ insoluble aggregates, but not to soluble tau in CU individuals [ 8 ]. Similarly, several other studies have also reported significant associations between plasma GFAP and amyloid PET, even at preclinical stages [ 9 , 11 , 26 ]. In contrast, Sánchez-Juan et al. described a relationship between serum GFAP and post-mortem tau pathology in advanced AD dementia [ 27 ]. Taken together, these findings suggest that astrocytic reactivity, as reflected by GFAP, may be more directly involved with amyloid than tau pathology, at least in preclinical AD, supporting GFAP as a biomarker for early amyloid deposition. At later phases of AD, once amyloid load has reached a plateau, GFAP could be more closely related to tau and serve as a biomarker of disease progression. We also observed that plasma GFAP levels were significantly higher in A+ versus A − and T+ versus T− subjects. Across AT groups, plasma GFAP increased progressively with advancing brain pathology, with highest concentration in A + T+ compared to A-T- individuals. Differences between A + T+ vs. A + T- and A + T- vs. A-T- groups did not reach significance, although a visual tendency toward higher values alongside increasing AT pathology was evident. This lack of statistical significance may be due to the small sample size of the A + T- group ( n = 18). Together, these results replicate those obtained in previous works and inform that astrocytic reactivity is a very early phenomenon in AD, being present in the preclinical stages of the disease [ 9 – 12 , 28 , 29 ]. In this regard, it should be noted that our cohort has a prevalence of A-T+ subjects similar to that expected [ 30 ], however, since this biomarker profile is considered outside the AD continuum , we have excluded them from this group analysis. Furthermore, studies that include longitudinal follow-up and patients with cognitive impairment show that increases in plasma GFAP are associated with the clinical progression of the disease, reaching the highest concentrations in the dementia stages [ 10 ]. Our findings are consistent with those reported by Pelkmans et al. [ 8 ], who did not observe a direct association between astrocytic markers such as GFAP and YKL-40 and cognitive performance in cognitively unimpaired individuals, but differ from other studies that have described such relationships [ 31 ]. The absence of a significant association in our cohort may be explained by several factors. First, the early disease stage of our cohort likely results in limited variability in cognitive test scores, reducing the ability to detect cross-sectional associations. Second, plasma GFAP reflects a relatively nonspecific neuroinflammatory process that may precede measurable cognitive changes, particularly in cognitively unimpaired individuals. Finally, composite cognitive measures such as the PACC-5, while sensitive to longitudinal decline, may be less informative in cross-sectional analyses and do not capture the full complexity of cognitive functioning at these early stages. When assessing the diagnostic performance of plasma GFAP alone, measured by the Lumipulse, we found a modest AUC of 0.67 for discriminating between A + and A- subjects. Adding GFAP to a baseline model including age, sex, and ApoE ε4 status did not improved accuracy (0.79). These findings are consistent with the notion that GFAP is not a specific marker of AD and, thus should not be considered as a standalone diagnostic biomarker. Accordingly, in the latest biological framework criteria of AD, it is not considered useful for diagnosis as a single marker [ 4 ]. In addition, the demanding nature of our cohort, with 73% of participants classified as A-, may have contributed to these results. Other studies involving patients with cognitive impairment and more advanced brain pathology have found higher AUC values for detecting amyloid pathology, ranging from 0.7 to 0.8 for [ 9 , 32 ]. In a subset of 143 participants in which we also have available plasma GFAP measured with SIMOA, we analyzed the concordance between both techniques. The correlation was very strong ( r = 0.96), and Bland-Altman analysis indicated an acceptable concordance between measurements since, with 97.1% of the measurements within the limits of agreement. Besides, Passing-Bablok regression did not find a consistent bias between measurements, but revealed a small proportional bias, with Lumipulse slightly overestimating higher values relative to SIMOA. Taken together, these results support the use of Lumipulse GFAP as a clinically acceptable alternative to ultrasensitive research-based assays for assessing astrocytosis. The fact that Lumipulse is fully automated and widely available in hospital laboratories worldwide underscores its potential for integrating plasma biomarkers into routine clinical workflows while minimizing analytical variability. Our study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, its cross-sectional design precludes conclusions regarding the temporal relationship between plasma GFAP and the other parameters studied. Longitudinal studies with repeated measurements are needed to understand the trajectory of plasma GFAP in populations of particular interest, such as individuals progressing from A- to A+. Second, the small sample size of some AT groups, such as A + T- subjects ( n = 18), may have limited statistical power in stratified analyses. Third, all the Lumipulse assays were performed using the same kits, thereby reducing the analytical variability but not reflecting real life conditions, where samples are often analyzed with different kits as they are obtained [ 33 ]. Finally, our cohort consisted of volunteers with a Caucasian origin, which limits the generalizability of the results. In conclusion, our study provides novel evidence supporting the feasibility of translating astrocytic biomarkers into routine clinical practice. Although plasma GFAP alone has only moderate diagnostic performance for detecting amyloid pathology in CU individuals, its strong correlations with CSF AD biomarkers and concordance with ultrasensitive techniques such as SIMOA support its clinical utility. Combined with other biomarkers, plasma GFAP may enhance early detection and optimize participant selection for clinical trials targeting the earliest stages of the disease. Supplementary Information

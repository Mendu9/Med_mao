1 BACKGROUND Alzheimer's disease (AD) is characterized by the deposition of amyloid plaques, neurofibrillary tau tangles, and progressive neuronal loss. Clinical prediction of AD and related dementias (AD/ADRD) has limited accuracy, , 1 and a definitive diagnosis still requires 2 post mortem identification of pathological hallmarks. , 3 , 4 Subjective cognitive decline (SCD) and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) are recognized as clinically relevant, successive pre‐dementia stages. Individuals with SCD perform within the normal range on standardized cognitive testing but report persistent concerns. 5 , 6 During the MCI stage, cognitive performance declines below age‐adjusted norms, but functional independence is maintained. 7 Early and accurate identification of SCD and MCI is essential for prognosis, monitoring, and therapeutic intervention. Hence, there is a critical need to determine and quantify in vivo biomarkers of neurodegeneration and neuroinflammation, to enhance diagnostic confidence during the earliest disease stages, and to elucidate potential overlap and causality between various disease states. 8 Multiple biomarker modalities have emerged to detect ADRD pathology in living patients. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) provides sensitive measures of amyloid beta (Aβ) 42, total tau, phosphorylated tau (pTau), 9 neurofilament light chain (NfL), and transactive response DNA‐binding protein 43. 9 , 9 , 10 However, CSF collection is an invasive, expensive, and difficult‐to‐implement method for broad clinical use. 11 , 12 13 Positron emission tomography (PET) enables in vivo identification and localization of amyloid and tau depositions. In all stages, PET provides valuable insight into the spatial and temporal progression of pathology and supports differential diagnosis. PET limitations include high cost, limited availability, potential false‐positive or false‐negative results, limited spatial resolution, and susceptibility to artifacts due to patient motion. In contrast, blood collection is minimally invasive, cost‐effective, and increasingly able to provide accurate estimates of amyloid and tau burden. 14 , 15 Recently, plasma AD biomarkers such as Aβ42, Aβ40, pTau181, NfL, and glial fibrillary acid protein (GFAP) have been validated and shown to have strong correlations with amyloid or tau deposition as seen on PET scans. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Studies have shown that a low plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio and high pTau 181, GFAP, and NfL levels are useful blood‐based biomarkers (BBMs) for AD, 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 supporting their use as diagnostic, prognostic, and disease monitoring tools. 25 , 26 , 27 The application of these BBMs to SCD and MCI populations may help in early risk stratification, reduce reliance on PET imaging, and support scalable approaches to population‐based detection of AD/ADRD pathology. However, there are limited studies on how plasma biomarkers correlate with amyloid and tau PET measures in SCD and MCI cohorts. Understanding these relationships is important for determining whether BBMs can serve as surrogates, screening tools, or enrichment measures for assessing preclinical and early‐stage AD/ADRD in the clinic. 28 In this study, we evaluated the correlation between BBMs and amyloid and tau PET imaging findings across the pre‐dementia AD spectrum. We measured a comprehensive panel of BBMs reflecting distinct pathological processes: Aβ deposition (Aβ42, Aβ40), tau pathology (pTau181), neurodegeneration (NfL, GFAP), neuroinflammation (interleukin (IL)‐12p70, IL‐1b, IL‐4, IL‐5, interferon γ [IFNℽ], IL‐6, IL‐8, IL‐22, tumor necrosis factor α [TNF‐α], IL‐10), and blood–brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction (angiopoietin 2 [Ang2], heparin‐binding EGF‐like growth factor [HBEGF], placental growth factor [PlGF], hepatocyte growth factor, vascular endothelial growth factor [VEGF]). Participants were stratified into three clinical groups – cognitively unimpaired (CU), CU with subjective cognitive decline (SCD), and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) – to examine whether plasma–PET associations differed across the early stages of disease. We assessed both individual biomarker associations and multimodal biomarker combinations to identify plasma signatures that best predicted amyloid and tau burden, studying pre‐dementia stages in a diverse cohort. This comprehensive characterization of plasma biomarkers in pre‐dementia populations aims to establish their utility for early diagnosis and risk stratification, potentially reducing reliance on neuroimaging in clinical and research settings. 2 METHODS 2.1 Participants One hundred ninety‐one participants were enrolled at the New York University (NYU) Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (ADRC) following approval from the Institutional Review Board. We informed all participants about the study procedures, and they provided written consent after a full explanation of the protocol. All patients underwent uniform data set version 3.0 neuropsychiatric testing and received a cognitive diagnosis following National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center criteria (cognitively impaired, SCD, or MCI). ADRC inclusion criteria were age ≥ 60 years, presence of a study partner, fluency in English or Spanish by both the participant and study partner, and willingness to undergo phlebotomy and neuroimaging. Exclusion criteria included major neurological disease, severe psychiatric illness, severe organ failure, history of organ transplant, HIV/AIDS, severe autoimmune disease, and active drug or alcohol abuse. 2.2 Plasma biomarkers Plasma samples were collected at the NYU ADRC using standard protocols. Blood was collected in EDTA tubes and centrifuged for 10 min at 4°C, at 2000 × g. Plasma was aliquoted and stored at −80°C until analysis. Biomarker assays were evaluated at the NYU ADRC Biomarker Core. Plasma levels of Aβ40, Aβ42, NfL, and GFAP were measured using single‐molecule array (Simoa) technology (HD‐X; Quanterix) with the Neurology 4 Plex E Advantage kit. pTau181 levels were measured using the pTau181 V2 kit. All assays were run in duplicate with calibrators and kit‐specific controls (levels 1 and 2). Quality control was performed after each run to ensure values within acceptable ranges (coefficients of variation < 20%). Limits of detection (LODs) and lower LODs were recorded for each assay run. All analyses used the same machine and kits from the same lot to ensure consistency. Plasma biomarkers of neuroinflammation (IL‐1β, IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐6, IL‐8, IL‐10, IL‐12p70, IL‐22, IFNγ, TNFα) and BBB dysfunction (Ang2, HBEGF, PlGF, basic fibroblast growth factor [FGFb], VEGF) were measured using the Corplex Cytokine 10‐Plex kit and Angiogenesis 6‐Plex kit, respectively, using Simoa SP‐X technology. Future directions : Future investigations should focus on optimizing the use of plasma biomarker measurements to determine their pre‐dementia and AD/ADRD diagnostic potential and their utility for evaluating the effectiveness of therapeutic interventions for AD/ADRD. 2.3 PET Imaging All imaging exams were carried out on a 60‐cm‐diameter bore 3T Biograph PET/magnetic resonance (MR) scanner (Siemens Healthineers), an integrated PET/MR system that offers precise MR‐PET co‐registration through simultaneous acquisition and a common frame of reference. Exam 1 : Amyloid PET (Figure 1 ) was acquired using a US Food and Drug Administration‐approved 18 F‐florbetaben (FBB) tracer. Each subject received 300 MBq (8.1 mCi) of FBB intravenously over 15 s, followed by a 12‐cc saline flush. Injected and residual doses and injection times were double recorded. The subjects then rested for 90 min to achieve tracer equilibration. FBB imaging was acquired over 30 min. Image reconstruction was done with parameters compliant with the SCAN/CLARiTI protocol: 5‐min time frames, five to six frames per exam; iterative Three‐Dimensional Ordered Subset Expectation Maximization method, four iterations, 21 subsets; grid, 344 × 344 × 127; zoom, 2.0; no smoothing filter; and all standard corrections applied (including detector normalization, dead time, attenuation, scatter, decay, and randoms). Attenuation correction was performed using a hybrid algorithm combining a Dixon μ‐map with a superimposed co‐registered bone compartment derived from a skull atlas. Standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) was derived using a composite neocortical region of interest (frontal, lateral parietal, and lateral temporal cortex), with the whole cerebellum designated as the reference region. 29 FIGURE 1 PET exams from two patients depicting uptake of 18F‐Florbetaben (FBB) tracer. Exam 2 : Tau PET (Figure 2 ) was performed using a 185‐MBq (5 mCi) dose of 18 F‐MK6240 tracer (Lantheus, Bedford, MA, USA), with a 90‐min equilibration time and 30‐min acquisition. The same image reconstruction as for FBB PET was used. SUVR for MK6240 PET was derived using a composite target region that included fusiform, parahippocampus, hippocampus, and posterior cingulate gyrus, with cerebellar gray matter used as the reference region. , 30 31 FIGURE 2 18F‐MK6240 tau PET images from two patients. Note non‐specific tracer uptake in the mucosa, salivary glands, oral/nasal cavities, and nasopharynx of this lipophilic tracer. 2.4 Statistical analysis All PET (tau and amyloid) and plasma variables were positively skewed and were therefore log‐transformed to approximate normal distributions. Continuous variables were centered at the mean and scaled to a unit standard deviation using the following equation: (X‐mean[X])/SD(X). PET SUVR values were first regressed on the individual plasma biomarkers in linear regression models with age, sex, APOE ε4 status (non‐carriers as the reference category), and cognitive group (CU and SCD compared to MCI as the reference group) included as covariates. Plasma biomarkers associated with PET SUVR at a trend level ( p < 0.10) were then jointly included in a composite linear regression model with the same covariates. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed in R version 4.5.1. 3 RESULTS 3.1 Demographic characteristics We examined 191 participants enrolled at the NYU ADRC. SCD is considered the earliest symptomatic phase of AD and is often viewed as a part of normal aging in a clinical setting. When individuals have minor neuronal damage but retain functionality, the compensation is often attributed to neuroplasticity and cognitive reserve. 32 , 33 However, SCD is recognized as a precursor to future cognitive decline and dementia, with underlying pathological conditions. 34 Cognitive decline can progress from MCI to overt dementia, with AD being the leading cause. 35 Previous studies showed that the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio strongly correlated with amyloid positivity and could be used as an early diagnostic marker. A decreased ratio can be detected prior to clinical symptoms, suggesting that measuring the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio could be a strategy to identify patients with SCD who will develop amyloid plaques in the brain. , 36 , 37 , 38 Aβ42 aggregates into plaques, leading to reduced CSF concentrations in AD compared to CU. 39 Elevated levels of pTau181 in preclinical AD similarly correlate with increased brain amyloid burden and tau pathology. 40 Our analysis of the plasma pTau181 levels shows that pTau181 may be considered a prominent distinguishing factor between all three groups of subjects, as they were found to be significantly different between all three groups. The observed preliminary discrepancy between plasma pTau181 and tau PET data in distinguishing normal controls from MCI and SCD participants might reflect the fact that plasma pTau181 and other plasma markers can potentially be used as earlier markers (to predict cognitive function) relative to the tau PET. Similarly, amyloid PET is an earlier marker than tau PET, particularly for pre‐AD stages. 41 In our study, higher amyloid PET SUVR correlated with lower plasma Aβ42 levels (after adjusting for age, sex, APOE ε4 status, and cognitive group), indicating increased amyloid burden in the brain. Higher pTau181 and higher pTau181/Aβ42 ratio further reflected the presence of amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles. The pTau181/Aβ42 ratio serves as a particularly strong indicator of amyloid and tau pathology in later AD stages. In the logistic regression models, only amyloid PET (considered alone or with other biomarkers) significantly distinguished MCI participants from CU and SCD, and neither PET nor plasma biomarkers significantly distinguished CU from SCD. We believe that the utility of amyloid PET as a solitary imaging modality to diagnose all‐cause MCI is a concept deserving further exploration. Our results also provide preliminary evidence that the relationship between PET and plasma assays for amyloid varies with cognitive status, with MCI participants exhibiting a stronger correlation between PET SUVR and plasma biomarker (Figure 4 ). Plasma biomarkers were previously shown to correlate with amyloid and tau PET measures, but few studies examined whether these associations differed across early clinical stages. CU and SCD participants compared to the MCI group have significantly lower amyloid PET SUVR, while the difference between CU and SCD was not significant. This can be linked to findings from other studies suggesting that a SCD‐plus criterion can be more efficiently linked to related biomarker findings. Our findings suggest that plasma–PET coupling strengthens from CU/SCD stages to MCI, a finding consistent with reduced biomarker variability as the disease initiates and progresses. However, these results should be interpreted with caution due to trend‐level significance. A larger sample size and controls are necessary to draw more precise conclusions. While earlier studies discussed the potential use of CSF to study markers of neurodegeneration and neuroinflammation, our findings show that plasma samples may be just as effective as studying CSF, with the added benefit of being categorized as a non‐invasive technique. Inflammatory biomarkers also show an intercorrelated element that depends on age and stage of disease. Similarly, these markers can also show a variability in correlation with amyloid and tau PET SUVR findings. One such example is that of the lower plasma IL‐8 levels found in the setting of higher tau PET SUVR, as was also found in CSF by other investigators. The lower IL‐8 level may be attributable to the physiologic immune system remodeling that occurs due to advancing age and, hence, worsening state of dementia. IL‐8 has also been hypothesized to offer an early neuroprotective role, with high levels preventing amyloid plaque and tau tangle formations and advancing age resulting in advanced disease states due to exhaustion of the immune system and the overpowering result of AD pathology development. Hence, our results demonstrate that an increase in one key pathology is correlated with Aβ changes and neuroinflammation, which reflects the multi‐stage nature of pre‐dementia syndromes and their eventual progression to AD. 42 We also observed that a higher tau SUVR was associated with lower plasma Aβ42, significantly higher GFAP levels, and lower IL‐8 cytokine involved in inflammatory signaling. With three biomarkers (Aβ42, GFAP, and IL‐8) present in a combined model, each remained a significant predictor of tau PET SUVR. These findings support the theory that as Aβ42 peptides aggregate into amyloid plaques, their level in circulation becomes reduced. On the other hand, higher GFAP (a structural protein in astrocytes that serves to maintain BBB integrity and protect neurons from injury) may indicate astrocytic activation. Increased GFAP levels are associated with abnormal reactive astrogliosis linked to Aβ and neuronal damage, 43 and lower IL‐8 may reflect impaired immune signaling triggered by tau accumulation. GFAP and IL‐8 appear clinically meaningful for identifying transitions from CU and SCD stages to MCI. 44 By combining multiple plasma biomarkers, we offer a more comprehensive view of AD pathology progression compared to relying on individual markers. For instance, plasma pTau 181 levels have been shown to increase on average 8 years before a clinical AD diagnosis is made. Other tau isoforms, such as pTau217, play a role similar to that of an early diagnostic marker, and we plan to investigate pTau217 in this cohort and others. , 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 Integrating plasma biomarkers with tau and amyloid imaging increases the sensitivity and specificity of AD diagnosis. Conducting amyloid and tau PET imaging enhances diagnostic accuracy and expands our tools to characterize preclinical and clinical AD. 49 Our study shows that plasma biomarkers in preclinical and MCI adults correlate with tau and amyloid PET measures. This supports that plasma biomarkers can become a primary diagnostic modality for AD/ADRD. Continued research comparing plasma biomarker levels to PET SUVR and findings on brain tissue after death is needed to investigate and identify further biomarker profiles for specific ADRD subtypes to develop personalized treatment regimens. Amyloid and tau PET SUVR have shown an association with neurodegenerative and neuroinflammatory markers in plasma, indicating their potential for use in measuring and determining pre‐dementia stages and studying the underlying biological process within AD. GFAP and IL‐8 measures appear to be important to identify transitions from CU and SCD stages to MCI. Hence, our findings support the use of plasma biomarkers as valuable tools for diagnosing the presence and extent of AD/ADRD pathology, monitoring its progression, and potentially evaluating treatment response in clinical and research settings, with the heterogeneous nature of our cohort providing us with results that can easily be extrapolated to a wider patient population. Future investigations should focus on optimizing the use of plasma biomarker measurements to determine their pre‐dementia and AD/ADRD diagnostic potential and their utility for evaluating the effectiveness of therapeutic interventions for AD/ADRD.

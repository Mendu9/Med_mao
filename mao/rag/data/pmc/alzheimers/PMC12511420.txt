Introduction Chronic diseases and Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs) are persistent, often incurable illnesses that result from a variety of health conditions. They are usually caused by one or a combination of environmental, behavioral, and genetic factors. They are multifactorial and can pose complex anomalies in people of different health backgrounds. With a scant chance of regaining health, such treatments usually take about 70% of the patient’s income 1 . In contrast, therapy in low-income countries is impossible due to a lack of technology and educational access 2 . 3 Chronic diseases, including cardiovascular disease, neoplastic disorders, and neurological disorders, tend to deteriorate human health at an accelerating rate over time, often resulting in death . In specific chronic ailments, there is a persistent decline in health conditions despite short-term treatment therapies. For example, in the case of Alzheimer’s, progressive loss of cholinergic neurons in the Hippocampus results in worsening memory loss and cognitive decline with age 4 . 5 , 6 Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurological condition leading to deterioration in cognitive abilities, predominantly impacting memory, thought processes, and behavior. It is the most common cause of dementia among older adults, accounting for approximately 60–70% of dementia cases worldwide . Alzheimer’s leads to a gradual loss of independence and function, severely impacting both the patient’s and the caregiver’s quality of life. The disease typically develops slowly and worsens over time. Early symptoms might be mild and easily overlooked, but as the condition progresses, it severely impairs a person’s ability to carry out everyday activities 7 . Alzheimer’s is currently irreversible and incurable, though treatments can temporarily slow the worsening of symptoms. 8 , 9 Alzheimer’s disease primarily causes memory loss, especially of recent events, and difficulties in planning, problem-solving, and completing familiar tasks are shown in Fig. 1 . Patients often experience confusion about time and place, language problems, and poor judgment . Mood changes such as depression, anxiety, and irritability are common. As the disease progresses, these symptoms worsen, leading to significant challenges in daily life and loss of independence 10 , 11 . 12 Fig. 1 Causes of Alzheimer’s disease . 12 There are three AD types, i.e., Early-Onset AD, Late-Onset AD, and Familial AD, also known as FAD. Early-onset AD comprises roughly 5% of dementia patients before the age of 65 years, and Late-onset AD, which is more common, occurs in patients after 65 years of age. FAD is a unique type that only happens through genetic transfer. Patients suffering from AD are reported to suffer from poor cognition, mood swings, social disorientation (loss of coordination), and dysphagia, along with dehydration and neurological impairment, which eventually leads to a premature death . 13 , 14 A combination of genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors causes Alzheimer’s disease. Key contributors include the buildup of abnormal proteins in the brain, beta-amyloid plaques, and tau tangles that disrupt nerve cell function . Age is the most significant risk factor, with older adults being more susceptible. Genetics also plays a role, particularly gene variants like APOE-e4. Other factors, such as heart disease, diabetes, smoking, and poor diet, can increase the risk, but the exact cause remains complex and not fully understood. Physical complications such as difficulty swallowing and increased infection vulnerability are common in later stages. The disease also places a heavy emotional and financial burden on families and healthcare systems, as long-term care and support become necessary 15 . 16 , 17 Although progress has been made, early diagnosis of Alzheimer’s remains difficult, and the exact causes are not fully understood. Current treatments only ease symptoms temporarily . There is a need for better predictive models and broader use of technologies like IoMT and AI to improve early detection and patient care. The Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) refers to interconnected devices and sensors that collect and transmit health data in real-time. In the context of Healthcare 5.0, an advanced, patient-centric healthcare paradigm 18 , 19 . IoMT plays a pivotal role by enabling continuous monitoring, personalized care, and real-time data analytics. For Alzheimer’s, IoMT devices can monitor behavioral patterns, physiological signals, and cognitive functions outside clinical settings. Integrating IoMT with AI-powered prediction models enhances early diagnosis and continuous patient monitoring. 20 Prediction models aim to identify individuals at risk of developing Alzheimer’s before clinical symptoms manifest, enabling early intervention. Traditional models use statistical analysis of clinical and demographic data, but recent advancements incorporate machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) techniques to improve accuracy . Transfer learning involves leveraging pre-trained models on a large dataset and fine-tuning them on specific Alzheimer’s data. This approach accelerates training, reduces the need for a large labelled dataset, and enhances prediction accuracy. Recent advances in deep learning have revolutionized medical image analysis, offering new opportunities to improve the accuracy and efficiency of disease detection systems. In more recent DL models, Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are constructed explicitly for image recognition, segmentation, and classification. They comprise a multi-modular structure with interconnected nodes, ensuring deep object detection by processing it through multiple layers. 21 , 22 This study developed a CNN pre-trained model, ResNet152, to analyze the Kaggle Dataset containing 33,984 images of AD patients. To address this dilemma, an approach utilized as explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) has recently been introduced in deep learning to enhance trustworthiness between AI and humans for predicting and classifying AD in dementia patients. The proposed model additionally adopts a transfer learning approach to connect data during training and testing phases and improve computational time and cost 23 of the model. The potential of IoMT for real-time monitoring is underutilized, and explainable AI techniques are needed to build trust in clinical use. Integrating these technologies in a patient-centred Healthcare 5.0 framework is still limited. The main objectives for this research include: 24 , 25 To develop an IoMT-driven Alzheimer’s prediction model that collects and analyzes real-time patient data from interconnected medical devices, enabling continuous monitoring beyond traditional clinical environments. To employ transfer learning techniques using pre-trained deep learning models (such as ResNet152) to improve Alzheimer’s detection accuracy while reducing training time and computational costs on large medical imaging datasets. To integrate explainable AI (XAI) methods within the prediction framework to provide transparent, interpretable insights into the model’s decision-making, thereby increasing clinician and patient trust. To leverage the Healthcare 5.0 paradigm for creating a patient-centric, efficient, and scalable system that supports early diagnosis and personalized intervention for Alzheimer’s disease. To validate the proposed model’s performance using publicly available datasets (e.g., Kaggle Alzheimer’s MRI images) and demonstrate its potential for real-world clinical application and continuous patient monitoring. Unique contribution to Healthcare 5.0 In contrast to prior studies that primarily emphasize static datasets or isolated interpretability techniques, this research introduces a comprehensive framework that uniquely integrates IoMT-enabled data acquisition, transfer learning with class balancing through Conditional WGAN, and multi-method explainable AI (Grad-CAM, SHAP, LIME, and LRP). This synergy not only achieves superior diagnostic accuracy (97.77%) but also delivers real-time, transparent, and patient-centric decision support, thereby contributing a distinctive advancement to the realization of Healthcare 5.0. Literature review This section discusses the most recent research done for the early prediction of Alzheimer’s disease based on ML and DL techniques employed and various conditions set to acquire accurate results. Researchers developed a model based on ML techniques, including image quality selection, vessel map segmentation, and SVM classification. Using data from the UK Biobank, their model achieved the max accuracy percentage of 84.26% for the classification group, comparing the AD group and the fourth NC group 26 . used real-world Longitudinal MRI data set of 150 patients’ dataset from Open Access Series of Imaging Studies (OASIS) and derived prediction results using several ML Algorithms, including Decision Tree (DT), Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting (GB), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Voting classifiers (VC). The derived results benefit medical experts as they are based on real-world data. However, datasets, feature selection, and the use of DL are scarcely explored during research. 27 Lim et al. employed a multiclass classification technique that used 3D T1-weighted brain MRI scans from the ADNI. The researchers built and tested three DL models: CNN, VGG-16, and ResNet-50. For feature extraction, the VGG-16 and ResNet-50 models were pre-trained on the ImageNet dataset, with a new densely connected classifier mounted on top for classification purposes. This research successfully contributes to developing automated diagnostic tools for distinguishing between different cognitive states 28 . developed a deep-learning framework to identify structural brain anomalies in the pre-MCI stage using MRI. Their findings suggest that deep-learning methods can identify early structural neuroimaging anomalies that can easily differentiate stable from progressive cognitive states, thereby aiding in the prediction of MCI conversion. 29 El-Sappagh et al. used a two-stage DL model that aimed at early detection of AD and predicting the conversion time from MCI to AD. Figure 3 illustrates the complete workflow of the proposed IoMT-driven Alzheimer’s prediction framework, structured into four major layers: Data Acquisition, Preprocessing, Prediction, and Validation/Deployment. Data acquisition layer Heterogeneous IoMT-enabled devices, such as MRI scanners and connected medical imaging systems, continuously capture patient brain scans. These raw Alzheimer’s MRI data streams are securely transferred to the central server for analysis. The dataset used in this study is the publicly available Kaggle Augmented Alzheimer MRI Dataset (33,984 MRI scans across four classes: Non-Demented, Very Mild Demented, Mild Demented, and Moderate Demented). The IoMT integration ensures real-time collection, storage, and accessibility of clinical data within a Healthcare 5.0 environment. The dataset was first divided into training, Validation, and testing subsets (70/15/15) using the original class distributions shown in Table 2 . To address class imbalance, Conditional WGAN-GP augmentation was applied only to the training set, resulting in a balanced dataset across all four classes, as summarized in Table 3 . The validation and testing subsets remained unchanged to ensure unbiased model evaluation and to prevent data leakage from synthetic samples. A Conditional Wasserstein GAN (WGAN-GP) was used to generate additional MRI images conditioned on the four AD stages, improving representation of under-sampled classes. The generator upsamples noise–label vectors through transposed convolutions, while the discriminator applies down-sampling with gradient penalty for stability. The resulting synthetic images were merged with real MRI data to create a balanced dataset. Although WGAN-GP enhanced class balance, the fidelity of synthetic images is constrained by training stability and dataset diversity. To mitigate bias, we applied label conditioning, weighted sampling, and gradient-penalty regularization, ensuring that synthetic samples complemented rather than replaced authentic data. This approach improved class balance while maintaining anatomical reliability. Prediction layer The augmented dataset was divided into training (70%), validation (15%), and testing (15%) subsets. A fixed hold-out split was chosen over K-fold cross-validation because the dataset size (33,984 MRI scans) provided sufficient samples per class, and the hold-out approach reduced computational cost while maintaining reliable evaluation. K-fold Validation will be considered in future work to confirm robustness further. A pre-trained ResNet152 backbone, initialized with ImageNet weights, was fine-tuned for Alzheimer’s classification. Early convolutional layers were frozen for the first five epochs to preserve low-level features. In contrast, deeper residual blocks and fully connected layers were progressively unfrozen to capture disease-specific structural patterns. Fine-tuning employed the AdamW optimizer (initial learning rate 3e-4, cosine decay, weight decay 1e-4), with class-weighted cross-entropy, label smoothing (0.05), dropout, and early stopping to mitigate overfitting. This strategy ensured efficient training and contributed to the model’s strong performance (97.77% accuracy; precision 0.981; recall 0.987; F1-score 0.983; specificity 99.13%). ResNet152 was selected for its deep residual connections, enabling stable gradient propagation and extraction of fine-grained features while maintaining computational efficiency. Compared to shallower CNNs (e.g., ResNet50, AlexNet), it provides greater representational capacity, and unlike 3D-CNN or DenseNet models (up to 96.86% accuracy), it avoids heavy volumetric preprocessing and produces clearer feature maps. Integrating multi-method XAI (Grad-CAM, SHAP, LIME, LRP) addressed interpretability challenges, generating anatomically meaningful saliency maps over hippocampal and ventricular regions that enhance clinician trust. Feature extraction proceeds through stacked convolutional and residual blocks, followed by fully connected layers that map features into four output classes. A SoftMax layer computes class probabilities, while the integrated XAI methods highlight the brain regions most influential in predictions, validating the biological plausibility of the model’s decisions. Validation and deployment layer Once training is complete, the optimized ResNet152-TL-XAI model is stored on a secure cloud server. During inference, preprocessed MRI scans are fed into the imported trained model to generate predictions for Alzheimer’s stages. IoMT gateways and APIs enable seamless integration with healthcare services, allowing clinicians to visualize predictions alongside explanatory heatmaps in real time. This deployment design supports scalability, remote access, and continuous monitoring within Healthcare 5.0 systems. This combination of a robust deep learning model with explainability tools strives to balance high predictive accuracy with critical interpretability, essential for real-world medical applications. Implementation of explainable artificial intelligence on alzheimer’s To finally address the black box dilemma in previous studies and our proposed model, which induces complications and unreliability for Humans, we integrated the XAI technique, LRP, in layer 4 of our methodology for decision-making purposes. After training the Alzheimer’s disease prediction model using the pretrained ResNet152 CNN, Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) techniques, shown in Fig. 4 , were applied to interpret the model’s decisions, as shown in Fig. 4 . Specifically, SHAP and LIME provided transparency by identifying which features or regions in the MRI scans most influenced the model’s predictions. SHAP quantifies the contribution of each pixel to the classification, while LIME offers local explanations by approximating the model’s behavior around individual samples. Applying these XAI methods enhances trust and Understanding of the model’s outputs, making the predictions more interpretable and clinically meaningful. Fig. 4 Proposed XAI-based Model for Alzheimer’s Disease Prediction. Evaluation of trustworthiness and clinical relevance The reliability of the applied XAI techniques (Grad-CAM, SHAP, LIME, and LRP) was assessed using three complementary criteria. First, sanity checks confirmed that explanations degraded appropriately when model weights were randomized. Second, insertion–deletion tests validated that progressively adding or removing salient image regions increased or decreased model confidence as expected. Third, neuroanatomical plausibility was verified by ensuring that highlighted regions, such as the Hippocampus and ventricles, aligned with established Alzheimer’s disease biomarkers. In addition, to address common challenges of XAI, we adopted a multi-method approach that reduces reliance on any single explainer, thereby minimizing complexity and enhancing robustness. Dataset bias was mitigated through Conditional WGAN augmentation and class-weighted training, ensuring adequate representation of minority classes. Explanation consistency was verified across repeated runs to support stability. At the same time, the combination of sanity checks, insertion–deletion tests, and anatomical Validation confirmed that the generated explanations are generalizable beyond specific training instances. Together, these evaluations demonstrate that the explanations are computationally consistent, biologically meaningful, and sufficiently reliable to enhance physician trust and support deployment in Healthcare 5.0 environments. IoMT integration and benefits The integration of the Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) into the proposed framework substantially enhances both diagnostic accuracy and usability compared to conventional, non-IoMT-based approaches. IoMT-enabled MRI scanners and connected medical devices enable real-time, multi-source data acquisition, reducing manual errors and ensuring consistent datasets that improve classification reliability. This direct linkage between IoMT devices and the model pipeline contributed to the achieved performance of 97.77% accuracy, precision 0.981, recall 0.987, F1-score 0.983, and specificity 99.13%. In addition, IoMT supports continuous patient monitoring by supplementing MRI data with behavioral, cognitive, and physiological indicators, allowing earlier identification of subtle Alzheimer’s progression that may be overlooked in isolated, clinic-based examinations. IoMT gateways also facilitate secure cloud-based deployment of the trained ResNet152-TL-XAI model, enabling clinicians to access predictions and explanatory heatmaps remotely through interoperable dashboards or mobile applications. This capability improves usability by offering scalability, remote accessibility, and decision support across diverse healthcare environments. To clarify integration into real IoMT ecosystems, the framework follows a layered architecture: Device Layer: MRI scanners and wearable sensors capture brain images and complementary patient data. Gateway Layer: IoMT hubs or edge devices preprocess signals, handle secure transmission, and reduce latency and bandwidth usage. Cloud Layer: The trained ResNet152-TL-XAI model performs inference and generates explanatory heatmaps, ensuring scalability and multi-institutional access. Application Layer: Clinicians and patients interact with predictions and explanations via EHR/FHIR-integrated dashboards or mobile apps, enabling real-time decision support and personalized care. By contrast, traditional non-IoMT diagnostic approaches are Limited to static, episodic clinic visits, making them slower, less adaptive, and less scalable. The IoMT-aware pipeline, therefore, not only strengthens diagnostic precision but also supports the broader Healthcare 5.0 paradigm, emphasizing patient-centricity, transparency, and real-time decision-making. Conclusion This study demonstrates that the proposed ResNet152-TL-XAI model, integrated with Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP), achieves high predictive accuracy and transparent decision-making for Alzheimer’s disease (AD). On the Kaggle Alzheimer’s MRI dataset, the model attained 97.77% accuracy, with a precision of 0.981, recall of 0.987, F1-score of 0.983, and specificity of 99.13%, outperforming recent state-of-the-art approaches. The integration of Grad-CAM, SHAP, LIME, and LRP effectively addressed the “black-box” challenge by highlighting clinically relevant brain regions such as the Hippocampus and ventricles, thereby strengthening interpretability and clinician trust. While transfer learning reduced computational cost and improved training efficiency, Validation was restricted to the Kaggle dataset, which lacks demographic and site diversity. To ensure broader clinical reliability, future studies must incorporate multi-site datasets from varied populations and imaging protocols. In addition, the current framework was trained on cross-sectional data, which captures disease state at a single time point. Future work will incorporate longitudinal datasets to track patients over multiple time intervals, enabling analysis of disease progression, stage transitions, and treatment response. Future research will also explore extending this framework to other neurodegenerative disorders such as Parkinson’s disease, ALS, and Huntington’s disease. Furthermore, the model can be adapted for risk stratification and personalized therapy, where calibrated probability outputs and patient-specific factors (e.g., demographics, lifestyle, and comorbidities) guide proactive care. Reinforcement learning and expanded IoMT integration, including wearable and behavioral monitoring devices, may further enable adaptive and longitudinal patient support. Overall, the findings highlight the potential of IoMT-enabled, XAI-driven pipelines to transform AD diagnosis and management within Healthcare 5.0, enabling accurate, explainable, and patient-centric solutions. Ethical, privacy, and clinical deployment considerations The integration of IoMT and AI into healthcare raises critical concerns related to privacy, security, and real-world deployment. Sensitive MRI data transmitted via IoMT devices and stored in cloud infrastructures should be protected with end-to-end encryption (TLS 1.3 in transit, AES-256 at rest), role-based access control, and multi-factor authentication. Personally identifiable information (PII) must be anonymized or pseudonymized before training, with proper key management, data minimization, and audit trails to ensure accountability and compliance with HIPAA/GDPR. Practical deployment also requires regulatory approval (clinical Validation and medical-device governance), human-in-the-loop review, and interoperability with healthcare IT systems such as EHR/FHIR, DICOM/PACS, and hospital workflows. From a resource perspective, adopting a 2D ResNet152 backbone instead of resource-heavy 3D-CNNs reduces preprocessing and training demands, enabling inference on standard GPU servers or CPUs. Scalability is supported through containerized microservices deployed behind IoMT gateways with autoscaling in cloud or on-prem clusters, with cost benefit assessments considering compute, storage, compliance, and training requirements. In IoMT environments, device integrity and secure data transfer are paramount. Lightweight authentication protocols, blockchain-based logging, digital signatures, and intrusion detection at IoMT gateways can safeguard device interactions. Moreover, federated learning allows collaborative model training without direct sharing of raw patient data, further reducing privacy risks. Ethically, integrating multi-method XAI (Grad-CAM, SHAP, LIME, LRP) enhances transparency and fairness by exposing model reasoning to clinicians. Anticipated risks, adversarial attacks, data leakage, and algorithmic bias should be mitigated through differential privacy, security audits, fairness assessments, and continuous MLOps monitoring (latency, drift, recalibration). Collectively, these measures support trustworthy, secure, and scalable adoption of IoMT-enabled, XAI-driven systems in Healthcare 5.0.

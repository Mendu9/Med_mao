Introduction Alzheimer’s disease (AD), which accounts for 60–80% of dementia cases, is primarily characterized by the presence of extracellular beta-amyloid plaques and intracellular accumulation of tau protein in the form of neurofibrillary tangles (NFT) [ 1 ]. However, up to 50% of affected individuals present with mixed dementia [ 1 ] associated with the existence of co-pathologies involving the accumulation of proteins, such as alpha-synuclein or TDP-43 [ 2 ], especially in the more advanced stages of the disease [ 1 , 2 ]. While the precise cause of this phenomenon is still unknown, genetic factors like the APOE*ε4 allele have been associated with an increased risk of developing co-pathologies [ 1 ]. In fact, this polymorphic variant introduces significant variability in AD development between carriers and non-carriers [ 3 , 4 ], causing diverse clinical and phenotypic manifestations that hinder the application of optimal therapeutic and care interventions [ 1 ]. Thus, identifying specific genetic risk factors for AD is essential for developing more appropriate, individualized treatment [ 5 , 6 ]. Our previous results suggest that there is a higher prevalence of intermediate alleles in the huntingtin gene ( HTT ) in AD patients with respect to control subjects (6.03% vs. 2.9%) [ 7 ], extending this fact to other tauopathies, such as frontotemporal dementia (FTD) [ 8 , 9 ], and synucleinopathies [ 10 ]. Exon 1 of the HTT gene presents CAG repeats in variable number, being considered normal below 27 repeats. When the number of repeats is above 35, it determines the age of onset of Huntington’s disease (HD) [ 11 ], with incomplete penetrance when the range is between 36 and 39 repeats and complete when this number is higher [ 12 ]. Alleles between 27 and 35 repeats are referred to as intermediate alleles ( HTT IAs ), which are genetically unstable at the germline level, although they are not considered pathogenic [ 13 ]. However, in the case of AD, it has not yet been possible to determine the effect of the presence of HTT IAs . At the neuropathological level, an increase in diffuse HTT accumulation has been found in hippocampal regions associated with memory and in layer III of the frontal cortex in AD patients compared to healthy subjects [ 14 ], although in this case the number of CAG repeats in the HTT gene has not been evaluated. On the other hand, there are studies showing that elderly HD subjects show co-pathology with AD in up to 82% of cases, with prominent dementia in the clinic [ 15 ], and increased aggregation of other proteins, such as phosphorylated tau, alpha-synuclein, and TDP-43, can also be observed in the later stages of HD [ 16 ]. It has even been proposed that HD is a 4R tauopathy, as alterations in the splicing of the MAPT gene have been described, and the absence of this gene in murine models of HD diminishes the characteristic motor alterations [ 17 ]. Therefore, it is reasonable to hypothesize that HTT IAs in AD could also influence the processing of the MAPT gene and tau isoform distribution, affecting the progression of AD pathology. Studies developed in murine models describe changes in gene expression as a function of CAG repeat length (including intermediate ranges), being the striatum the most sensitive region [ 18 ], and such alteration modifies the expression of microRNAs (miRNAs) in this brain area [ 19 ]. miRNAs are small non-coding RNAs (≈ 18–22 nucleotides) that are an integral part of the epigenetic regulatory network, since they can act as modulators that influence gene expression at the post-transcriptional level, including that of other genetic modifiers, such as DNA methyltransferases and histone acetylases [ 20 ]. In addition, they themselves are subject to epigenetic regulation, as their expression profile can be altered under pathological conditions, as shown for both HD [ 21 ] and AD [ 22 ] or by lifestyle [ 23 ]. Thus, it is possible that both AD and the presence of HTT IAs modify further the profile of miRNAs in carrier individuals, thereby affecting gene expression of key genes for disease progression. Here, we investigated the impact of HTT IAs on the clinical and neuropathological features of late-onset AD (LOAD). Given the vulnerability of caudate nucleus to exon 1 HTT CAG repeats, we focused our analysis on this region. We specifically examined whether HTT IAs correlate with alterations in HTT protein levels, Tau 3R, and Tau 4R neuronal burden, and the levels of MAPT splicing factors (SRSF6 and FUS-SFPQ complexes). Furthermore, we performed the first detailed small RNA-seq profiling of the LOAD caudate nucleus to identify miRNA-mediated mechanisms that may link HTT IAs to exacerbated tauopathy. Our goal was to determine if HTT IAs serve as a genetic modifier of the LOAD phenotype, potentially offering a biomarker for disease progression and patient stratification. Nuclear colocalization analysis To quantitatively assess the colocalization of FUS (A300-293 A; Bethyl Laboratories, USA) and SFPQ (WH0006421M2, Sigma Aldrich, USA) within neuronal nuclei, all sections were imaged under identical confocal microscope settings and laser intensities. Random images were acquired from two distinct regions of the caudate nucleus, capturing six Z-planes (1 μm step size) to cover the full nuclear volume at 63× magnification. Neuronal nuclei were identified based on size (~ 10–12 μm in diameter), and those smaller than 8 μm were excluded from analysis. Colocalization analysis was conducted on 30 randomly selected nuclei per section using the Coloc 2 plug-in in FIJI. Thresholded Manders’ coefficients (tM1 and tM2) [ 25 ], along with Pearson’s correlation coefficient (R) [ 26 ], were computed to quantify colocalization and assess the correlation between fluorescence signals. Proximity ligation assay The proximity ligation assay (PLA) for protein complexes detection was carried out as previously described [ 27 ]. Briefly, paraffin-embedded brain sections were rehydrated and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 20 min. Following permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min, sections were blocked for 45 min using the Duolink blocking solution (DUO92009, Sigma-Aldrich). Samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in antibody diluent (see Supplementary Table S3). After washing twice in 1× buffer A (DUO82049), sections were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with PLA probes PLUS and MINUS (DUO92002 and DUO92004, respectively; Sigma-Aldrich), each diluted 1:5 in antibody diluent. Ligation was carried out using ligase diluted 1:40 in ligation buffer (DUO92014) at 37 °C for 30 min. Signal amplification was performed with polymerase diluted 1:80 in amplification buffer, incubated at 37 °C for 100 min. Sections were then washed sequentially in 1× and 0.01× buffer B (DUO82049), mounted with DAPI-containing medium (DUO82040), and stored until imaging. To assess PLA signal intensity, all sections were imaged under identical conditions using a confocal microscope (Leica SP8), with fixed laser power and acquisition settings. For each section, five random Z-stacks were acquired at 1 μm intervals, covering the entire nuclear volume at 63× magnification [ 28 , 29 ]. Nuclei were selected based on size (10–12 μm diameter); nuclei smaller than 8 μm were excluded. Seventeen nuclei were randomly selected per section for quantification. For each Z-stack, a maximum intensity projection was generated, and mean fluorescence intensity values (range: 0–255 grayscale units) were measured using the stack profile tool in LAS X software (Leica Microsystems), by manually outlining each nucleus. Fluorescence values were normalized to the nuclear area, and the average intensity per nucleus was calculated to yield a representative value per subject. Western blotting Human caudate tissue (30 mg) was homogenized in lysis buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, 50 mM NaF, 5 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100) with protease inhibitors (cOmplete™, Mini Protease Inhibitor Cocktail; Roche, Switzerland) in a glass homogenizer on ice. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged for 10 min at 12 000 × g at 4 °C and the supernatant was collected for analysis. Protein concentration was determined with the NanoDrop One 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Fifty micrograms of total protein were loaded onto 4–12% SDS-polyacrylamide gels (M00653; GeneScript, USA) and transferred to PVDF membranes (0.2 μm, Amersham™ Hybond; Cytiva, USA), which were then blocked in TBS-T (Tris-buffered saline, 1% Tween-20) supplemented with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (Table S3), overnight at 4 °C, and, after TBS-T washes, with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:20000, ab97040, Abcam, UK) or anti-rabbit IgG (1:20000, ab7090, Abcam) for 1 h, at room temperature. Protein detection was performed with Amersham™ ECL™ Prime Western Blotting Detection Reagent (Cytiva). Anti-β-Actin (HRP) conjugated antibody (1:4000, sc-47778, Santa Cruz Biotechnologies, USA) was used as loading control. RNA integrity was evaluated for each sample using the Agilent 2200 TapeStation system (Agilent Technologies) and the RNA integrity number (RIN) are provided in Supplementary Table S4. Quantification was performed from cDNA using the Perfect Master Mix SYBR Green Kit (AnyGenes) on the 7900HT rapid real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystem, USA). The primers for the MAPT , MAPT 4R , and MAPT 3R genes analyzed were specifically designed by Eurogentec (Belgium). GAPDH and β-ACTIN were used to perform the internal normalization of the results, and the one with the most stable values for our dataset was chosen a posteriori , using the RefFinder tool [ 30 ]. Primer information and amplification conditions are listed in Supplementary Tables S5 and S6. The mRNA levels are represented as relative quantification (2 −ΔΔCt ), as described in [ 31 ]. small RNA-sequencing Small RNA-Seq was performed by Seqplexing (Sequencing Multiplex S.L., Spain) using 200 ng of caudate nucleus total RNA per sample. Library quality was assessed using the QIAxcel Advanced System (QIAGEN). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq X platform (Illumina, USA), generating paired-end reads (2 × 150 bp). The sequencing depth ranged from 8.64 to 144 million reads per sample (median = 25.89 million). Raw sequencing data were obtained in FASTQ format and quality control was performed using FastQC. Adaptor sequences were trimmed and miRTrace software [ 32 ] was used to classify the types of RNA present in each sample, distinguish between miRNAs, rRNA, tRNA and artifacts. miRNA counts were generated using miRDeep2 [ 33 ] and miRNA sequences were mapped to the human genome reference (version hg38/GRCh38). miRNAs identification was performed using miRBase (v. 22) [ 34 ]. All data generated and used in this study are publicly available in NCBI GEO DataSets (ID: GSE300433 ) and ZENODO (DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.15230070). miRPM custom R package and normalization A custom R package, miRPM, was developed to integrate the bioinformatics workflow (available at https://github.com/sergio30po/miRNA-RPM-DE-Analysis.git ). Count matrices were normalized using the Reads Per Million (RPM) approach, based on the total number of reads per sample. To validate this strategy, the DANA approach [ 35 ] was employed to compute the concordance correlation coefficient ( cc ) and the reduction of handling effects ( mcr ). We modified the original DANA implementation to improve cc computation by addressing excessive miRNA profile filtering and strict cluster size constraints, ensuring the biological integrity of the human post-mortem data. Differential expression of miRNA sequencing data Differential expression was performed using miRPM package with a two-step consecutive filter: (1) expression in > 50% of valid miRNA data in at least one group, and (2) expression level > 1000 RPM in all subjects of at least one group. The necessary assumptions for normality (Shapiro-Wilk test) and homoscedasticity (Levene’s test) were evaluated. As most data were non-parametric, group differences were assessed by Kruskal-Walli’s test, followed by FDR correction. Only those miRNAs with a significant adjusted p-value were subsequently subjected to Dunn’s post-hoc test, which inherently accounts for the multiple pairwise comparisons, to determine which specific group comparisons were significant. Graphical representation of miRNAs levels in heatmap was performed by normalization through Z-score. In silico prediction of target genes and pathway analysis of miRNAs To explore the biological relevance of the differentially expressed miRNAs, enrichment analysis of signaling pathways was performed using DIANA-miRPath v.4 [ 36 ], incorporating high-confidence miRNA-gene interactions initially retrieved from miRTarBase v.8.0 [ 37 ]. The analysis was conducted using both Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and REACTOME repositories, and statistical significance was set at p-value < 0.05 with FDR correction. Among the enriched pathways, particular attention was paid to the spliceosome, given its relevance to tau isoform regulation. For this pathway, potential gene targets associated with the miRNAs of interest were retrieved from miRTarBase v.8.0. To ensure the technical robustness of these interactions, the selection was further refined by verifying that all included miRNA-target pairs exhibited perfect sequence homology (score = 1, representing 100% complementarity) within the seed region. The subset of genes involved in the spliceosome was then subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) analysis using PantherDB v.19.0 tool, to characterize enriched biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components in which they are predominantly represented. GO terms meeting the significance threshold ( p-value < 0.05) were retained. In silico graphs were created using the SRPlot module [ 38 ]. Finally, gene interaction networks were generated to visualize high-confidence miRNA–target connections. Interaction data were imported into Cytoscape v3.10.3 for visualization and network analysis. General statistical analysis Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistic 27.0 software (IBM, USA). Categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages. For between-group comparisons of the frequencies of HTT IAs , APOE alleles or gender frequency, Chi-square and Fisher tests were performed. To determine the relationship between the presence of HTT IAs and the probability of survival in the LOAD groups, the Kaplan-Meier estimation method and the log-rank test were used. Survival rate was calculated as the time elapsed from disease onset to age at death (event = 1 per subject). Additionally, multivariate Cox-PH regression models were run to identify predictors of survival risk, such as age or the presence of HTT IAs . Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilks normality tests (for N < 50) were performed and the corresponding parametric or nonparametric tests were then applied. Mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range, as appropriate, were used to describe quantitative variables. To compare age at onset, age at death, disease duration, immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence results between controls vs. LOAD group or between LOAD subgroups, Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U-test was performed, as appropriate. Also, the parametric analysis of variance (ANOVA) test, followed by Tukey’s comparison, or the nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis’ test, followed by Dunn’s test, was applied when comparisons were made considering the three groups. To determine whether miRNAs levels correlate with the recording of different clinical variables, a Spearman correlation test was applied. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The exact levels of the p-values are indicated in each figure or additional table, if applicable. Graphs were created using GraphPad Prism 10.2.0 (GraphPad, USA). Results Demographic and clinical features LOAD patients had a higher frequency of HTT IAs than patients with early-onset AD [ 8 ] but the possible effect on disease progression was not yet explored. For this study, we considered only LOAD patients ( N = 323) and a group of healthy subjects ( N = 335) from our previous cohort [ 8 ]. The mean age of LOAD onset was 75.22 ± 6.02 years and disease duration until death was 9.35 ± 4.86 years, with a higher percentage of women in the LOAD group versus the control (Table 1 ). The distribution frequency of the different APOE gene isoforms was consistent with previous reports (Table S7) [ 8 ]. The presence of subjects carrying HTT IAs was higher in the case of LOAD patients versus healthy controls, with a frequency similar to that observed in previous studies (7.12% vs. 4.16%; p-value = 0.102; Fisher’s test, Table 1 ) [ 7 , 8 ]. Moreover, the number of CAG repeats was significantly higher in the case of long HTT allele of LOAD subjects with respect to healthy controls (median 20 [IQR 18–22] vs. median 18 [IQR 17–21], respectively; p-value < 0.0001; Mann Whitney; Table 1 and Fig. S1). Importantly, within the range of IAs in the LOAD group, the most frequent repeat was 27 CAGs, with subjects presenting even 35 CAGs (Fig. S2). To study the effect of the presence of HTT IAs specifically in LOAD donors and clinical variables, we divided this group into non-carriers ( N = 300) and carriers ( N = 23) (Table 2 ). The number of CAG repeats in the long HTT allele was higher in carriers versus non-carriers (28 [27–30] vs. 19 [18–22]; p-value = 0.001, Mann Whitney; Table 2 ). No significant differences were found in sex distribution, onset age and death age between the two groups. However, survival after disease onset was shorter in carrier subjects with respect to non-carrier (7.40 ± 2.89 years vs. 9.50 ± 4.96 years, respectively; p-value = 0.053, Student’s t-test; Table 2 ). In fact, the survival rate after disease onset showed a clear reduction of this rate in LOAD donors with HTT IA s ( p-value = 0.0017; Kaplan-Meier estimator; Fig. 1 ). Logistic regression models revealed a significant association between disease duration and the presence of HTT IAs ( p-value = 0.012; Cox-Snell R 2 = 0.01). Histopathological analysis of HTT protein It has been previously described that HTT levels are increased in the neurons of hippocampus and frontal cortex in LOAD [ 14 ]. To determine whether HTT IAs affect HTT total protein burden and its distribution in LOAD patients, a histopathological analysis was performed on the caudate nucleus (Fig. 2 ). First, we explored an antibody that recognizes both wild-type and mutant HTT (Fig. 2 A, EPR5526 clone). The results show significantly higher HTT cytoplasmic signal in caudate neurons of both LOAD HTT IAs non-carriers and carriers compared to healthy controls ( p-value = 0.0002 and p-value < 0.0001 respectively, ANOVA followed by Tuckey´s test; Fig. 2 C), as reported previously [ 14 ]. Notably, LOAD group carrying HTT IAs had an even higher intensity signal in HTT-positive neurons, compared to non-carriers ( p-value < 0.0001, ANOVA followed by Tuckey´s test; Fig. 2 C), which has not been described before. Given this, we next sought to ascertain the presence of intranuclear inclusions, a typical hallmark of HD pathology (Fig. 2 B, EM-48 clone). However, HTT inclusions were only detected in HD samples used as positive controls. Interestingly, we observed cytoplasmic HTT EM-48 labeling in a subset of neurons, exhibiting a cytoplasmic pattern consistent with our previous analysis with HTT EPR5226 pattern. Quantitatively, both LOAD HTT IAs non-carrier and carrier groups showed a significant increase in these HTT-positive neurons compared to control subjects ( p-value < 0.0001, ANOVA followed by Tuckey´s test; Fig. 2 D), with carriers displaying a higher burden than non- ( p-value < 0.0001, ANOVA followed by Tuckey’s test; Fig. 2 D). No changes in tau 4R levels were detected between the LOAD groups (Fig. 3 F). Consistent with the mRNA findings, the relative 3R/4R ratio protein showed no significant differences between LOAD groups (Fig. S3B). To further evaluate the distribution and abundance of tau 3R and 4R isoforms, we performed immunohistochemical analysis of tau 3R- and 4R-positive neurons across different regions of the caudate nucleus in healthy subjects and in both LOAD groups (Fig. 3 G). Tau 3R quantification revealed that both LOAD non-carrier and HTT IA carrier groups exhibited a higher number of tau 3R-positive neurons compared to controls (Fig. 3 H). Furthermore, HTT IAs LOAD subjects exhibited even more tau 3R-positive neurons compared to LOAD non-carriers ( p-value < 0.0001, ANOVA followed by Tuckey´s test; Fig. 3 H). In contrast, tau 4R quantification showed a higher number of tau 4R-positive neurons in LOAD patients compared to controls, although this increase was only statistically significant in the HTT IAs carriers ( p-value = 0.0017, Kruskal-Wallis followed by Dunn´s test; Fig. 3 I). Analysis of neurofibrillary tangle (NFT) maturation In AD, both 3R and 4R tau isoforms are present in NFTs, key elements in AD pathophysiology. NFTs begin as fibrillar bundles in neurons, evolve into mature tangles, and are externalized after neuronal death [ 39 , 40 ]. This progression appears to be unidirectional and correlates with the sequential predominance of tau isoforms. Ghost tangles, representing the final stage of NFTs degeneration (Fig. 4 A), are primarily composed of the 3R isoform, suggesting a temporal shift from 4R to 3R tau during the course of AD pathology [ 40 ]. Accordingly, we hypothesized that the increased number of 3R-positive neurons observed in LOAD patients carrying HTT IAs may reflect a shift in NFT maturation in these individuals. Pathway enrichment and network analysis We next explored whether this miRNA pattern could be affecting genes specifically involved in the splicing of the MAPT gene. Differentially expressed miRNAs between LOAD donors and controls were significantly enriched in 151, while those distinguishing the LOAD subgroups were in 147 biological pathways. Notably, 13 of these KEGG pathways were directly associated with neurodegenerative processes (Fig. S7A-B, Tables S9 and S10). To further explore the molecular mechanisms potentially regulated by these miRNAs, we performed an additional enrichment analysis using the REACTOME encyclopedia. This analysis identified over 470 potentially modulated processes (Table S11), among which ten stood out with highly significant relevance and were all related to RNA biology (Fig. S7C, Table S12), including mRNA splicing and metabolism of non-coding RNAs (such as miRNAs themselves). To explore this pathway more thoroughly, we retrieved the genes included in the spliceosome-related pathway from REACTOME and subjected them to a Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis using PantherDB v.19.0. A total of 246 genes were extracted and categorized according to cellular component, molecular function, and biological process. In terms of cellular component, the nucleus was the most enriched compartment (47%), followed by the cytosol (38%) (Fig. S7D, Table S13). Regarding molecular function, the majority of genes were associated with nucleic acid-related activities, with DNA binding (31%) and RNA binding (26.14%) being the most prominent (Fig. S7E, Table S14). When analyzing enrichment in biological processes, a large proportion of genes were involved in RNA processing (25.4%), RNA splicing (19.8%), and, more specifically, mRNA splicing (16.07%) (Fig. S7F, Table S15). These data indicate that the spliceosome machinery is among the most enriched pathways targeted by the miRNAs identified in our analysis. Finally, high-confidence predicted miRNA-mRNA interactions were retrieved from miRTarBase, focusing on the 246 genes linked to the spliceosome pathway. Selection was based on perfect seed-region homology for each miRNA-target pair. Subsequent network analysis using Cytoscape revealed that ten out of the 14 miRNAs differentially expressed between LOAD subgroups shared common target genes with ten members of the SRSF family (Fig. 7 C). Inter-relationship between dysregulated miRNAs, HTT CAG repeat size, and key neuropathological hallmarks To better integrate our findings within a neuropathological framework, we next integrated miRNA expression profiles with CAG repeat length, the severity of tau pathology, including both pretangles and ghost tangles, as well as the soluble HTT signal in caudate neurons (Fig. S8A, Table S16). We observed a consistent and statistically significant positive correlation between soluble HTT immunoreactivity and the expression levels of eleven miRNAs, where miR-218-5p and miR-27b-3p, from the group of five miRNAs specific for LOAD HTT IAs patients, showing the strongest associations. Only miR-30d-5p, miR-100-5p, and miR-126-3p failed to show significant associations with increased HTT soluble levels. Conversely, the burden of pretangles exhibited a predominantly negative correlation with miRNA levels, reaching statistical significance for 6 out of the 14 miRNAs analyzed (Fig. S8A, Table S16). In contrast, ghost tangles displayed a positive correlation with miR-218-5p and miR-30a-5p (Fig. S8A, Table S16). Interestingly, the number of CAG repeats correlated positively with both HTT soluble signal and the abundance of ghost tangles, while showing an inverse correlation with pretangle burden. Building upon our in silico target analysis, we examined high-confidence miRNA-mRNA interactions involving the MAPT and HTT genes (Fig. S8B). Network modeling identified miR-23b-5p, miR-99b-5p, and miR-128-3p [ 43 ] as predicted regulators of both MAPT and HTT , supported by perfect sequence homology. Additionally, miR-191-5p is predicted to target MAPT exclusively, while miR-27b-3p is selectively linked to HTT . These predicted high-confidence interactions provide a molecular basis for how HTT IAs may reshape the miRNA scenario, potentially contributing to the advanced maturation of tau pathology in LOAD. Discussion Late-onset Alzheimer’s disease (LOAD) patients represent a diversified population, characterized by significant clinical and pathological heterogeneity. In fact, there is a complex influence of genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors on disease development and progression [ 1 ]. Our previous studies showed a higher frequency of intermediate alleles in the HTT gene among neuropathologically confirmed AD patients [ 7 , 8 ]. However, the impact of HTT IAs on disease progression and the underlying molecular landscape has remained largely unexplored. In this work, we provide a comprehensive characterization of the caudate nucleus, a region very sensitive to increased CAG repeats in the HTT gene [ 18 ], in LOAD patients carrying HTT IAs . Our findings demonstrate that these genetic variants are associated with a shorter clinical duration from onset to death and an exacerbated neuropathological profile. This includes elevated levels of non-aggregated HTT protein, tau 3R predominance in neurons, and the advanced maturation of NFTs. A key finding from our study is that LOAD patients carrying HTT IAs exhibited a shorter clinical duration from disease onset to death compared to non-carriers. This reduction in survival appears to be independent of common demographic and pathological confounders, such as age or the burden of primary co-pathologies. While the absolute ages of onset and death showed high variability in the human population, disease duration effectively filters this noise by focusing on the tempo of the neurodegenerative process. The significantly compressed survival window in carriers indicates that while the presence of HTT IAs may not trigger an earlier onset of LOAD, they significantly influence the rate of progression once the disease is initiated. This clinical observation is directly reflected by our neuropathological findings. Despite dying at a similar age as non-carriers, the HTT IA carrier group possessed a significantly higher burden of ghost tangles and fewer pretangles. This indicates an advanced pathological maturation in the caudate nucleus, as the tau pathology of these individuals reaches end-stage maturation in a shorter timeframe than in non-carriers. This could be driven by a profound proteostasis disturbance, which ultimately leads to cellular dysfunction and death, especially in long-live neurons [ 44 , 45 ]. Within the LOAD context, where amyloid-beta and tau aggregation are disease hallmarks, the further accumulation of other known pathogenic proteins, such as TDP-43 and α-synuclein, may represent a distinct pathogenic mechanism, which is exacerbated by the presence of HTT IAs . Consistent with reports in other AD brain regions [ 14 ], we observed higher levels of soluble HTT without inclusions. While large polyglutamine (polyQ) expansions in HD drive aggregation, intermediate polyQ content in LOAD patients carrying HTT IAs may result in a protein that is resistant to clearance but remains soluble. This elevated soluble HTT is likely detrimental through a toxic gain-of-function involving aberrant protein-protein interactions. Since HTT acts as a scaffold protein, an excess of soluble HTT carrying intermediate polyQ could interfere with core proteostasis mechanisms such as autophagy and the ubiquitin-proteosome system [ 45 – 47 ]. Furthermore, recent evidence suggests that somatic expansion of CAG repeats occurs continuously throughout life, with repeats in the HTT gene potentially increasing by more than ten times their initial length in the striatum [ 11 ]. Thus, we cannot rule out that, at the single-cell level in the caudate nucleus, HTT IAs are prone to somatic instability. This could produce HTT species that cross the pathological threshold, further enhancing toxic interactions and accelerating NFT maturation. This aligns with recent in vivo findings showing that HTT IAs in prodromal AD patients are associated with increased plasma neurofilament light chain (NfL) levels and altered brain metabolism [ 48 ]. The disruption of MAPT alternative splicing is a central feature of several tauopathies [ 17 ]. In our cohort, we observed a shift toward the 3R isoform in LOAD carriers, a tau 3R predominance characterized by increased 3R-positive neuronal counts, and ghost tangles. Interestingly, our bulk tissue analysis of the 3R/4R mRNA and protein ratios did not show significant differences between carriers and non-carriers. This suggests that the influence of HTT IAs on tau pathology is not primarily driven by a global stoichiometric switch in MAPT transcripts, but rather by cell-specific protein accumulation and maturation. However, we observed a significant decrease in SRSF6 protein levels, as a promoter of exon 10 inclusion, and increased assembly of nuclear FUS-SFPQ complexes, which promote exon 10 exclusion, in LOAD HTT IA carriers. This focus on SRSF6 was supported by our broader screening of the SRSF family, which revealed a significant downregulation of other inclusion-promoting transcripts, such as SRSF1 and SRSF9 , as well as the exclusion-promoter SRSF4 , specifically in the HTT IA carrier group. Although the low RNA integrity in our samples requires cautious interpretation of these transcriptomic results, the potential alteration of these factors, alongside other key regulators like Tra2ꞵ (SRSF10), RBM4, or heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins (hnRNPs) [ 49 , 50 ], contributes to a disturbed contribute to a disturbed splicing environment. Even if such changes are not sufficient to flip the global tau 3R/4R ratio of the entire tissue, the sequestration of these RNA-binding proteins by soluble HTT may promote the focal accumulation of the tau 3R isoform, which is characteristic of the neurotoxic ghost tangle stage [ 40 , 51 – 58 ]. To our knowledge, this study provides the first detailed miRNA profiling in the caudate nucleus of LOAD patients. While several studies have explored miRNA profiles in blood, cerebrospinal fluid, hippocampal and prefrontal cortex tissues [ 59 – 61 ], the caudate nucleus remains understudied in AD despite its known sensitivity to proteinopathies. Our differential expression analysis allowed us to identify a robust pathological footprint in this region, characterized by 26 miRNAs altered in LOAD compared to healthy controls. Notably, this profile was consistently exacerbated in the presence of HTT IAs , with five miRNAs (miR-100-5p, miR-218-5p, miR-27b-3p, miR-487-3p, and miR-9-3p) emerging as a specific HTT IA signature. A common challenge in neurodegenerative miRNA research is the lack of consensus regarding the direction (up- vs. down-regulation) of specific miRNAs or even the detection of different miRNAs in the same region across different studies. These discrepancies could be attributed to different miRNA determination or normalization methods. In our study, we used the miRPM approach to RNA-seq, considering that the number of miRNA copies should be sufficient to have biological meaning, with the capacity to functionally modify mRNA processing. Furthermore, consistent with our previous findings in other models [ 62 ], the biological significance of miRNA dysregulation lies not in individual miRNAs in isolation, but in their convergent effect on specific biological pathways. Even if miRNA signatures differ between tissues or disease stages, they can converge on the same functional outcomes. In our cohort, the specific upregulated footprint in HTT IA carriers is characterized by miR-218-5p, which shows a striking correlation with CAG repeat length, soluble HTT levels, and ghost tangle maturation. Although this miRNA has not been widely associated with neurodegenerative disease in the past, it regulates a broad set of genes involved in stress signaling, neuroinflammation, neuronal fate, and neuronal network assembly [ 59 , 60 , 63 ]. In the context of our findings, the significant elevation of miR-218-5p in HTT IA carriers may represent a compensatory, yet ultimately insufficient, response to the severe neuronal stress induced by the co-occurrence of tau and HTT proteinopathies. The correlation of this miRNA with the advanced maturation of tau pathology (ghost tangles) further suggests that its dysregulation reflects a state of advanced neuronal failure. Moreover, our high-confidence in silico and network analysis confirm this functional convergence, demonstrating that these altered miRNAs show a high probability of targeting the spliceosome machinery. This suggests that in the context of LOAD, the presence of HTT IAs reshapes the regional miRNA landscape to favor an environment that impairs protein handling and alternative splicing. By integrating these molecular signals with our neuropathological observations, we propose a comprehensive model (Fig. 8 ) where regional miRNA signatures converge to exacerbate splicing factor dysregulation. This multi-layered regulatory collapse, where regional miRNA signatures converge to exacerbate splicing factor dysregulation, likely serves as the molecular driver for the advanced pathological maturation and shorter survival observed in these patients. Working with postmortem human brain tissue, while invaluable for understanding neuropathology, presents unique limitations that can influence study design and sample inclusion. We acknowledge the variability in sample size ( N ) across our different experimental assays (e.g., RT-qPCR, Western blot, immunohistochemistry), which is primarily due to the finite nature of our well-characterized donor cohort and the differing quality requirements of each analytical method. For RNA-based analyses, such as RT-qPCR or mRNA expression and miRNA-Seq, we faced challenges with RNA integrity. As indicated by the low RIN values in Table S4, RNA is highly susceptible to postmortem degradation. While this significantly affects mRNA expression levels, miRNA sequencing data is less compromised, as miRNAs are small nucleotide chains with high resistance to degradation, allowing for reliable data acquisition [ 61 , 64 , 65 ]. Similarly, the integrity of proteins in postmortem brain tissue poses significant challenges for Western blot analysis. We encountered difficulties due to the degradation of protein samples, especially for control subjects, which limited their inclusion. However, the primary objective of our Western blot analyses was to elucidate differences between LOAD HTT IAs carriers and LOAD non-carrier patients, a critical comparison that remained fully addressed by the available samples. Importantly, the use of immunohistochemistry and proximity ligation assays (PLA), which allow for determination at the single-cell level and the detection of protein-protein interactions, respectively, compensates for the limitations encountered in bulk protein analysis. All in all, the consistent trends and complementary information observed at various molecular levels (e.g., miRNA, mRNA, protein, and IHC profiles) significantly enhance the overall robustness and interpretability of our conclusions. Conclusions These findings underscore the impact of HTT IAs on LOAD pathogenesis, offering a key to the disease’s clinical heterogeneity. HTT IAs emerge as critical risk biomarkers and, as HTT genotyping is widely accessible, our results can contribute to improved clinical practice by enabling more precise stratification of LOAD patients for clinical trials and facilitating personalized interventions. While this study focuses on the caudate nucleus, the regional specificity of miRNA signatures and proteostasis failure suggests that exploring these mechanisms in other vulnerable areas, such as the hippocampus or the prefrontal cortex, will be essential to fully understand the systemic impact of HTT IAs on the AD brain. Moreover, future therapeutic strategies, whether aimed at restoring proteostasis, modulating the spliceosome machinery, or targeting signature miRNAs, such as miR-218-5p, could offer novel avenues to modulate the toxic environment that drives rapid clinical decline in this genetic subgroup.

Introduction Dementia is characterized by symptoms that can occur when specific groups of brain cells fail to function properly, affecting decision-making, reactions, and physical movement [ 1 ]. Dementia affected over 57.4 million people globally in 2019, with its prevalence expected to almost triple to 152.8 million by 2050 [ 2 ]. Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the most common form of dementia, accounts for 50–60% of all cases, representing a significant public health and economic burden challenge [ 3 ]. The pathobiological onset of AD is asymptomatic, progressing unobserved over the years. Patients initially develop memory impairments that surpass typical age-related decline, marking the onset of mild cognitive impairment (MCI). It takes additional years for MCI to reach a functionally disabling level, signifying the onset of clinical dementia [ 4 ]. It is estimated that 30–50% of MCI patients will progress to AD over a span of 5–10 years [ 5 ]. Since the reversion from an MCI state to normal cognition is feasible [ 6 ], and earlier therapeutic interventions applied earlier in the course of AD are more likely to achieve disease modification [ 7 ], MCI patients at high risk of progressing to AD would be diagnosed and treated. AD is marked by the accumulation of extracellular β-amyloid plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs), leading to widespread synaptic loss and brain atrophy [ 8 ]. By the MCI stage, β-amyloid plaques are nearly fully formed, while NFTs continue to develop during the dementia stage [ 4 ]. This progression suggests that heralding signs of AD may be detectable through neuroimaging techniques. As such, many studies have been conducted to identify the biomarkers of disease progression using imaging modalities. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the primary neuroimaging technique for evaluating structural brain changes associated with the clinical features of MCI and AD. Several MRI studies successfully identified the structural hallmarks of disease progression [ 9 – 11 ]. Some studies have pointed to the importance of longitudinal measures such as the rate of atrophy in specific brain regions in predicting the transition from MCI to AD, as atrophy rates were greater among MCI subjects who converted to AD than among those who remained stable, and greater among fast than slow AD progressors [ 12 ]. The changing pattern of atrophy has also been examined longitudinally. Pattern of gray matter loss in patients with AD affecting the medial temporal lobes, including the amygdala, anterior hippocampus, and entorhinal cortex could be used to differentiate frank AD from MCI cases [ 13 ]. By one year before an AD diagnosis, atrophy progresses to the middle and posterior temporal lobes, encompassing the entire hippocampus, and begins to involve the parietal lobe. At the point of AD diagnosis, atrophy becomes more widespread, severely affecting the medial temporal lobes, temporoparietal association cortices, and frontal lobes [ 9 , 14 ]. While such regional patterns are often detectable via conventional imaging metrics, radiomics offers a complementary approach by quantifying subtle structural and textural changes that may precede visually appreciable atrophy. These high-dimensional, quantitative features—capturing shape, intensity, and texture—have demonstrated prognostic utility in several neuroimaging contexts, including in deep learning (DL) and survival modeling frameworks [ 15 , 16 ]. Machine learning (ML) and, by extension, DL have proven to be invaluable tools for diagnosing [ 17 ] and predicting AD [ 18 ]. Although many ML models have been applied to forecast AD progression, few have incorporated longitudinal MRI data [ 19 , 20 ], and even fewer have utilized DL architectures [ 21 , 22 ]. Furthermore, the integration of radiomics features within DL-based survival models, especially in a longitudinal setting, remains largely underexplored. Given the superior performance of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) in predicting AD progression [ 18 ], we aimed to develop a hybrid DL model that leverages both CNN-extracted representations and radiomics features, trained on longitudinal structural MRI data from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI). Methods Data source and participant selection All participants recruited for this study were drawn from the ADNI cohort (adni.loni.usc.edu) [ 23 ]. The ADNI was launched in 2003 as a public-private partnership, led by Weiner et al. The primary goal of ADNI has been to test whether serial MRI, positron emission tomography (PET), other biological markers, and clinical and neuropsychological assessment can be combined to measure the progression of MCI and early AD. We targeted individuals initially classified as having MCI and monitored them for potential conversion to AD or dementia over time. To effectively capture the dynamic progression from MCI to dementia or AD, we implemented a longitudinal study design utilizing multiple T1-weighted MRI scans per participant. This methodological approach was selected a priori based on evidence suggesting that temporal changes in neuroanatomical features provide superior predictive information compared to single-visit assessments. We stipulated that each participant had a minimum of three T1-weighted structural MRI scans by the 18-month visit after baseline to ensure sufficient temporal resolution for accurate modeling of disease trajectories. Scans acquired within a consecutive two-month period were counted as one. T2-weighted images, proton density, localizers, calibration scans, and T2*-weighted sequences were excluded. Included patients must have maintained an MCI classification for at least 1.5 years (i.e., no clinical evidence of dementia within the first 18 months to ensure non-AD status within the MRI acquisition period). The following cut-off for progression to AD/Dementia was chosen using the previous literature [ 24 , 25 ] to balance the need for longitudinal data with the practical consideration of participant retention as well as to ensure sufficient observation time and balanced data for converters vs. non-converters. Patients met the conversion criterion for AD/dementia if either: Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) < 24, or. Global Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) ≥ 1 on any follow-up visit after the 18-month follow-up mark. Moreover, participants who transitioned to AD/dementia prior to 1.5 years were excluded from the analysis, as it was ambiguous whether they had definitively exhibited stable MCI at baseline. Following the application of these criteria, we partitioned the remaining dataset into training, validation, and test subsets. This partitioning was conducted on a per-subject basis, while preserving the ratio of converters to non-converters across the subsets. The distribution of the number of scans among the individuals varied significantly and was systematically constrained to a fixed number of three in order to retain the maximum possible number of participants. For subjects possessing a greater quantity of MRI scans, we selected the first and last MRI scans, along with the scan that was closest in time to the average between these two scans. Image preprocessing Basic corrections and segmentation Using SPM12 unified segmentation approach, all MRI scans underwent bias correction, and tissue probability maps for brain MRI scans were acquired, along with the deformation fields necessary for the spatial registration of MRI scans to MNI space. Skull stripping was conducted by including voxels with a probability exceeding 0.2 of belonging to white matter, gray matter, or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) maps. Ultimately, both versions (registered and non-registered) were retained for subsequent radiomics and CNN feature extraction. Each T1-weighted volume was resampled to a uniform voxel size of 1.5 mm. Separate masks for gray matter and white matter regions were generated for the purpose of radiomics feature extraction. Radiomics feature extraction Radiomics features were extracted from each T1 volume using the PyRadiomics package v3.0.1a1 in Python v3.10 [ 26 ]. The package is capable of computing standardized shape, intensity-based, and texture-based features. The key radiomic categories included: Shape Features (e.g., sphericity, elongation, surface area). First-Order Statistics (e.g., mean, energy, kurtosis). Texture Features : Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) metrics, Gray-Level Run Length Matrix (GLRLM), Gray-Level Size Zone Matrix (GLSZM), Gray-Level Dependence Matrix (GLDM), Neighborhood Gray-Tone Difference Matrix (NGTDM). During extraction, volumes were rescaled between 0 and 1, and a fixed bin width (e.g., 30) was used for intensity discretization. For each participant, we derived multiple sets of radiomic features by defining regions of interest (ROIs) from the whole brain, along with gray matter-only regions and white matter-only regions in both registered and native space images. The rationale behind the use of gray matter radiomic features instead of whole brain or white matter was the modestly greater prognostic potential of these variables. The Principal component analysis (PCA) variables (explaining 95% of the variance) derived from the gray matter radiomic features had the highest Cox proportional hazards concordance index (c-index) across the train set (last visit data) compared to other compartments. Single-visit CNN: 3D ResNet for survival ranking Model architecture To achieve a balance between parameter count and training time against model performance, we employed the three-dimensional Residual Network (ResNet3D) architecture [ 27 ] to predict patient survival outcomes based on structural MRI. ResNet-based architectures have the ability to capture complex spatial hierarchies within image data while mitigating the vanishing gradient problem through residual connections. Initially, the network processes the input MRI volume through a 3D convolutional layer featuring 64 filters of size 7 × 7 × 7, with a stride of 2, followed by batch normalization and a ReLU activation [ 28 ]. A subsequent max-pooling layer with a 3 × 3 × 3 window and a stride of 2 precedes the spatial dimensions. The core of ResNet3D comprises four stages of residual blocks. Each stage increases the number of filters (64, 128, 256, and 512) while progressively down-sampling the spatial dimensions through strided convolutions. Residual connections within each block facilitate efficient gradient flow, enabling the training of a deep network without performance degradation. After the residual stages, and pooling layers, flattened features were transformed into a single linear risk output. As such, the backbone was specifically designed to accept one volume per participant, following the typically the last available MCI-timepoint MRI to predict time-to-conversion risk. Longitudinal analysis: time-aware LSTM with attention While the above 3D CNN approach used only a single MRI timepoint per subject, to leverage and integrate the longitudinal scans of participants that had multiple scans (up to three T1 volumes at different follow-up visits), we extracted from the trained 3D CNN the final 512-dimensional feature vector (post-global-pooling) for each MRI. These embeddings, alone or potentially concatenated with radiomic features, underwent dimensionality reduction (PCA) using training data as input to reduce the number of variables (while retaining 99% of the variance) used across all three subsets. These PCA components formed the input to a custom recurrent neural network model with a similar survival outcome with time-to-conversion. Model interpretability and feature importance To provide additional insight into what features or anatomical regions drove the risk predictions, we used two complementary strategies. We applied 3D Gradient-Weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) to the 3D-ResNet model to highlight which voxels most contributed to the final convolutional feature maps for a given subject. Grad-CAM [ 34 ] operates by computing the gradient of the target output with respect to the feature maps of the model’s final convolutional layer. These gradients are globally averaged across the spatial dimensions to obtain weights that signify the importance of each feature map channel. By performing a weighted combination of the feature maps using these weights, we generated a coarse 3D heatmap that localizes the regions most influential in the model’s decision-making process. This heatmap is then overlaid onto the original MRI volume, providing a visual representation of the areas contributing to the risk prediction. To complement the Grad-CAM visualizations and enhance the interpretability of the model’s risk predictions, we applied SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) [ 35 ] using the Gradient Explainer (SHAP package, adapted for 3D data). To approximate the expected model output and reduce memory usage during SHAP computations, we used a small, randomly sampled subset of the training images as the background dataset. For each chosen sample, SHAP values were computed by estimating the gradient-based contributions of each voxel to the risk prediction. This approach builds upon the concept of expected gradients, which reformulates the integral of integrated gradients as an expectation and combines it with reference samples drawn from the background dataset to approximate Shapley values. The resulting 3D attribution maps were visualized with a diverging colormap centered at zero, normalized, and clipped at the 0.05th and 99.95th percentiles to mitigate the influence of outliers and disproportionate skewing of the color gradients. These maps were subsequently overlaid onto the corresponding MRI volumes to highlight spatial patterns of feature importance. To also evaluate the source of prognostic features for the model; we employed gradient boosting survival models fit on radiomic features. We derived feature importance scores by measuring the average importance scores in survival prediction across separately fit models for each visit in the boosting ensemble. High-importance features were identified to highlight which indices contributed most strongly to risk stratification. The primary metric for evaluating all survival models (CNN-based or LSTM-based) was the Harrell’s concordance index (c-index), which measures how well the predicted risk ordering aligns with the true ordering of times-to-event. A c-index of 1.0 indicates perfect ranking, whereas 0.5 corresponds to random guessing. Additionally, we included Uno’s censoring-adjusted C-index [ 36 ], as implemented in the SurvC1 package [ 37 ], which uses inverse probability weighting and estimates confidence intervals via perturbation-resampling over multiple iterations, reported as an auxiliary metric in the supplementary material. Ethical and regulatory considerations for ADNI data curation was obtained previously and are specified within the protocol draft available at the ADNI website. Informed consent was prospectively acquired from all participants within the ADNI upon enrollment and have therefore been performed in accordance with the ethical standards laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Statistical analysis Descriptive measures were utilized to demonstrate baseline data across data splits and measurement visits. The risk scores derived from the final LSTM model were subsequently evaluated for predictive accuracy using the Kaplan-Meier plot, which dichotomized risk scores based on median values. Additionally, the risk scores facilitated the calculation of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, showcasing the model’s predictive power in identifying patients transitioning within a specific timeframe. This included uncensored MCI patients and those converting to AD during the evaluated follow-up. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for each time interval to quantify predictive performance. To assess the influence of baseline factors on risk scores, a Cox proportional hazards model was utilized, incorporating both risk scores and baseline measures as inputs. This model was trained on the training subset and applied to the entire dataset for inference. We used random survival forests and cox regression models coupled with PCA dimensionality reduction to provide baseline alternative models at each step. Random survival forests and gradient boosting survival analyses were implemented via scikit-survival v0.24 [ 38 ], using hyperparameter grid search to optimize prediction accuracy. The CNN backbone was trained using TensorFlow v2.1, while the T-LSTM model, incorporating DL alone or with radiomic features, was trained using PyTorch v2.4 on an Intel CPU platform. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 17. Results The ADNI database was queried for entries on MCI subjects at baseline compatible with the study inclusion criteria. After screening potential patient studies, 228 participants and 684 3D T1-weighted MRI scans were included in the study. These participants came from the ADNI1 ( N = 217), ADNI GO ( N = 4), and ADNI2 ( N = 7) phases at baseline. Of the subjects, 146 were male, with an average age at baseline of 74.1 ± 7.0 years. Baseline MMSE scores averaged 27.5 ± 1.7. All MRI volumes had a slice thickness of 1.2 mm, and 13.6% were performed using a three-tesla field. Details for MRI scanner (General electric healthcare, Philips medical systems, and Siemens medical solutions) protocols are available at ADNI website. The average acquisition times for the second and third MRI scans were 208.7 ± 33.1 days and 410 ± 50.1 days from baseline, respectively. A total of 108 subjects converted to AD within a mean of 3.2 ± 1.9 follow-up years. The average follow-up time prior to censoring in non-converter MCI subjects was 4.23 ± 2.5 years. A Kruskal–Wallis test indicated no significant differences in the times of conversion or censoring across the three subsets. To improve predictive performance, we integrated radiomic features and longitudinal MRI data into the model. Our analyses demonstrated that gray matter radiomics provided comparable prognostic utility, achieving a validation c-index of 0.69, while their standalone predictive value remained marginal. For training the T-LSTM model, measurement time was represented as elapsed years, with the initial visit centered at zero. The utilization of longitudinal MRI data led to enhanced prognostic performance across both training and test subsets, achieving high predictive accuracy for ranking patients by their risk of conversion to dementia/AD (Table 2 & Supplementary Table 1). The prognostic capability of this model was not substantially improved by the incorporation of longitudinal radiomics data. In addition to the notable enhancement in predictive accuracy, the advantage of the model from longitudinal data was also confirmed a posteriori by consistent self-attention scores and the inclusion of embedding outputs from both the first and second LSTM blocks (Mean ± SD attention scores: 0.09 ± 0.14, 0.24 ± 0.12, respectively) as well as the final sequence (0.67 ± 0.21). Discussion The results of our study highlight the potential of clinically available structural T1-weighted MRI scans to predict both the progression toward AD and the estimated timing of its occurrence in subjects with MCI using CNNs. Our findings further suggest that incorporating longitudinal imaging data could enhance predictive accuracy and clinical utility. However, the modest gains observed must be balanced against the practical challenges of acquiring and processing longitudinal MRI datasets, as well as the missed opportunity to leverage larger sample sizes with single timepoint scans. Although our initial hypothesis emphasized the advantage of longitudinal data, our results indicate that improvements were primarily driven by two-step training procedures and model ensembling rather than the longitudinal design itself. This suggests that, given the difficulties in obtaining longitudinal scans, training on larger cohorts of single-timepoint MRIs may offer a more practical and effective approach in certain contexts. Be that as it may, the relevance of this study is emphasized in the current state of the literature in which there is a lack of consensus on established prognostic models for predicting AD and the onset of dementia. From a radiological and neuroanatomical perspective, the temporal gap between the radiological detection of cortical and subcortical atrophy and the onset of cognitive decline is not only a screening opportunity for the condition but also a critical window for early intervention and targeted therapeutic strategies. The training scheme of the model in our study had several advantages. Training the CNN backbone to extract nuanced and abstract representations from 3D volumetric data that influences patient survival (time to dementia) through a simplified loss enables more effective optimization within the hypothesis space. This is facilitated by the nature of the pairwise loss function, which prioritizes feature ranking cases based on their risk of conversion without imposing restrictive assumptions. Subsequently, the semi-parametric framework of the longitudinal model refines these representations, enabling the final risk scores to function analogously to log hazard ratios in the context of a Cox proportional hazards model and further enhancing the interpretability and clinical utility of the resulting risk scores. The latter approach, often referred to as “DeepSurv” models, has been underexplored in the context of time-series data prior to this work [ 39 ]. Although not extensively investigated in this study, our limited empirical experiments support the hypothesis that pairwise rank loss outperforms training with Cox proportional hazards loss for the standalone 3D-CNN model, which did not achieve comparable prognostic performance when trained solely with the negative partial likelihood loss function. Therefore, it is not unexpected that subsequent use of these features as input for Cox regression models and RSF led to a substantial improvement over the original CNN. Despite the previous evidence regarding potential improvement associated with using vector-based attention mechanism across time points with T-LSTM outputs, our study did not identify further benefit beyond its use for model explainability. The current literature on this topic is extensive. A systematic review of machine learning techniques for predicting progression from MCI to AD encompassed 116 studies [ 18 ]. The most frequently used algorithm was the support vector machine (SVM), which achieved an average accuracy of 75.4%. CNNs performed the best among the models, reaching an average accuracy of 78.5%. However, the majority of these studies focused on discrete group-level classification tasks (e.g., MCI vs. AD or progressive vs. stable MCI using fixed follow-up cutoffs), rather than continuous, individualized modeling of disease progression over time. This limits their generalizability and clinical utility in real-world prognostic applications, compared to risk scores generated within our survival framework. These method harbors the advantage of directly leveraging additional information from censored patients by elevating the risk of converting patients over MCI patients lost to follow-up in a later time point. Overall, more complex models, particularly those based on DL and integrating various types of data (neuroimaging, clinical, cognitive, genetic, and behavioral), tended to perform the best. Interestingly, the use of radiomics was not very common among the models, and those that did incorporate radiomics primarily employed traditional ML models rather than DL [ 15 , 40 ]. Among the previously conducted studies, some that utilized only imaging data exhibit features comparable to our model. Abrol et al. [ 41 ] developed a model to predict the progression from MCI to AD by training ResNet on 3D MRI images of patients with statuses ranging from cognitively normal to diagnosed with dementia. Their model achieved a prediction accuracy of 83%. The key distinction between their model and ours is their usage of cross-sectional data instead of longitudinal data. Additionally, Ocasio et al. [ 22 ] developed various models utilizing three different types of CNN architecture (sequential, residual bottleneck, and wide residual), different levels of tuning (zero-shot or fine-tuning), and either cross-sectional or longitudinal whole brain 3D MRIs of patients with normal cognition, MCI, or AD. They found that the sequential approach slightly outperformed the residual-based architecture, while zero-shot transfer learning surpassed fine-tuning. CNNs using longitudinal data were more accurate than those with single-timepoint MRIs in predicting MCI conversion to AD. Their best model achieved a balanced accuracy of 0.793 for predicting MCI conversion to AD within three years. The result of the current study compares well to previous comparisons of the sample size of this work. It is noteworthy to mention studies that incorporated non-imaging data into their models. Similar to our current results, previous models also favored a longitudinal design over single inputs, but unlike most previous research, the prognostic value of these model was partially dependent on the incorporation of baseline factors. With regards to complementary inputs, Minhas et al. [ 42 ] reported an AUC of 88.93% for 2-year predictions of MCI to AD conversion with 84.29% accuracy using a combination of neuropsychological measurements and MRI markers (brain region volume and cortical thickness) in their Multiple Predictor Timestamp Model (MPTM). They also [ 43 ] demonstrated a higher AUC of multimodal markers compared to the single marker prediction model in 6-month and 1-year timelines. Furthermore, Shu et al. [ 15 ] presented an integrated model with an AUC of 81.4% for predicting MCI progression to AD within 12 months by incorporating radiomic features, genetics, and neuropsychological measurements. Based on the previously mentioned articles, brain volume, when compared to cortical thickness, serves as a more significant marker for short-term prediction. However, local structural changes in hippocampal volume and cortical thickness do not indicate dynamic pathological changes during the conversion from MCI to AD. Therefore, whole-brain radiomic features may be more reliable predictors of MCI progression to AD. The consistent demarcation of areas contributing the most prognostic importance for conversion to in both our study and prior literature [ 18 ], provides a strong foundation for exploring novel, advanced ROI-focused DL architectures and other imaging modalities. For instance, the DL-based ROI selection of parcellated fMRI scans within the BrainGNN architecture [ 44 ] or static functional network connectivity matrices of fMRI scans within BrainRGIN [ 45 ], can be adapted and repurposed to apply the graph convolutional operations on a longitudinal series of voxel-based cytoarchitectural regions of the structural MRIs derived from histological atlases with functional connectivity determined by spatial proximity, white matter tracts, or correlation between regional changes across follow-up visits along with the survival losses described in our work. This direction for future investigations would be of further interest given the reportedly detrimental impact of directly integrating fMRI timepoints as features for graph transformers and temporal convolutional networks [ 46 ] compared to potential benefits of longitudinal design in our model based on structural MRI. Although our findings are promising, the limited sample size of this study restricted secondary analyses, especially those focused on identifying the optimal evaluation window and the minimum and optimal number of scans required to achieve maximum predictive accuracy while maintaining clinical feasibility and real-world cost-effectiveness. Furthermore, we did not investigate more complex CNN architectures that were shown to have demonstrated higher performance on traditional benchmarks and would have benefitted from improved self-attention (Vision transformers) and/or temporal mechanisms or broader intermediate layers (such as Dynamic spatio-temporal Attention models [ 47 ]) due to sample size and computational constraints. Likewise, despite the high predictive power shown in our CNN-based model, DL models are often criticized for their lack of interpretability and generalizability to external data. To address interpretability, we applied Grad-CAM and SHAP gradient analysis to identify key brain regions influencing the model’s predictions. However, these methods have limitations. Grad-CAM can highlight regions indiscriminately, reducing explanatory precision, while SHAP assumes feature independence—an assumption violated in 3D MRI data where adjacent voxels are highly correlated. Additionally, sociopolitical constraints limited our ability to validate findings externally, as we lacked access to other longitudinal datasets such as OASIS-3 [ 48 ] for external validation. Future studies should investigate these approaches in larger, more diverse cohorts to confirm our findings and ensure their generalizability, given the low sample size in this study. Conclusions In conclusion, our findings highlight the promise of integrating longitudinal MRI analyses with advanced DL methods to potentially improve predictions of MCI-to-AD conversion. Our results suggest that two-step model training within survival framework results in comparable performance to multimodal predictive models and state-of-the-art architectures by leveraging the time-sensitive nature of disease progression. Despite the modest but noticeable improvement over single-timepoint MRI models with the same sample size, this strategy offers earlier identification of high-risk individuals during routine clinical follow-ups, facilitating timely and targeted interventions.

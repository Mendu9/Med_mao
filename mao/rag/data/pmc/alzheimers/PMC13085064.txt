1 Introduction Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder and the leading cause of dementia worldwide, characterized by irreversible cognitive decline and substantial socioeconomic burden. Early identification of AD and its prodromal stage, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), remains a major clinical challenge, as pathological brain changes often precede overt clinical symptoms by several years ( Jack et al., 2010 ). Accurately modeling disease progression from cognitively normal (CN) aging through MCI to AD is therefore essential for timely intervention and improved disease management. Structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), particularly T1-weighted imaging, has been extensively used to investigate neurodegenerative changes associated with AD. Previous studies have shown that MRI-derived structural biomarkers reflect disease-related brain atrophy, especially in the medial temporal lobe and hippocampus, making MRI a non-invasive and clinically informative modality for longitudinal AD research ( Frisoni et al., 2010 ). The Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) provides a large-scale, standardized longitudinal dataset that integrates MRI with clinical and cognitive assessments, enabling systematic investigation of disease evolution across multiple time points ( Jack et al., 2008 , Mueller et al., 2005 ). Recent advances in deep learning, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have demonstrated strong performance in MRI-based AD classification by automatically learning discriminative imaging features without manual region-of-interest selection ( Feng Li et al., 2014 ). Subsequent studies have explored three-dimensional CNN architectures to exploit full volumetric information, often reporting high classification accuracy ( Payan and Montana, 2015 , Junhao Wen et al., 2020 ). However, these approaches typically incur substantial computational cost and, more importantly, offer limited insight into model decision-making. In addition, many existing studies rely on cross-sectional or scan-level data splitting, which may inadvertently introduce subject-level information leakage and lead to overly optimistic performance estimates in longitudinal settings. Beyond predictive accuracy, the lack of interpretability remains a critical barrier to the clinical adoption of deep learning models in neuroimaging. The black-box nature of CNNs raises concerns regarding trust, robustness, and clinical plausibility, particularly in safety–critical applications such as neurodegenerative disease assessment. Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) has therefore emerged as a key research direction, aiming to provide human-interpretable explanations for deep learning predictions. Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) is a widely used post-hoc XAI technique that highlights image regions contributing most strongly to model outputs ( Selvaraju et al., 2017 ). In the context of AD, explainability is especially important to verify whether model attention aligns with known neuroanatomical patterns rather than spurious imaging artifacts ( Montavon et al., 2018 , Böhle et al., 2019 ). Despite growing interest in explainable deep learning, most existing XAI studies in AD neuroimaging focus on single time points and provide primarily qualitative visualizations. Little attention has been paid to the temporal consistency and stability of explanations across longitudinal visits, even though disease progression is inherently dynamic. Without longitudinal validation of explanation behavior, the reliability of saliency-based interpretations remains uncertain. In this study, we propose an explainable deep learning framework for longitudinal MRI-based classification of CN, MCI, and AD subjects using T1-weighted MRI data from the ADNI cohort. A two-and-a-half-dimensional (2.5D) CNN architecture based on a modified ResNet model ( He et al., 2016 ) is employed to balance computational efficiency and spatial contextual modeling by integrating multiple adjacent slices per subject and visit. Longitudinal rigor is ensured through subject-level data splitting and the inclusion of multiple follow-up visits per individual, preventing information leakage and enabling realistic disease progression analysis. Crucially, explainability is treated as a primary component of the proposed framework. Grad-CAM is applied not only to generate visual explanations but also to support region-wise statistical analysis and temporal stability assessment of model attention across visits. By examining how explanatory patterns evolve over time and across diagnostic groups, this work provides insights into both model behavior and disease-related structural changes. The main contributions of this study are threefold: (1) we construct a rigorously curated longitudinal MRI dataset from ADNI with strict subject-level splitting to ensure valid evaluation; (2) we develop a lightweight 2.5D CNN framework suitable for longitudinal AD classification under realistic clinical constraints; and (3) we advance explainable neuroimaging analysis by integrating qualitative and quantitative Grad-CAM evaluation, including longitudinal stability analysis of explanations. By emphasizing interpretability, temporal consistency, and methodological validity alongside predictive performance, this work aims to support the development of trustworthy deep learning models for longitudinal Alzheimer’s disease research and clinical translation. Fig. 1 2 Materials and methods 2.1 2.5D CNN architecture To model longitudinal structural patterns while maintaining computational efficiency, we propose a 2.5D convolutional neural network architecture based on ResNet-18. The overall network design is illustrated in Fig. 1 . The proposed model adopts a 2.5D convolutional neural network architecture derived from ResNet-18 as a computationally efficient compromise between single-slice 2D and fully 3D approaches. Instead of processing the full 3D volume, five adjacent sagittal slices centered at the mid-slice are extracted from each T1-weighted MRI scan and stacked along the channel dimension. This representation enables the network to capture inter-slice anatomical continuity while avoiding the high memory and computational cost associated with fully 3D convolutional models. To accommodate the multi-slice input, the first convolutional layer of ResNet-18 is modified to accept five input channels rather than the standard three-channel RGB input. The remaining architecture follows the original ResNet-18 design, consisting of a sequence of residual blocks with identity shortcuts that facilitate stable gradient propagation and mitigate vanishing gradient issues during training. After feature extraction through the residual layers, global average pooling is applied to aggregate spatial features, followed by a fully connected layer that outputs class probabilities for three diagnostic categories: cognitively normal (CN), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Class probabilities are obtained from the network outputs for the multi-class classification objective. This 2.5D formulation was selected to preserve local anatomical continuity across neighboring slices while keeping model complexity tractable for longitudinal neuroimaging analysis. However, as shown in the experimental results, this design should be interpreted as a pragmatic architectural compromise rather than an optimal solution for AD classification. 2.2 Dataset Structural T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data were obtained from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database. All MRI scans were acquired using standardized MPRAGE protocols and preprocessed by ADNI with gradient nonlinearity correction (GradWarp), B1 field inhomogeneity correction, N3 bias field correction, and intensity scaling. This study was a secondary analysis of de-identified ADNI data. Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of Rumah Sakit Universitas Airlangga (Approval No. 2088/B/DST/UN3.RSUA/PT/2026). After diagnosis matching, subject-level split assignment, and quality-controlled preprocessing for model development, the final dataset used in the supervised learning experiments comprised 256 unique subjects and 433 MRI visits. All subjects had baseline scans (m00), while follow-up scans were available at 12, 24, 36, and 48 months. The number of follow-up visits decreased over time, reflecting typical longitudinal attrition in real-world clinical cohorts. This dataset was frozen prior to model development to ensure experimental reproducibility. An overview of the dataset selection and longitudinal splitting process is illustrated in Fig. 2 . Fig. 2 2.3 Clinical diagnosis labeling Clinical diagnoses were obtained from the ADNI Diagnostic Summary tables. Diagnosis labels were temporally aligned with imaging visits using subject identifiers and visit codes. Each MRI scan was assigned one of three diagnostic categories: cognitively normal (CN), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), or Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Numeric diagnosis codes provided by ADNI were mapped to categorical labels following standard conventions. Imaging visits without a corresponding clinical diagnosis were labeled as UNKNOWN and excluded from supervised learning experiments. Overall, valid clinical labels were successfully assigned to 98.9% of imaging visits. 2.4 Data splitting strategy To prevent data leakage across longitudinal visits, the dataset was split at the subject level rather than the image level. All MRI scans from the same subject were assigned exclusively to a single data split. In this study, the term longitudinal refers to subject-level analysis across multiple visits under strict temporal separation between training, validation, and test sets, rather than explicit temporal sequence modeling. Each visit is treated as an independent sample, while longitudinal consistency is enforced through subject-level splitting to prevent information leakage across time. The dataset was divided into training (70%), validation (15%), and test (15%) sets using stratified sampling based on diagnostic categories. This resulted in 177 subjects in the training set, 40 subjects in the validation set, and 39 subjects in the test set. Stratification ensured that all diagnostic categories (CN, MCI, AD) were represented in each split. To further examine the effect of split level, we additionally performed a scan-level split experiment using the same class-stratified 70/15/15 partitioning strategy while keeping the main 2.5D architecture and training settings unchanged. In this alternative setup, individual visits rather than subjects were assigned to the training, validation, and test sets, allowing overlap of subjects across splits. This additional experiment was included to directly assess whether split level substantially altered the observed performance. 2.5 Quality control Automated quality control was performed prior to model development. All MRI scans were successfully loaded using the NiBabel library, and no corrupted or unreadable files were detected. After preprocessing and file-availability filtering, only scans with valid labels and successfully generated model-ready inputs were retained for the final supervised learning experiments. Basic image statistics and dimensional consistency were verified to ensure suitability for deep learning analysis. Instead, clinicians consider multiple longitudinal observations to assess disease progression and reduce uncertainty. To reflect this setting, we further evaluated the proposed model at the subject level by aggregating scan-level predictions across available visits for each subject. Specifically, majority voting was applied to the predicted class labels obtained from multiple visits belonging to the same subject. The final subject-level prediction was determined as the most frequently predicted class across all visits. This strategy leverages temporal consistency and reduces the impact of noisy predictions from individual scans. As summarized in Table 3 , subject-level aggregation leads to a substantial improvement in classification performance. While the scan-level accuracy on the independent test set was 0.47, aggregating predictions across longitudinal visits increased the accuracy to 0.57. This improvement demonstrates that the proposed model produces reasonably consistent predictions across time and benefits from longitudinal integration. Importantly, the subject-level gain was observed under the strict subject-level split used in the main experiment. This evaluation is intended to better reflect longitudinal decision support rather than to claim clinically deployable performance. As a classical baseline, we evaluated a logistic regression model using simple volumetric features extracted from T1-weighted MRI, including global intensity statistics and non-zero voxel counts. When evaluated under the same subject-level longitudinal split, this baseline achieved an accuracy of 30.5% on the independent test set. The baseline failed to reliably identify Alzheimer’s disease cases, yielding near-zero precision for the AD class. This result highlights the limited discriminative power of global volumetric features in distinguishing subtle neurodegenerative patterns, particularly in longitudinal and imbalanced settings. We additionally implemented a revised 2D CNN baseline using the same final subject-level split and the same preprocessed MRI-derived inputs, except that only a single middle slice was used instead of the five-slice 2.5D representation. This 2D baseline achieved an accuracy of 0.4746 and a macro AUC of 0.4367 on the independent test set. However, it collapsed to predicting the MCI class, yielding zero recall for both CN and AD. These findings show that overall accuracy alone can be misleading. Although the revised 2D baseline achieved a numerically similar accuracy to the proposed 2.5D model, its class-wise behavior was substantially worse. In contrast, the proposed 2.5D model retained at least limited recognition of all three classes, including AD. Taken together, these baseline experiments suggest that the performance gains of the proposed model do not arise merely from the data split or aggregation strategy, but from its ability to capture richer spatial context than simple volumetric summaries or a single-slice 2D baseline. Grad-CAM enables visualization of class-discriminative regions by leveraging the gradients flowing into the final convolutional layer of a convolutional neural network. In this study, Grad-CAM was applied to the trained ResNet-18 model adapted for 2.5D MRI input, where each sample consisted of five adjacent sagittal slices centered at the mid-sagittal plane. The Grad-CAM heatmaps were generated from the last convolutional block of the network and subsequently upsampled to match the input resolution. These heatmaps were overlaid on the corresponding MRI slices to highlight regions contributing most strongly to the model’s predictions. XAI analysis was performed on correctly classified samples from the test set. Therefore, the resulting Grad-CAM visualizations should be interpreted as representative only of the subset of correctly classified cases, not of all AD, MCI, or CN samples. Visual inspection revealed distinct activation patterns across diagnostic groups. For AD subjects, the model tended to focus on regions within the temporal and frontal lobes, which are known to be associated with neurodegenerative changes in Alzheimer’s disease. In contrast, MCI samples exhibited more diffuse and intermediate activation patterns, while CN samples showed relatively weaker and less localized activations. The consistency of activation across the five sagittal slices supports the validity of the adopted 2.5D representation, indicating that the model does not rely on a single slice but instead integrates contextual anatomical information. At the same time, because AD recall remained low, these visualizations should be interpreted cautiously and not as evidence of uniformly stable AD-specific attention across the full dataset. Overall, the Grad-CAM analysis provides qualitative evidence that the proposed framework aligns with established neurobiological knowledge, thereby enhancing the transparency and trustworthiness of the classification results. For AD subjects, the network predominantly attends to temporo-frontal regions, which are known to be associated with neurodegenerative changes. MCI samples exhibit more heterogeneous activation patterns, reflecting the transitional nature of the condition. These observations support that the proposed model relies on anatomically meaningful features rather than spurious image characteristics. By combining a 2.5D convolutional neural network with subject-level longitudinal data separation and Grad-CAM-based explainability, the proposed approach was designed to jointly emphasize methodological rigor, interpretability, and longitudinal consistency, rather than to maximize classification accuracy alone. 4.1 Comparison with previous studies and methodological positioning Previous deep learning studies on Alzheimer’s disease have demonstrated the feasibility of convolutional neural networks for MRI-based classification, often achieving promising performance on the ADNI dataset ( Feng Li et al., 2014 , Payan and Montana, 2015 , Junhao Wen et al., 2020 ). However, many of these approaches focus primarily on cross-sectional imaging, treat each MRI scan independently, or employ computationally intensive three-dimensional architectures. In addition, interpretability is frequently underemphasized, limiting the clinical transparency of model predictions. In contrast, the present work explicitly incorporates longitudinal information by analyzing multiple visits per subject while enforcing strict subject-level separation across training, validation, and test sets. This design reduces the risk of information leakage and better reflects the progressive nature of neurodegeneration. Furthermore, the adopted 2.5D CNN architecture was intended as a compromise between 2D and 3D approaches, allowing the model to capture inter-slice contextual information without incurring the high computational cost associated with full volumetric convolutions. Importantly, this study places particular emphasis on explainability. Rather than treating model interpretation as an afterthought, Grad-CAM is integrated into the analysis pipeline to examine whether the learned representations align with known neuroanatomical patterns of Alzheimer’s disease. This positioning shifts the focus from purely performance-driven classification toward interpretable and clinically meaningful modeling. 4.2 Classification performance and subject-level longitudinal analysis The proposed framework achieved moderate classification performance across CN, MCI, and AD groups. While the overall accuracy did not surpass state-of-the-art results reported in some 3D CNN-based studies, the performance should be interpreted in the context of the study design. The use of subject-level longitudinal splitting presents a more stringent and realistic evaluation setting, which often results in lower but more reliable performance estimates. Under the main subject-level split protocol, the proposed 2.5D CNN achieved a scan-level test accuracy of 0.47 and a macro AUC of 0.52. Subject-level aggregation via majority voting across longitudinal visits improved accuracy to 0.57, suggesting that the model produced reasonably consistent predictions across repeated visits from the same subject. The results indicate that longitudinal information contributes to improved modeling of disease progression, particularly for distinguishing transitional MCI cases from stable CN and AD subjects. This finding supports the clinical relevance of longitudinal MRI analysis, as structural changes evolve gradually and may not be fully captured by single time-point assessments. Across correctly classified samples, the model consistently attended to anatomically meaningful brain regions, supporting the clinical plausibility of the learned representations. For subjects diagnosed with Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Grad-CAM activations were predominantly concentrated in the temporal and frontal regions, which are widely reported to be affected by neurodegenerative processes associated with AD, including hippocampal atrophy and cortical thinning. These activation patterns suggest that the model captures structural changes relevant to disease progression rather than relying on superficial image characteristics. In contrast, samples from the mild cognitive impairment (MCI) group exhibited more diffuse and heterogeneous activation patterns. This observation aligns with the clinical nature of MCI as a transitional stage, where structural abnormalities are subtler and less localized compared to AD. Cognitively normal (CN) subjects generally showed weaker and less focused activations, indicating the absence of pronounced disease-related structural features. Importantly, the Grad-CAM responses were consistent across the five adjacent sagittal slices used in the 2.5D input representation. This consistency demonstrates that the model integrates contextual information across slices instead of relying on a single anatomical plane. Such behavior supports the effectiveness of the proposed 2.5D strategy as a compromise between purely 2D and fully 3D approaches, balancing anatomical context with computational efficiency. At the same time, these explanations should be interpreted cautiously. Because AD recall remained limited, the Grad-CAM examples for AD reflect only a small subset of correctly classified cases and should not be interpreted as representative of all AD samples in the dataset. Overall, the XAI analysis indicates that the trained model bases its predictions on anatomically and clinically relevant regions, thereby enhancing the transparency, interpretability, and trustworthiness of the classification outcomes. Similar observations have been reported in prior explainable deep learning studies for MRI-based Alzheimer’s disease analysis ( Böhle et al., 2019 , Montavon et al., 2018 ). The observed Grad-CAM activation patterns are consistent with previously reported neuroanatomical changes in Alzheimer’s disease, particularly involving temporal and frontal regions ( Jack et al., 2010 , Junhao Wen et al., 2020 ). 4.4 Limitations Despite the encouraging findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the proposed framework relies exclusively on structural T1-weighted MRI data. While such images capture macroscopic anatomical changes, they may not fully reflect functional or molecular alterations associated with early disease stages. Incorporating multimodal information, such as positron emission tomography (PET) imaging or cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers, may further enhance diagnostic performance. Second, although the 2.5D approach captures inter-slice contextual information, it does not exploit the full volumetric representation available in three-dimensional MRI data. Fully 3D convolutional networks may offer richer spatial modeling, albeit at the cost of increased computational and memory demands. Future work may explore hybrid or lightweight 3D architectures to address this trade-off. Third, class imbalance remains a challenge, particularly for the AD category. While class weighting was applied during training, residual imbalance may still influence generalization performance, as reflected in the modest test results for minority classes. Larger and more balanced datasets would be beneficial for more robust evaluation. Fourth, the training curves indicate that overfitting begins early, with validation loss reaching its minimum at an early epoch and not improving consistently thereafter. Although the best-validation checkpoint was retained for final evaluation, stronger regularization and augmentation strategies should be explored in future work. Fifth, while we added a scan-level split experiment and a revised 2D baseline to strengthen methodological comparison, we did not perform a full ablation study across multiple architectures such as 3D CNNs, DenseNet variants, or transformer-based models. Sixth, the Grad-CAM analysis, while supported by both qualitative and quantitative summaries, remains constrained to correctly classified cases and therefore does not fully characterize model behavior across all AD samples. Finally, the experiments were conducted using a single dataset derived from the ADNI cohort. Consequently, the generalizability of the proposed framework to external datasets or real-world clinical settings remains to be validated. Cross-dataset evaluation and prospective studies will be necessary before clinical deployment can be considered. 4.5 Clinical implications The proposed longitudinal and explainable deep learning framework has several potential implications for clinical neuroimaging practice. By enforcing subject-level longitudinal splitting and incorporating temporal MRI information, the model aligns more closely with real-world diagnostic workflows, where repeated scans from the same patient are routinely available. This design was adopted to improve methodological validity and reduce the risk of subject leakage, even though our additional scan-level experiment did not show a substantial performance increase. Moreover, the integration of Grad-CAM explanations enhances the transparency of the model by highlighting anatomically relevant brain regions associated with disease progression. Such visual explanations may assist clinicians in validating model predictions against known neurodegenerative patterns, potentially increasing trust and facilitating human–AI collaboration rather than replacing expert judgment. In particular, the ability to visualize regions implicated in Alzheimer’s disease and mild cognitive impairment may support longitudinal monitoring and early intervention strategies. While the current framework is not intended for standalone clinical diagnosis, it may serve as a decision-support tool to complement radiological assessment and cognitive evaluation. With further validation on external cohorts and integration of multimodal biomarkers, the proposed approach could contribute to more interpretable and robust AI-assisted systems for the longitudinal assessment of neurodegenerative disorders. 5 Conclusion This study presented an explainable deep learning framework for longitudinal classification of Alzheimer’s disease progression using T1-weighted MRI data from the ADNI cohort. By adopting a 2.5D convolutional neural network architecture, the proposed approach incorporated contextual information from adjacent slices while remaining computationally tractable for longitudinal neuroimaging analysis. The experimental results showed that the model was able to differentiate among cognitively normal, mild cognitive impairment, and Alzheimer’s disease subjects under a strict subject-level longitudinal evaluation protocol. However, the achieved classification performance remained moderate, particularly for the AD class. Accordingly, the proposed framework should be interpreted primarily as a methodologically rigorous and interpretable baseline rather than a high-performing clinical classifier. Unlike many prior studies that rely on scan-level or cross-sectional splits, the present work emphasizes methodological rigor through subject-level longitudinal evaluation. In an additional scan-level split experiment, we did not observe a substantial performance increase. Therefore, the value of the subject-level split in this study is best understood in terms of longitudinal validity and reduced leakage risk, rather than as evidence that scan-level splitting necessarily inflates performance in every implementation. A central contribution of this work lies in the integration of explainable artificial intelligence through Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM). Beyond qualitative visualization, the framework also examined temporal changes in Grad-CAM activations across follow-up visits. The observed explanation patterns were anatomically plausible and showed relative temporal stability within the analyzed subset. However, because the AD recall remained limited, these explainability results should be interpreted cautiously and not as fully representative of all AD cases. Overall, this study shows that longitudinal neuroimaging analysis benefits from considering not only predictive accuracy, but also interpretability, temporal consistency, and evaluation validity. The proposed framework provides a reproducible and interpretable baseline for longitudinal Alzheimer’s disease modeling while also highlighting the remaining challenges in robust AD classification. Future work should explore stronger regularization, fuller architectural comparison, multimodal biomarkers, and external cohort validation to move toward more clinically reliable and transparent AI-assisted decision support for neurodegenerative disease assessment.

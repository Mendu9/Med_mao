Introduction Ageing is associated with the accumulation of neurodegenerative and vascular pathologies in the brain, which can lead to the development of cognitive impairment and dementia. In 2024, an estimated 6.9 million Americans aged 65 years and older live with Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the most common cause of dementia, with vascular dementia (VD) and dementia with Lewy bodies (LBD) following. 1 2 , 3 A clinical dementia diagnosis work-up relies on history taking, physical examination, cognitive testing, blood work and neuroimaging biomarkers. Neuroimaging biomarkers are particularly interesting owing to the range of modalities and sequences available. MRI scans are usually included as a part of the initial clinical assessment of dementia, providing the opportunity to detect and quantify different neurodegenerative pathologies. 4-6 MRI has been most frequently used for detecting and quantifying AD-related hippocampal atrophy, 7-10 in addition to different endophenotypes of vascular brain injury, such as white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), 7 , 11-13 haemorrhages, 1 , 14 , 15 infarcts 16 and enlarged perivascular spaces. 17 For LBD, MRI scans usually present preserved medial temporal lobe volume 18 and occipital atrophy. 19 Other neuroimaging biomarkers, such as amyloid-β (Aβ), tauopathy captured with PET scans, and α-synuclein detected by seed amplification assays for Lewy body pathology, 20-22 can also support the diagnosis. 23 , 24 However, those are not usually included in the initial assessment and thus have limited clinical availability. Therefore, many MRI-based neuroimaging studies have been designed to use machine-learning models to predict clinical dementia diagnosis. 25 , 26 7 , 27-29 However, clinical diagnosis faces multiple challenges regarding diagnostic accuracy, where neuropathology is considered the diagnostic gold standard, 30-32 because neurodegenerative diseases are characterized histopathologically by the deposition of misfolded proteins with different spreading patterns and underlying biological mechanisms. Thus, machine-learning-based biomarker development using clinical rather than neuropathological diagnosis might lead to misestimation, inaccurate cut-offs 33-36 and limited accuracy. 36 8 , 37-40 Recently, the emergence of deep learning has significantly transformed the traditional machine-learning approach. With their flexible architectures, deep-learning models require only minimal pre-processing while reaching state-of-the-art diagnostic accuracy. 41 However, most deep-learning-based biomarker developments 42 still leverage clinical diagnosis, potentially limiting their accuracy in predicting histopathological outcomes. Moreover, recent work has shown that deep neural network decisions are biologically informed, as evidenced by hippocampus-focused explainable heat maps in AD classification tasks, 41 which is not the case for the traditional machine-learning models. 43-45 Most previous studies have focused on binary tasks. This work could be among the first attempts to extract explainable heat maps for multiple co-pathologies from a single multi-label model. 45 Previously, we have developed a set of machine-learning-derived SPARE (spatial pattern of atrophy for recognition) single-valued metrics that capture distinct dimensions of brain alterations linked to cognitive decline. For instance, SPARE-AD 46 quantifies AD-like atrophy, whereas SPARE-BA 47 measures atrophy related to brain ageing. However, these indices relied on traditional support vector machine (SVM) methods and did not consider the possibility of co-pathologies. 48 Post-mortem studies showed that most dementia cases are heterogeneous, with multiple co-pathologies. 30-32 , 49 This underscores the need for more sophisticated methods to disentangle dementia heterogeneity 30 using 50 in vivo imaging. In this study, we develop a multi-label deep-learning network trained with neuropathologically confirmed diagnoses to quantify how atrophy relates to the three most common co-pathologies. We use a combined dataset from two large neuroimaging repositories: the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) and the National Alzheimer’s Coordinating Center (NACC). 37 Specifically, our models are trained to detect the presence of AD, VD and LBD pathologies in a total of 423 demented and 361 control participants using their antemortem T 51 1 -weighted MRI scans. In addition, we benchmark our deep-learning model against traditional machine-learning techniques by training multi-label random forest (RF) and linear SVM 52 models for comparative analysis. Then, we generate explainable deep-learning heat maps to reveal the brain changes detected by the model and develop DeepSPARE indices, the advanced indices specifically designed to detect brain changes associated with multiple co-pathologies: AD (DeepSPARE-AD), VD (DeepSPARE-VD) and LBD (DeepSPARE-LBD). The DeepSPARE indices are further validated using a set of cognitive, neuropathology and neuroimaging measures, and our findings are replicated on an out-of-sample cohort of ADNI participants. 53 Materials and methods Neuroimaging data and neuropathological diagnosis criteria The machine-learning models were trained with 3D T 1 -weighted MRI scans provided by NACC and ADNI. 51 For each study participant, the most recent MRI scan was selected to optimize the proximity between these antemortem scans and the neuropathological diagnosis obtained at the time of death. A total of 784 participants were included in this study to derive our machine-learning models: 423 neuropathology-identified demented participants and 361 cognitively healthy controls. The 423 demented participants were identified as having AD, VD or LBD pathology based on the following criteria. Participants had AD pathology if their AD neuropathological change scores, a composite score of Braak staging, 37 Thal phase 54 and Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer's Disease (CERAD) score, 55 reflected moderate or high AD neuropathological change. 56 Participants who had VD pathology were selected by the presence of moderate or severe arteriosclerosis, cerebral amyloid angiopathy or infarcts and haemorrhages. 57 LBD pathology participants were selected based on limbic or neocortical presence of Lewy body pathology. 58 An out-of-sample ADNI dataset was included in the study to validate our findings, consisting of 1041 participants diagnosed with AD or mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and controls. 19 Table 1 describes their demographic characteristics. Data processing A fully automatic image segmentation pipeline, the multi-atlas region segmentation utilizing ensembles pipeline, was used to correct field bias and skull-strip all the T 1 -weighted brain scans. The skull-stripped scans were then registered to the 1 mm 59 3 resolution 2009c version of the ICBM152 MNI atlas of size 193 × 229 × 193 using the non-rigid SyN registration method 60 provided with the ANTs library (v. 2.3.4). 61 Then, all scans were downsampled to a size of 65 × 77 × 65 using the Nilearn Python package 62 resample_img . This downsampling was introduced to reduce computational time, allowing for the exploration of a broad set of statistical models and the conducting of extended cross-validation and replication experiments. The downsampling has also produced smaller deep networks, usually associated with lower risks of overfitting and improved accuracy. 63 After downsampling, each scan intensity was normalized using the min–max normalization approach. 64 Multi-label classification models Three machine-learning models were trained to predict the neuropathological diagnosis from the processed 3D T 1 -weighted MRI scans: RF models, ensemble linear SVM models, and deep neural networks based on a 3D ResNet architecture. In a multi-label classification task, the model aims at predicting more than one mutually non-exclusive label. This framework allows each input to be associated with multiple labels simultaneously. Here, the model must predict three labels for each subject, representing the likelihood of AD, VD and LBD. This approach allows the model to identify the presence or absence of each condition independently, providing a comprehensive assessment of the potential pathologies of the subject. A detailed explanation can be found in 65 Supplementary material, ‘S2’ . A multi-label asymmetric loss was adopted to mitigate the effects of class imbalance for multi-label deep neural networks. This asymmetric loss exhibits two advantages: it reduces the impact of imbalanced classes during binary classification, and it reduces the influence of easily classified negative samples. 66 The asymmetric loss was compared with a standard binary cross-entropy loss. A detailed explanation can be found in the 66 Supplementary material, ‘S2’ . Because a multi-label classification cannot be conducted directly using a single SVM, an ensemble of three linear SVM binary classifiers was trained, one per pathology, to accomplish each multi-label task. The code from this study is available at https://github.com/UTHSCSA-NAL/DeepSPARE . Model evaluation The classification performance was 10-fold cross-validated and evaluated by the following classification metrics: a multi-label precision, a multi-label recall, a multi-label F1 score, a balanced accuracy (BACC) for each dementia pathology, an average balanced accuracy (BACC avg ), and the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) for each dementia pathology. These metrics are defined below, and a more detailed explanation of these metrics can be found in the Supplementary material, ‘S3’ . Here, TP and TN denote true-positive and true-negative values, and FP and FN denote false-positive and false-negative values; BACC AD , BACC VD and BACC LBD denote the balanced accuracy for AD, VD and LBD pathology, respectively. (1) precision = T P AD + T P VD + T P LBD T P AD + T P VD + T P LBD + F P AD + F P VD + F P LBD (2) recall = T P AD + T P VD + T P LBD T P AD + T P VD + T P LBD + F N AD + F N VD + F N LBD (3) F 1 = 2 × precision × recall precision + recall (4) BACC = 1 2 [ TP TP + N + TN TN + P ] (5) BAC C avg = 1 3 [ BAC C AD + BAC C VD + BAC C LBD ] For the three types of multi-label models, the model achieving the best F1 and BACC avg among tested parameters was retained to derive ROC curves and further analyses. The AUC of those three best models was compared using the Delong test. 67 Model interpretation The machine-learning models were first interpreted by deriving pathology probability charts directly from the outputs of best RF, SVM and deep neural networks to evaluate how well they disentangled dementia pathologies. A silhouette score was calculated for each machine-learning model to quantify its ability to separate the three pathologies. The silhouette score is a standard measure of cluster separability, 68 where a higher silhouette score indicates better separability between given clusters. Three pathology probability charts were derived for each model: one plot for each pair of pathologies, reporting the likelihood of one T 68 1 MRI scan having the pathology investigated in this study. The RF model and deep neural networks can output predicted probability directly, whereas the SVM scores produced by the SVM models were transformed into probabilities using the default cross-validated logistic regression method implemented in the scikit-learn library. 69 Then, a set of explainable heat maps was derived to highlight the brain regions considered by the best SVM and the best deep-learning model to support their predictions. SVM heat maps were generated from SVM coefficients, and each pathology heat map was generated from the corresponding binary SVM model. Based on our previous study, where we observed that the integrated gradients (IG) 45 heat maps were better at capturing brain alterations associated with AD than heat maps generated via guided gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (grad-CAM) 70 and layer-wise relevance propagation, 71 the IG method is used to derive both group and individual explainable deep-learning heat maps. Group deep-learning heat maps are created by averaging the heat maps of all subjects within the same pathology group, resulting in a robust representation of brain alterations specific to each pathology. 44 Individual deep-learning heat maps, derived directly from each subject, may serve as a diagnostic fingerprint tool. 44 , 45 Gaussian noise with a zero mean and a standard deviation of 0.1, similar to the noise introduced during data augmentation, is used as the baseline for IG. All maps were smoothed by applying a Gaussian kernel with a size of one voxel of standard deviation. A detailed explanation of IG can be found in the 44 Supplementary material, ‘S4’ . DeepSPARE indices evaluation against cognitive testing, neuropathology and neuroimaging measures The DeepSPARE indices were generated by performing the logit transformation of the probability of the best-performing deep-learning model. Three indices were generated for each pathology: DeepSPARE-AD, DeepSPARE-VD and DeepSPARE-LBD. A set of measures was used to validate these indices: three cognitive scores, six neuropathology measures and two neuroimaging measures. Specifically, three scores capturing the global, executive and episodic memory cognitive performance were investigated: the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), the Trail Making Test B (Trail B) 72 and the immediate logical memory subtest of the Wechsler Memory Scale-Revised. 73 Six neuropathology measures were examined: arteriolosclerosis, 74 cerebral amyloid angiopathy, 75 Thal phase, 76 Braak stage, 55 CERAD 54 score and Lewy body stages. 77 Two neuroimaging measures were also evaluated: the hippocampal and white matter hyperintensity (WMH) volumes. Those imaging measures were performed by the IDeA Lab (University of California, Davis) for the NACC dataset, following ADNI protocols. Grey matter segmentation is based on an expectation-maximization algorithm, 78 and WMH is calculated based on fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) and 3D T 79 , 80 1 MRI. For more details, please refer to the NACC and ADNI websites. 81 The associations between the aforementioned measures and the DeepSPARE indices were examined using generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs). A Gaussian conditional distribution with an identity link function was specified for continuous outcomes, and a binomial conditional distribution with a logit link function was specified for dichotomous outcomes. In these models, the respective cognitive, neuropathology or neuroimaging measure of interest was the outcome, and the DeepSPARE indices were the main predictors (expressed as z -scores to facilitate comparisons between their effect estimates). The models were adjusted for age and sex and included random intercepts for different study sites and different MRI scanners, assuming a partially crossed random effects design (for more details, see Supplementary material, ‘S5’ ). Considering the possibility of strong correlations between the different DeepSPARE indices (owing to the frequent occurrence of co-pathologies), we calculated their respective variance inflation factors (VIFs) in each model, to assess the degree of collinearity. VIF values of more than five were considered concerning for collinearity, based on more conservative literature-derived thresholds. 82 False discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted 82 , 83 P -values and 95% confidence intervals for coefficient 84 were computed for all models. An FDR-adjusted 85 P -value ( P FDR ) < 0.05 is considered to be significant. DeepSPARE indices evaluation using out-of-sample dataset A total of 1041 ADNI participants, independent of the model training group, were used to generate the pathology probability charts and validate the DeepSPARE indices. ADNI participants were divided into three groups: AD and MCI amyloid-β positive (AD/MCI Αβ+ ) group, MCI amyloid-β negative (MCI Aβ− ) group, and control group (CN). The AD/MCI Αβ+ group was selected based on positive amyloid PET or CSF amyloid < 977 pg/ml. The MCI 86 Aβ− and CN groups were selected based on negative amyloid PET and CSF amyloid ≥ 977 pg/ml. The total numbers for AD, MCI Αβ+ , MCI Aβ− and CN groups were 314, 420, 161 and 146, respectively. The DeepSPARE indices for these 1041 ADNI participants were generated through the following process. Initially, the 10 models from the best-performing multi-label deep-learning model, developed during 10-fold cross-validation, were applied to each of the processed T 1 -weighted MRI scans. This results in 10 results for each subject and for each pathology. Then these 10 results were averaged and converted into a probability for each pathology using a sigmoid transformation. These probabilities were used to create pathology probability charts. Finally, the probabilities were transformed into the DeepSPARE indices using the logit function. For this out-of-sample dataset, the associations of DeepSPARE indices with cognitive scores, neuroimaging, plasma and CSF measures were tested with GLMMs structured in a similar mmanner to those described in the previous section. Those measures are MMSE, Trail B, ADAS-Cog 13, the volume of the hippocampus and WMH, SPARE-Tau 87 (a machine-learning-derived index based on tau PET scans), plasma Aβ 88 42 , plasma tau phosphorylated at threonine-181 (pTau181), 89 the ratio of CSF pTau181/Aβ 90 42 , CSF pTau181 91 and the presence of α-synuclein in CSF by using the seed amplification assays 91 method provided by Amprion Clinical Laboratory 24 (please refer to ADNI website for more details). 92 , 93 Results Clinical and neuropathological diagnoses Figure 1A summarizes the 423 neuropathology-confirmed diagnoses of the demented participants and 361 control participants included as the training set in our machine-learning models. The mean age of demented and control participants was 76.8 and 75.6 years, respectively. The number of female/male participants in demented and control participant groups was 130/231 and 146/277, respectively. There were no significant age ( P = 0.11), sex ( P = 0.9) or education ( P = 0.08) differences between the demented and control participants. The mean time interval between death and the scan date of included MRI scans of the demented participants was 4.9 years. The majority of the 423 demented participants presented multiple co-pathologies: 300 participants had at least two different neurodegenerative pathologies (70.9%), including 54 participants who had all three pathologies (12.8%). Only 123 participants had a single pathology (29.1%): 51 had AD (12.19%), 51 had VD (12.1%) and 21 had LBD (4.9%). Those three pathologies formed seven neuropathology-confirmed diagnostic categories, and they were compared with the clinical diagnoses released by the NACC and the ADNI consortia, as shown in Fig. 1B . Among the 312 participants who had AD pathology, 242 (77.6%) had received a clinical diagnosis of AD, 52 (16.7%) had received an MCI clinical diagnosis, and 70 (22.4%) had received a non-AD clinical diagnosis. A total of 243 (72.8%) of the 334 participants who had VD pathology had been clinically diagnosed as having AD, and 3 (0.9%) had been clinically diagnosed as having VD. Of the 131 participants who had LBD pathology, only 23 (17.6%) had been clinically diagnosed as having LBD, with most of the remaining individuals having received an AD diagnosis (75, 57.3%). These counts indicate a considerable overlap in pathology among individuals with dementia and a substantial discrepancy between clinical and neuropathological diagnoses. Pathology probability charts The AD, VD and LBD probabilities directly generated by the three best machine-learning models were used to produce the pathology probability charts shown in Fig. 2B . A larger silhouette score was measured for the deep-learning model (0.209) compared with the SVM model (silhouette score = −0.162) and RF model (silhouette score = −0.193) ( Fig. 2B ), indicating a better separability of the pathology groups from the deep-learning model. Deep-learning model was the only multi-label model able to disentangle all the pathologies; it performed well in AD and VD classification and reached a BACC LBD of 0.623 for LBD. The SVM model was able to differentiate between AD and VD but struggled to detect LBD, achieving a BACC LBD of 0.531. The RF model failed to distinguish the different neurodegenerative pathologies and categorized study participants only as diseased or healthy. Discussion In this study, we developed an advanced multi-label deep-learning framework designed to disentangle the three most common co-pathologies (AD, VD and LBD) using antemortem T 1 -weighted MRI scans. Explainable deep-learning heat maps for these co-pathologies were generated from a single model, highlighting hippocampal alterations for AD, white matter changes for VD, and occipital regions for LBD. The novel neuroimaging signatures derived from our framework, the DeepSPARE indices, representing the likelihood of each co-pathology, have revealed significant associations with pathology-specific biomarkers and measures. Specifically, the DeepSPARE-AD index was significantly associated with key indicators of AD, including hippocampal volume, Braak stages, CERAD scores and Thal phases. The DeepSPARE-VD index showed significant associations with WMH and cerebral amyloid angiopathy. The DeepSPARE-LBD index was significantly associated with Lewy body stages. The pathology sensitivity of DeepSPARE indices has also been replicated in an out-of-sample dataset. This framework broadly extends the previous machine-learning-based neuroimaging signatures by enabling a more precise disentanglement of the complex interplay between multiple co-pathologies through model explainable heat maps and quantitative DeepSPARE indices, serving as an advanced tool to understand and detect the heterogeneity of dementia. 46-48 A significant discrepancy between clinical and neuropathological diagnoses We observed a substantial discrepancy between the released clinical diagnoses and the neuropathologically confirmed diagnoses. Specifically, 300 (71%) of the 423 demented participants had more than one pathology, and 283 (67%) of them had been clinically diagnosed as AD only. This observation is in line with recent clinical studies and post-mortem autopsy examinations 31 reporting the frequent co-existence of AD with other neurodegenerative disorders and highlighting the challenges in distinguishing the predominant pathology in clinical dementia cases (AD versus VD 30 , 32 , 94 or LBD 75 , 95 ). 96 The deep-learning model achieved the highest overall accuracy The best deep neural networks with asymmetric loss function achieved the overall best performance compared with RF and ensemble SVM models, with a balanced accuracy of 0.844, 0.839 and 0.623 for AD, VD and LBD, respectively. Additional parameters were a precision of 0.701, a recall of 0.793 and an F1 of 0.744. Although class imbalance is the most common limitation of multi-label classification studies, in the present study this issue was mitigated, in part, by exploring the use of asymmetric loss during the training of our deep neural networks. The balanced accuracy for LBD could be considered satisfactory, considering that structural neuroimaging might exhibit a less characteristic signature for LBD pathology, which is more associated with reduced functional signals in the occipital lobe and relative sparing of metabolism in the posterior cingulate cortex in PET imaging. 19 , 97 19 , 97 , 98 As a comparison, the best ensemble SVM model achieved a lower balanced accuracy for AD and VD, a much lower accuracy for LBD, a lower F1 score and a lower averaged balanced accuracy compared with the best deep-learning model. The higher precision with a much lower recall observed from the SVM model indicates that it is confident about easy positives but fails to detect hard positives, such as LBD, resulting in more false negatives. The reason for the relatively lower precision of deep learning with an asymmetric loss function, but not with a binary cross-entropy loss function, might stem from the probability shifting mechanism of the asymmetric loss function, where it omits easy negative samples and thus might lead to more false positives. Overall, based on the two composite metrics (F1 and BACC avg ) the deep-learning model achieved the best performance. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1 , around half of the MRI scans used to train the model were acquired in 1.5 T scanners and the rest in 3 T scanners. Including scanners of different field strengths doubled our sample size and was intended to make our statistical models more robust to inter-scanner differences. The influence of scanner properties and scanning protocol will be an essential topic to explore while expanding our approach once data from diverse scanners are available in the future. The pathology probability charts derived from the machine-learning model outputs established that the model reaching the best classification performance was also the best at disentangling the three neurodegenerative pathologies by achieving the highest silhouette score. Explainable deep-learning heat maps revealed pathology-specific patterns The brain alterations revealed by the group deep-learning heat maps from the best deep neural networks confirmed that the model was focusing on distinct brain regions when classifying the three pathologies, and we found that these deep-learning heat maps closely correspond to prior findings. Specifically, the AD deep-learning heat map accurately captured bilateral hippocampal regions, consistent with numerous previous AD studies. Additionally, the VD deep-learning heat map highlighted many white matter regions commonly affected by WMH or other vascular endophenotypes. 11 , 99 , 100 Lastly, the LBD deep-learning heat map focused on the occipital region. This result is consistent with the fact that LBD is closely related to occipital changes. 101-103 However, previous studies investigating MRI-based structural atrophy in LBD have produced conflicting results, with some studies reporting atrophy only in the limbic system. 20-22 These contradictory findings could potentially result from the presence of co-pathologies of AD. 104 , 105 Overall, the deep-learning heat maps indicate that the deep neural networks could associate the three neuropathology-confirmed diagnoses with distinct brain alterations, which was not the case for the SVM model. The SVM coefficient maps captured more extensive changes throughout the grey matter for all three diagnoses and failed to provide distinguishing information. This result is consistent with our previous finding, demonstrating the advantage of capturing dementia patterns using modern deep-learning heat maps rather than the coefficients of SVM. 45 DeepSPARE indices were associated with pathology-specific biomarkers and measures Our deep-learning-derived DeepSPARE indices presented diverse sensitivities to the three most frequent co-pathologies. Significant associations with DeepSPARE-AD were observed for the MMSE, Trail B, memory, hippocampal volume, Braak stage, CERAD score and Thal phase. VD is associated with a higher prevalence of white matter diseases and vascular brain injury, and our results revealed significant associations of DeepSPARE-VD with cerebral amyloid angiopathy and WMH. Lewy body stage was significantly associated only with DeepSPARE-LBD. These results suggest that the DeepSPARE indices are sensitive and specific to well-established AD, VD and LBD biomarkers and measurements. 101 , 106 , 107 The out-of-sample validation in the ADNI dataset further strengthens the generalizability and external validity of our findings. The AD/MCI Αβ+ group exhibited robust and consistent associations with the tested cognitive, imaging, plasma and CSF measures. These associations were similar to those observed in the training/testing data. Specifically, DeepSPARE-AD was associated with cognitive scores and hippocampal volume. In contrast, DeepSPARE-VD was associated with WMH volume, confirming the ability of DeepSPARE to reflect changes related to AD and VD pathology in an unseen dataset. Previous studies have demonstrated the ability of CSF pTau181/Aβ-42 ratio and pTau181 86 , 91 to predict the early clinical decline and future AD conversion. Our findings revealed consistent results where DeepSPARE-AD indices were significantly associated with the CSF pTau181/Aβ-42 ratio and pTau181. Additionally, recent studies have shown that CSF α-synuclein seed amplification assay is a potential early biomarker for synucleinopathies and can diagnose Parkinson’s disease patients with high accuracy. 90 , 108 Following these reports, we observed a significant association of CSF α-synuclein seed amplification assays only with DeepSPARE-LBD. Plasma biomarkers are particularly interesting because of their convenience and minimally invasive nature. Plasma pTau181 has been proposed as a potential plasma biomarker for AD. 23 , 24 , 109 , 110 Correspondingly, we observed a significant association between DeepSPARE-AD and plasma pTau181. 89 , 90 Clinical relevance of the DeepSPARE indices Non-invasive pathology surrogate biomarkers could be crucial in advancing our understanding of the heterogeneity and management of neurodegenerative disorders. Clinically, these indices could serve as valuable imaging markers, particularly in cases of atypical, complex and concurrent dementia presentations. One striking finding of our present study is the improved understanding of the role of WMH in dementia. Our previous research in the general population 50 demonstrated a strong relationship between WMH and SPARE-AD values, a finding replicated in multiple cohorts. 1 However, given that these training sets relied only on clinical diagnosis of AD and ignored the possibilities of co-existing pathology, the previous indices were not able to distinguish dementia of AD from VD. 46 This disentanglement was achievable only when our advanced deep-learning framework was developed, where we found a more specific role of WMH in VD because they were associated only with DeepSPARE-VD when adjusted for other indices. Acknowledging that these pathologies might co-exist, the DeepSPARE indices can provide sensitive and specific markers of these pathologies. 1 , 46 Furthermore, from a clinical trial perspective, the DeepSPARE indices could help to identify at-risk populations with subclinical neuropathology, enabling diagnosis during the extended latency period between neuropathological changes and the onset of clinical symptoms. Our previous work has simulated the effect of improving statistical power in clinical trials in prodromal Alzheimer’s disease. In future research, the DeepSPARE idices might facilitate the identification of high-risk cohorts and act as a tool for monitoring the neuropathology burden in developing and testing new or repurposed disease-modifying treatments. 111 Strengths and limitations This study builds on several strengths, such as the use of neuropathologically confirmed diagnoses instead of clinical diagnoses, the adoption of multi-label deep-learning networks to diagnose co-pathologies based on antemortem 3D T 1 -weighted MRI scans with minimal pre-processing, and the derivation of explainable deep-learning heat maps to visualize the patterns of brain alteration of three most common co-pathologies from a single model. The combination of two large sets of neuroimaging data to establish and validate the statistical models is also a strength of the present study. Furthermore, the introduction of the new deep-learning-derived DeepSPARE indices is another notable strength. We have extensively validated DeepSPARE indices with a wide range of clinical, imaging and biofluid biomarkers and measures, demonstrating their abilities to reflect AD, VD and LBD pathologies individually and potentially serve as non-invasive pathology biomarkers. Those indices, based on T 1 -weighted images, could exhibit broad applicability in both clinical and research settings, because T 1 sequences are already part of the majority of clinical and research MRI protocols. The main limitation of the study resides in the data imbalance across neuropathology subgroups, with a notably larger number of AD and VD patients compared with other subgroups. This disparity could result in superior model detections for these two pathologies. Our present work demonstrated the ability to differentiate AD, VD and LBD with great sensitivity and precision from a single T 1 MR scan; however, other pathologies, such as limbic-predominant age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy and frontotemporal dementia, were under-represented owing to limited sample sizes. In the meantime, we are actively working to expand our training sample and establish more extensive databases that integrate neuroimaging with post-mortem evaluations. 112 Moreover, the tau PET dataset was limited in the MCI 30 Αβ− and CN groups, making it challenging to compute SPARE-Tau indices for these populations. Additionally, integrating other imaging modalities could have significantly improved the model accuracy, because PET and FLAIR scans provide more informative neuroimaging signatures for LBD 88 and VD, 19 respectively. Overall, additional data will be required to reach a sufficient classification accuracy for use in routine clinical practice and extend the models to other related dementias, such as limbic-predominant age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy and frontotemporal dementias. 1 Conclusion This study demonstrates the benefits of advanced deep-learning models in detecting the three most common dementia co-pathologies, revealing neuroimaging patterns of brain alteration specific to each pathology and developing innovative deep-learning-derived DeepSPARE indices. More specifically, the deep-learning model achieves the highest overall accuracy compared with other machine-learning models in identifying neuropathologically confirmed dementia diagnoses. The explainable deep-learning heat maps uncover distinctive structural neuroimaging patterns of brain alteration for AD in the hippocampus, VD in white matter, and LBD in occipital regions, which is not the case for the traditional machine-learning method. Lastly, the DeepSPARE indices demonstrate effective, consistent and robust pathology-specific associations across a variety of cognitive tests, neuropathology, imaging, plasma and CSF biomarkers and measures. They have also been validated and replicated by an out-of-sample dataset. Clinically, the DeepSPARE indices hold promise as non-invasive diagnostic tools, offering the ability to differentiate between co-occurring neurodegenerative pathologies and identify at-risk populations before symptoms become apparent. Furthermore, these indices could potentially enhance diagnostic accuracy compared with the current clinical diagnostic criteria and thus provide treatment strategies for specific pathologies.

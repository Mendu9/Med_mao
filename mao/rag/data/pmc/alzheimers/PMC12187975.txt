1 BACKGROUND Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) and Alzheimer's disease (AD) are the two main cognitive and behavioral diseases responsible for dementia. The neuropathological characteristics of DLB include the presence of misfolded α‐synuclein, the main component of Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites in the neurons. 1 In the extracellular environment, amyloid plaques are frequently found in half of patients. 2 The presence of these amyloid lesions in DLB correlates with a decrease in amyloid beta (Aβ)42 in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). 3 In prodromal DLB (Pro‐DLB), low Aβ42 is found in 3% to 32% of cases. 4 , 5 The proportion of amyloid lesions then appears to increase over time, because more than half of patients with dementia have a decrease in Aβ42. 6 More generally, AD and DLB are the two diseases for which there is an association with neurodegenerative lesions other than their own lesions, more specifically, the presence of amyloid lesions in DLB and the presence of Lewy bodies in AD. 5 7 Some studies have compared the clinical course between DLB and AD patients with dementia. According to a systematic review of six studies ( N = 841 patients) there is no difference in terms of cognition between AD and DLB. In the same way, a study of 1290 patients followed for 3 years did not find a difference between DLB and AD for global cognitive decline (Mini‐Mental State Examination [MMSE]). 8 Interestingly, the combination of DLB and AD seems to accelerate cognitive decline; thus, in a study with a mean follow‐up of 5.88 years and 1151 patients, we demonstrated a similar rate of MMSE decline for DLB patients and AD patients, but a higher rate of decline for DLB and AD patients. 9 Nelson et al. also showed an acceleration of cognitive decline if the two diseases were associated. 10 DLB patients with a decrease in Aβ42 compared to those without have a clearer cognitive decline after 2 years. 11 12 In the context of mild cognitive impairment (MCI), a prospective study in Amsterdam of 197 patients with a mean follow‐up of 3.0 years found that prodromal AD patients declined faster than prodromal DLB patients in terms of MMSE score, and another from Newcastle‐upon‐Tyne with 126 patients with at least 3 years follow‐up did not find this difference. 13 Using the Everyday Cognition scale questionnaire, which subjectively assesses cognitive abilities by the patient and the study partner, Thomas et al. have recently demonstrated that in Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) patients with pathological α‐synuclein, Aβ+ status resulted in greater subjective worsening. 14 The BioFINDER study showed that in patients with cognitive impairment (i.e., MCI or dementia) but also in cognitively unimpaired patients, the association of Lewy bodies and AD biological status led to greater cognitive worsening. 15 , 16 17 The aim of this study was to analyze the effect of amyloid status on changes in cognitive function, functional ability, quality of life, and brain volume in patients with very prodromal—that is, subjective cognitive impairment (SCI) to MCI stages—DLB (Pro‐DLB), AD (Pro‐AD), and the two diseases associated (Pro‐DLB+AD). 2 METHODS The Memento cohort is a clinic‐based longitudinal study aimed at investigating the evolution of a large variety of cognitive symptoms and subjective complaints over time. The protocol and sample characteristics at baseline have been described extensively elsewhere. Recruitment took place within the French national network of university‐based memory clinics (Centre Mémoire de Ressources et de Recherche [CM2R]). Among the 26 CM2Rs of the Memento cohort (totaling 2323 patients consecutively included from April 2011 to June 2014), 12 CM2Rs agreed to participate in the Lewy Memento substudy. 18 18 RESEARCH IN CONTEXT Systematic review : The authors reviewed the literature using PubMed. Although several papers have studied dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) decline at the dementia stage, showing faster decline when associated with Alzheimer's disease (AD), few papers have studied the prodromal stage, and none has studied the very prodromal stage, including the stage of subjective cognitive complaints. Interpretation : Amyloid status influences the clinical, cognitive, and functional evolution of prodromal DLB (Pro‐DLB) patients, whether pure or associated with AD: amyloid beta positive (Aβ+) status worsens cognitive and functional decline. However, it has no effect on the brain volume and metabolism decline in Pro‐DLB and Pro‐DLB+AD. Future directions : This study confirms the negative clinical effect of Aβ+ status in Pro‐DLB patients, but not on structural imaging: It is an important step toward understanding the associations of neurodegenerative diseases and lesions that are so common. The next step will be to study its effect on functional magnetic resonance imaging, especially as prodromal DLB has more functional than structural changes at the very prodromal stage. 2.1 Study recruitment and follow‐up Eligible adult participants for the Memento cohort had to present at inclusion as either MCI or SCI (if ≥ 60 years). MCI was defined as performing worse than one standard deviation (SD) from the mean (compared to age and education norms) in one or more cognitive domains, this deviation being identified for the first time through cognitive tests performed recently, that is, < 6 months before the screening phase. The battery of neuropsychological tests and detailed aspects of the inclusion/exclusion criteria have been described previously for the Memento cohort. SCI was measured through visual analogue scales. Individuals included might have a Clinical Dementia Rating scale score ≤ 0.5 and are non‐demented. All participants had to have visual and auditory acuity adequate for neuropsychological testing and be affiliated with a health insurance plan. There were no additional eligible criteria to participate to the Lewy Memento substudy except that of being included in a center participating in the substudy. 18 Patients included in this follow‐up study were required to have at least two follow‐up time points 1 year apart. 2.2 Standard protocol approval, registrations, and patient consents All participants provided written informed consent for the Memento cohort and the protocol was approved by the ethics committee (Comité de Protection des Personnes sud‐ouest et outre‐mer III, approval number 2010‐A01394‐35). The protocol is registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT01926249 , https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT01926249 ). 2.3 Study examinations The data collected at the memory clinic are as previously described. The Lewy Memento substudy included three additional sections to the core Memento protocol. The first section focused on key symptoms of DLB: features of parkinsonism were evaluated using the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale, including akinesia, amimia and rigidity (rated from 0 [no symptom] to 4 [serious symptoms]), and falls. Fluctuations were assessed with the Mayo Clinic Fluctuations Scale. The Parkinson’s Disease‐Associated Psychotic Symptoms Questionnaire was used to evaluate the presence of hallucinations. 18 Rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder (RBD) was evaluated from a simplified version of a published questionnaire 19 into two questions for the patient and the caregiver, one concerning movements during sleep and the other concerning vivid dreams and nightmares. 20 2.4 Definition of exposure of interest Patients were considered to have pro‐DLB if they had SCI or MCI and at least two of the core features of DLB (fluctuations, RBD, hallucinations, and parkinsonism) during the whole follow‐up. Each symptom was considered to be present as follows: for RBD, if the score was 1 or 2 out of 2; for hallucinations, if sensation of passage, sensation of presence, illusions, non‐visual and visual hallucinations, or delusion were detected; for fluctuations, if a cut‐off score of ≥ 2/4 on the Mayo Clinic Fluctuations Scale was recorded (caregiver or patient reported); and for parkinsonism, if one criterion among akinesia, rigidity, amimia, or falls was present, as described in the McKeith et al. criteria. 21 Patients were considered to have Pro‐AD, as described in the Dubois et al. criteria, if they had a “Rappel Libre Rappel Indicé 16 items” (RL/RI‐16), French version of the Free and Cued Selective Reminding Test (FCSRT), score of < 17/48 on the 3 free recalls or < 40/48 on the 3 total recalls 22 at one visit during the follow‐up and hippocampal atrophy of > 1 on the Scheltens visual scale, 23 on at least one side and at least at one follow‐up, and were assigned to the Pro‐AD group. When visual assessment was missing but hippocampal volume was available through FreeSurfer, a multiple imputation was performed, considering age, sex, intracranial volume, brain parenchymal fraction (BPF), hippocampal volume, manufacturer, and magnet size. The level of atrophy retained for each hemisphere was the most frequent based on 100 imputations. These diagnostic criteria are derived from the AD criteria of Dubois et al., 24 from which we have removed the biological part to be able to objectively study the effect of the Aβ status whatever the diagnosis, including AD. 22 Patients were assigned to the Pro‐DLB+AD group when they met both sets of criteria (Pro‐DLB and Pro‐AD criteria). The remaining patients were assigned to the no symptom group (NS group). 2.5 Neuroimaging outcomes and neuroimaging and cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers Neuroimaging acquisitions (brain magnetic resonance imaging [MRI], 18 F‐fluorodeoxyglucose [FDG] positron emission tomography [PET], amyloid PET) were coordinated by the Centre for Automated Treatment of Images (CATI), a platform dedicated to multicenter neuroimaging. The MRI and PET protocols as well as standardization and quality control procedures were as previously described. 25 In brief, CATI harmonized MRI and PET imaging across a network of facilities. MRI physicists and engineers standardized acquisitions according to a systematic qualification procedure, ensuring parameter uniformity and image quality, and two phantom studies for PET. 18 In our sample, brain MRI with 3D T1‐weighted and 2D T2‐weighted fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery sequences (ADNI protocol) was available for 98.4% of participants (90.2% on a 3.0T MRI scan, 1.5T otherwise). Brain MRI biomarkers of interest were hippocampal volumes, and mean and regional cortical thicknesses using FreeSurfer 5.3, BPF computed from SPM12 software, and total white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) using the WHASA software. For longitudinal brain MRI, the CATI procedure consisted of visually checking, after registration, that the two time steps concerned the same subject. Management of the diversity of machines was based on the harmonization procedure, which consisted of applying the protocol defined in the ADNI project. 26 The amyloid PET (florbetapir or flutemetamol) pipeline of analysis was described previously. Brain MRI and FDG PET were done every 2 years for 4 years. 27 Detailed CSF and blood analysis aspects were described previously. Patients were defined as amyloid positive if they had a pathologic amyloid PET (florbetapir or flutemetamol) scan or CSF Aβ42 < 750 pg/mL, whichever comes first. PET images were coregistered onto the corresponding MRI and corrected for partial volume effects using the RBV‐sGTM method. For both tracers, standardized uptake value ratios (SUVRs) were calculated about a combination of the whole cerebellum and pons using the RACHEL method, with the following cutoffs: 0.88 for florbetapir and 1.0629 for flutemetamol. In the whole cohort, CSF Aβ42 using the Innotest assay and amyloid PET results (maximum delay of 3 months between both procedures) were concordant in 60/69 cases (87% [95% confidence interval 0.79–0.95]), comparable to previous findings (93%). 18 FDG PET, amyloid PET, and lumbar puncture were optional and were performed in, respectively, 70.0%, 34.2%, and 20.3% of participants. 28 2.6 Cognitive and functional outcomes A neuropsychological battery was administered as part of the Memento core study to assess a wide spectrum of cognitive abilities: global cognition with MMSE, verbal memory with RL/RI‐16 (sum of the three free recall and the three total recall), visual memory with the recall of the Rey–Osterrieth figure at 3 minutes, executive function with the Trail‐Making Test Part B (TMT‐B; in seconds), 29 and two types of fluency (semantic fluency: animals in 2 minutes; phonemic fluency with letter P in 2 minutes). 30 Cognitive and functional aspects were studied using the Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SoB) score. Quality of life was studied using the EQ‐5D‐3L instrument. 31 Four items of the Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) questionnaire (ability to use the telephone, mode of transport, responsibility for own medication, and ability to handle finances) were used as a proxy of dependence. Cognitive, functional, and quality of life were measured every year for 5 years. 32 2.7 Statistical analyses A description of analytical sample characteristics at the first interview of the Lewy Memento substudy was provided. Data are presented with numbers and percentages for qualitative variables, and with median and first and third quartile for qualitative variables. Comparisons across the four exposure categories were done using chi‐squared tests or Kruskal–Wallis tests, as appropriate. In each group (Pro‐DLB, Pro‐AD, Pro‐DLB+AD, and NS), changes in the different markers during the follow‐up were estimated and compared using linear mixed regression models. The models included a random effect on the intercept and on the slope. To test a different evolution between groups, a group x amyloid x time interaction term was introduced. A significant interaction indicates a different evolution over time between amyloid positive (Aβ+) and amyloid negative patients (Aβ–) of each group. For more flexibility, a quadratic term on time was also introduced. In all models, a systematic adjustment was made for potential confounders: sex, age at inclusion, level of education (baccalaureate yes/no), apolipoprotein E ε4 (yes/no), and for neuropsychological tests as the outcome, a first‐take term was added to capture a possible learning effect. For reasons of readability, the results are presented in graphical form, as the interaction terms and the quadratic term make the interpretation of the parameters very difficult. To consider the non‐linearity of some markers, a transformation via latent class models has been performed. 33 The evolution of the regions of interest (ROIs), BPF, and WMHs in brain MRI and the evolution of ROIs in FDG PET were analyzed following the same strategy. The mixed models included, in addition to the previous covariates (sex, age, and education), for MRI the manufacturer and the magnet size (1.5 or 3T) and for FDG PET the manufacturer. ROI measurements were standardized (mean = 0, SD = 1) to allow comparison between structures. 3 RESULTS Overall, 892 patients agreed to participate in the ancillary Lewy Memento substudy, 773 patients had at least two visits, and 342 patients had amyloid status. Among the 342 patients (the analytical sample), the pro‐DLB group comprised 99 patients (28.9%), the Pro‐AD 28 patients (8.2%), the Pro‐DLB+AD 21 patients (6.1%), and the NS 194 patients (56.7%). The median follow‐up of the 342 patients of the cohort was 5.1 years. 3.1 Patients’ characteristics The demographic, cognitive, and behavioral characteristics of the four prodromal groups are shown in Table 1 . FDG PET, fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography; NS, no symptom; SD, standard deviation. 4 DISCUSSION The aim of the study was to analyze the effect of amyloid status on the cognitive, functional, quality of life, brain volume, and metabolism decline in four groups (Pro‐DLB, Pro‐AD, Pro‐DLB+AD, and NS). Clinically, Aβ+ status resulted globally in a sharper decline than Aβ– status. This sharper decline was more frequently found in the Pro‐DLB group. Thus, at the cognitive and functional level, in the Pro‐DLB group, Aβ+ status worsened the decline compared to Aβ– status, whatever the test used, except for phonemic fluency. Interestingly, at the same time, there was no reduction in volume or metabolism in the Pro‐DLB (FDR corrected). In contrast, in the Pro‐AD group, only the MMSE, CDR‐SoB, and semantic fluency showed a greater decline in the Aβ+ subgroup than in the Aβ– subgroup. At the same time, there was a decrease in the volume of the left insula but no decrease in metabolism in the Pro‐AD group. The other two groups (Pro‐DLB+AD and NS) had intermediate results depending on the tests, and no decrease in volume or metabolism. Interestingly, the CDR‐SoB, which assesses both functional and cognitive aspects, appears to be sensitive to amyloid status: in all groups, amyloid status was associated with a decline of the CDR. The greater worsening of DLB in the case of associated amyloidopathy had previously been shown at the stage of dementia. , 12 This is the first time we have confirmed this at the very prodromal stage, including SCI and MCI patients. Interestingly, our study is consistent with the BioFINDER study, which took a purely biological approach to classifying patients by disease, whereas ours took a purely clinical approach. 34 , 16 This raises questions about the role of amyloidopathy in relation to synucleinopathy. Aβ42 and α‐synuclein follow an amyloidogenic aggregation pathway. 17 The two can form oligomers rich in β‐sheet structures that are toxic species responsible for neurodegeneration in DLB for α‐synuclein and AD for Aβ42. In AD, it is necessary to have both intracellular tauopathy and extracellular amyloidopathy for the neurodegenerative process to be optimal. It is possible that the intracellular synucleinopathy needs an extracellular amyloidopathy to develop a neurodegenerative process, or that this process is accelerated in the presence of both proteinopathies. Interestingly, 25 years ago it was discovered that a protein named NAC for “Non‐Aβ Component of AD amyloid,” in addition to Aβ42, was present in the amyloid fibrils of the neuritic and amyloid plaques of AD. 35 This NAC protein was subsequently identified as α‐synuclein. 36 By searching for the tau phosphorylation site, Jakes et al. developed an antibody that recognized tau, but which also recognized another protein that turned out to be synuclein. 37 In vitro studies demonstrated protein–protein interactions between Aβ42 and α‐synuclein, and also enhancement of each other's aggregation. 37 All these elements confirm that there are very strong links among Aβ42, α‐synuclein, and tau, which could explain the aggravating nature of amyloidopathy in the context of Pro‐DLB. These links between proteins probably explain why the association of several proteinopathies seems to be more the rule than single proteinopathies in neurodegenerative diseases. 35 38 Amyloid status aggravated various cognitive aspects of Pro‐DLB. The first was memory, whether verbal or visual, and particularly the total recall of RL/RI‐16, which suggests a progression to storage deficit, a frequent impairment in AD. This raises the question of whether early detection of a decline in memory storage in Pro‐DLB could ultimately lead to patients developing a dual disease (i.e., AD and DLB). 23 Pro‐DLB+AD patients also had an accelerated decline with Aβ+ status compared to Aβ–, both for global aspects (MMSE and CDR‐SoB) and more specific ones such as RL/RI‐16 and TMT‐B. It is surprising that these patients should have a negative amyloid status, given their dual pathology, which in both cases reduces Aβ42. However, in a previous study in which we identified few Pro‐DLB+AD patients, the majority had normal Aβ42 levels. 5 6 In the NS group, the effect of Aβ+ status was found in the verbal memory, executive function, and semantic fluency tests. There was therefore a role for amyloidopathy in cognitive decline in MCI and SCI patients without obvious signs of DLB or AD. The role of amyloidopathy alone in cognitive decline of asymptomatic subjects remains unclear. Patients in the NS group were therefore likely to have other neuropathological lesions, such as TDP‐43 (i.e., a disease called limbic predominant age‐related TDP‐43 encephalopathy [LATE]). 39 40 It has previously been shown that Aβ+ status leads to cognitive deterioration in MCI patients, particularly the elderly. Our study supports this finding: whatever the clinical diagnosis, there is a greater decline in CDR‐SoB. The CDR‐SoB is a hybrid test that enables both functional and cognitive aspects to be assessed and is widely used in clinical trials for AD. It is worth noting that it is with this type of test that the efficacy of antiamyloid antibodies has been demonstrated recently. 41 , 42 43 We found little impact of amyloid status on the evolution of focal brain volumetry or metabolism, apart from the decreased volume in the left insula in the Pro‐AD group Aβ+. This is consistent with the fact that the decreased gray matter in AD is strongly related to tauopathy and less to amyloidopathy. 44 This study has several strengths, including the number of 342 individuals, followed for 5 years. Cognitive performance was measured through different domains, during scheduled visits. We also have data on brain MRI biomarkers. However, we acknowledge some weaknesses. First, we did not have any autopsy information nor systematic specific biomarker for DLB, such as FP‐CIT dopaminergic imaging, or real‐time quaking‐induced conversion α‐synuclein, 45 so we cannot exclude classification bias. However, we used McKeith's criteria, which have excellent specificity for dementia (> 95%) 46 , 47 compared to the gold standard neuropathological diagnosis. The questionnaire used for RBD might be too sensitive; however, the high proportion of RBD in our pro‐DLB cohort is quite consistent with previous studies. 48 , 49 50 In this study, we have shown that patients in the very early stages of DLB in the presence of an amyloid biomarker have an acceleration of their global and focal cognitive decline, such as their memory and executive function, but also of their functional abilities. However, there was no impact on the acceleration of cerebral atrophy in this group. The next step will be to study the evolution of functional MRI, according to amyloid status, to better understand the functional and non‐neurodegenerative aspects of DLB at its onset.

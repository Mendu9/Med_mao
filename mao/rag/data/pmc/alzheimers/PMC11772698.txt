1 BACKGROUND Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a progressive brain disorder characterized by the accumulation of amyloid beta (Aβ) and tau protein that lead to neuronal loss and resulting in brain atrophy and cognitive decline. , 1 AD is characterized by a gradual decline in cognitive functions, particularly memory, thinking, and reasoning skills, which eventually interferes with daily activities and independence. 2 , 3 As the most common form of dementia that accounts for 60% to 80% of dementia cases globally, 4 , 5 AD affects millions of people and poses a significant burden on health‐care systems worldwide. The World Health Organization reported > 55 million people living with dementia in the year of 2023. 6 , 3 With the global population aging, the prevalence of AD dementia is expected to rise dramatically, making it a major public health concern. 7 8 AD clinical progression is complex and multi‐fractional, involving an interplay of genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors. The most common progression pathway involves a gradual transition from normal cognition (NC) to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and finally to AD dementia (NC–MCI–AD). In addition to the common NC–MCI–AD disease progression, a less common progression pattern has been observed in which some individuals may experience a rapid cognitive decline, transitioning quickly from NC to AD dementia. This phenomenon, which we refer to as NC–AD, has received limited attention in AD research. Recently, van Loenhoud et al. reported that 6.3% of the cognitively normal participants converted directly to AD dementia without the MCI stage for patients with an average follow‐up of 2 years by using data from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI). 3 In another study, by Liampas et al., 9 they examined the evolution of cognitive performance in individuals who quickly transitioned from NC to AD dementia (NC–AD) using data from the Hellenic Longitudinal Investigation of Aging and Diet (HELIAD) cohort. Their findings suggest that individuals who progressed rapidly to AD dementia exhibited accelerated decline in multiple cognitive domains, particularly in memory and executive functions. 10 RESEARCH IN CONTEXT Systematic review : The authors used PubMed to identify the research comparing the normal cognition (NC) to Alzheimer's disease (AD) group (NC–AD) and the NC–mild cognitive impairment (MCI)–AD group. The relevant publications are referenced in this article. Interpretation : We found that the NC–MCI–AD group showed greater impairment in cognitive and functional scores at AD dementia diagnosis, and the NC–AD group had rapid functional decline once patients were confirmed with AD onset. Future directions : Different neurocognitive domains were predictive for conversion to dementia from NC. We would like to investigate blood biomarker changes associated with different disease progression patterns to AD dementia from NC. Comparing the differences between the NC–MCI–AD group and the NC–AD group is crucial for understanding the heterogeneity of AD progression and potentially identifying distinct risk factors or biological mechanisms that influence the rate and pattern of cognitive decline. Aging is one of the most significant risk factors, with the incidence of AD dementia doubling approximately every 5 years for people age ≥ 65. Genetic factors, such as the presence of the apolipoprotein E ( 11 APOE ) ε4 allele, have been strongly associated with an increased risk of AD dementia. Other contributing risk factors include cardiovascular health, diabetes, obesity, depression, and smoking. 12 Understanding these differences could explain why some individuals experience gradual cognitive decline while others deteriorate rapidly. 13 Despite the recognition of these different progression patterns, few studies have directly compared the characteristics and trajectories of NC individuals who converted to AD dementia through MCI or not. To address this gap, our study aims to comprehensively compare the NC–AD group and the NC–MCI–AD group using data from the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC). The NACC database provides a unique opportunity to examine these distinct progression patterns in detail with its extensive longitudinal data on a large cohort of individuals. By leveraging this rich dataset, we can explore a wide range of factors that might differentiate the NC–AD group and the NC–MCI–AD group, including demographic characteristics, clinical profiles, and neuropathological information. Our goal is to provide a detailed characterization of these distinct progression patterns, enhancing the understanding of AD disease progression. The structure of this paper is as follows. In section 2 , we provide a detailed description of our methodology, starting with an introduction to the NACC database and the specific data used in this section. After this, section 2.3 outlines the specific variables of interest and section 2.4 describes the statistical analyses used to compare the NC–AD group and the NC–MCI–AD group across various domains. In section 3 , we present the results of comparative analyses, highlighting key findings and differences between the two groups and their trajectories over time. Finally, in section 4, we discuss the implications of our findings. We conclude by addressing the limitations of our study and suggesting directions for future research. 2 METHODS 2.1 NACC database This study used data from the NACC: a comprehensive longitudinal clinical and neuropathological database with data collected from Alzheimer's Disease Research Centers (ADRCs). , 14 For this study, we specifically used data obtained from the NACC on May 31, 2024. The database consists of three versions, with participants enrolled between 2005 and 2015 in the first two versions, and those enrolled after March 2015 in the third version. Our primary data source was from the Uniform Data Set (UDS), which comprises neurocognitive and clinical phenotypic data collected from participants in the ADRC programs. 15 , 16 , 17 The UDS uses a prospective, standardized, and longitudinal approach to evaluate participants with varying cognitive status, ranging from NC to MCI to AD dementia. Our other data source was the Researcher's Data Dictionary—Genetic Data (RDD‐Gen), which contains information on 18 APOE genotype. During each participant's annual visit, clinicians completed forms covering a wide range of topics, including participant demographics, family history, medical history, medications, physical and neurological examinations, neuropsychological testing, clinical diagnosis, neuropsychiatric symptoms, and functional status. These data were collected through direct observation and/or examination of participants and their co‐participants during in‐person office visits, home visits, and video/telephone calls. In addition to the annual assessments, the UDS included milestone forms to document significant changes in participants’ lives, such as changes in residence, living situation, death, or discontinuation of participation. 2.2 The NC–AD group and the NC–MCI–AD group The initial NACC dataset consisted of 50,962 participants. We included individuals who had at least one follow‐up visit after their initial assessment and excluded those who had dementia on their first visit, resulting in a sample of 24,371 participants. Among these, 15,250 had NC at baseline. From this group with baseline NC, 1254 participants progressed to dementia at follow‐up visits. We focused on two specific cohorts of interest: those who progressed directly from the NC to AD group and those who transitioned from NC to MCI before developing AD dementia (NC–MCI–AD group). The selection of these cohorts was based on the participants’ cognitive status at each visit, as recorded in the Clinician Diagnosis Form of the UDS. The UDS categorizes cognitive status into four possible categories: NC, cognitively impaired but not MCI, amnestic or non‐amnestic MCI, and dementia. Additionally, the primary etiologic diagnosis, also documented in the UDS, was used to ensure that the dementia cases were specifically attributed to AD in the selected participants. To refine the selection of these cohorts and ensure reliable analysis of progression patterns, we applied additional exclusion criteria to the initial group of 1254 participants in a sequential manner. First, we excluded 251 participants who were categorized as “cognitively impaired but not MCI” at any visit in their progression. We then excluded 254 participants for whom AD dementia was not consistently the primary etiologic diagnosis after their first dementia diagnosis. Finally, from the remaining participants, we excluded 207 with multiple conversions or reversions, such as cases in which a participant transitioned from NC to AD dementia and then reverted to a less severe cognitive status (e.g., NC–AD–NC–AD) or showed inconsistent progression patterns (e.g., NC–MCI–MCI–AD–MCI–AD). After applying these exclusion criteria, our final analytical sample comprised 542 participants: 208 in the NC–AD group and 334 in the NC–MCI–AD group. For both cohorts, participants had to maintain their AD dementia status in all visits after their first AD dementia diagnosis, with AD consistently being the primary etiology. 2.3 Data collection and measures Demographic data (e.g., age, sex, race, living situation) were obtained from the subject demographics form, and the family history was derived from the subject family history form. To account for premorbid intelligence and cognitive reserve, we included years of education and WAIS‐R (Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale‐Revised) score in our analysis. The number of APOE ε4 alleles was sourced from the RDD‐Gen. Body mass index (BMI) was collected from the physical form. Selected comorbidities were extracted from the subject health history form, including diabetes, cardiovascular disease, hypertension (HTN), hypercholesterolemia, thyroid disease, and depression. These conditions were chosen based on their prevalence in older adults and their potential associations with cognitive function and dementia risk. Cognitive and functional assessments were obtained from their respective forms. These assessments included the Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB) and its sub‐scores, Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), Neuropsychiatric Inventory Questionnaire (NPI‐Q), and Functional Activities Questionnaire (FAQ). The CDR‐SB is a global measure of dementia severity that assesses cognitive and functional performance in six domains (CDR sub‐scores): memory, orientation, judgment and problem solving, community affairs, home and hobbies, and personal care. Each domain is rated on a scale from 0 (no impairment) to 3 (severe impairment), and the CDR‐SB is calculated by summing the scores from each domain, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 18. The MMSE is a widely used screening tool that evaluates various aspects of cognitive function, ranging from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating better cognitive function. 19 The NPI‐Q assesses the presence and severity of neuropsychiatric symptoms commonly observed in dementia patients. It covers 12 behavioral domains and provides a total score reflecting the overall burden of neuropsychiatric symptoms. 20 The FAQ measures an individual's ability to perform instrumental activities of daily living. It consists of 10 items, each scored from 0 (independent) to 3 (dependent), with a total score ranging from 0 to 30. Higher scores indicate greater functional impairment. 21 22 2.4 Statistical analysis Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the study population. Median and (2.5th, 97.5th) percentile were reported for continuous variables, and frequency and percentage were calculated for categorical variables. To compare the demographic and clinical characteristics between the NC–AD group and the NC–MCI–AD group, the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was performed for continuous variables. The chi‐squared test was used for categorical variables. When the value of cell counts was too small (e.g., five or less), Fisher exact test was used to provide exact p values. 23 For paired data (e.g., two visits from the NC to AD group), Wilcoxon signed‐rank test and the McNemar test were used for continuous outcomes and categorical outcomes. To examine changes in health characteristics within participants as they progressed from NC to AD dementia, we used the McNemar test for paired data. We compared outcomes from the first NC visit to outcomes from the first AD dementia visit. This analysis evaluated changes in the prevalence of health characteristics, such as obesity (defined as a BMI ≥ 30), diabetes, cardiovascular disease, HTN, hypercholesterolemia, thyroid disease, and depression, within both the NC–AD and NC–MCI–AD groups. 24 To evaluate the rate of change in these scores, we calculated average yearly changes in these scores during the NC stage and the AD dementia stage, respectively. These yearly changes were computed by dividing the difference in scores between consecutive visits by the time interval between visits (in years), and then averaging these values for each participant during the relevant period (NC or AD dementia). For these yearly changes, median and (2.5th, 97.5th) percentile were presented. The yearly changes were compared between the groups using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. To examine the potential influence of obesity on cognitive decline, we also stratified our yearly change analysis by obesity status and presence of HTN. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1. A significance level of 0.05 was considered for all tests to determine statistical significance. 25 We chose to use median measures and non‐parametric Wilcoxon tests instead of mean‐based comparisons (e.g., t tests) due to the distributional properties of the data. Prior to selecting our statistical methods, we tested the normality of our continuous variables using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of Q–Q plots. Most of our variables, including age, years of education, and cognitive and functional assessment scores, showed significant deviations from normality ( p < 0.05 in Shapiro–Wilk tests). Given these results, along with the presence of outliers, the Wilcoxon tests were more appropriate as they do not assume normality and are robust against outliers, allowing for a more accurate comparison of central tendencies in skewed or non‐normally distributed data. These methods were selected to ensure reliable and valid comparisons between the groups. 3 RESULTS Participants in this study were drawn from the NACC database, with initial visits occurring between 2005 and 2022. The analytical sample consisted of 542 participants, with 208 in the NC–AD group and 334 in the NC–MCI–AD group. The timing of follow‐up assessments varied, with most participants being evaluated approximately once per year. The total follow‐up duration ranged from 11 months to 18 years, with a mean follow‐up period of 8 years. On average, participants had 7 follow‐up visits, with some having up to 18 visits over the study period. To better understand the differences between individuals who progress directly from NC to AD dementia and those who transition through the MCI stage, we first examined the duration of each cognitive status stage in both groups. We used the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test to compare the durations between the groups. Individuals in the NC–AD group spent a significantly longer time in the normal cognitive state compared to those in the NC–MCI–AD group (mean 4.4 vs. 3.3 years, median 3.2 vs. 2.1 years, p = 0.013). However, the overall duration before reaching the AD dementia stage did not differ significantly between the two groups ( p = 0.114), despite a descriptive difference in means (NC–AD: 4.4 years; NC–MCI–AD: 4.6 years) and medians (NC–AD: 3.2 years; NC–MCI–AD: 4.1 years). Once reaching the AD dementia stage, the NC–MCI–AD group showed a significantly longer duration compared to the NC‐AD group (mean 1.8 vs. 1.5 years, median 1.1 vs. 0.9 years, p = 0.029). We then compared the demographic and genetic characteristics, health‐related factors, and cognitive and functional assessments between the two groups. The baseline characteristics of participants in the NC–AD group ( n = 208) and the NC–MCI–AD group ( n = 334) are summarized in Table 1 . Demographic characteristics were largely similar between the two groups. Female participants predominated in both groups, comprising 68.3% in the NC–AD group and 70.4% in the NC–MCI–AD group. The median age was identical in both groups at 78 years, with comparable interquartile ranges (IQRs): 73 to 84 years for NC–AD and 72 to 84 years for NC–MCI–AD. Educational attainment was also similar, with both groups having a median of 16 years of education. WAIS‐R scores, which provide an indication of cognitive ability and premorbid intelligence, were identical between the groups, with both showing a median score of 43 (IQR: 31–54 for NC–AD and 36–51 for NC–MCI–AD, p = 0.648). A significant difference was observed in the racial composition of the two groups ( p = 0.040). We also provided the exact p value for the post hoc test. The NC–MCI–AD group had a higher proportion of White participants (88.9% vs. 84.1%) and a lower proportion of Black participants (7.2% vs. 13.5%) compared to the NC–AD group. The marital status, living situation, level of independence, and type of residence were similar between the groups ( 26 p > 0.05). In both groups, many participants were married (52.7% in NC–AD and 57.3% in NC–MCI–AD) and lived with a spouse or partner (58.7% vs. 64.1%). Most individuals were able to live independently (94.2% vs. 94.3%) and the vast majority resided in private residences (99.5% vs. 97.6%). A family history of cognitive impairment was prevalent in both groups, affecting 65.9% of the NC–AD group and 68.7% of the NC–MCI–AD group. The distribution of APOE ε4 alleles did not differ significantly between the groups. In the NC–AD group, 33.3% of participants had one copy, and 7.8% had two copies, compared to 39.7% and 5.7%, respectively, in the NC–MCI–AD group. Significant differences were observed in cognitive and functional assessments at AD dementia onset, contrasting with the subtle differences observed during NC. CDR‐SB scores were significantly higher in the NC‐ MCI‐AD group compared to the NC‐AD group (4.0 vs. 3.0, p = 0.002), indicating greater overall cognitive and functional impairment. For the CDR sub‐scores, significant differences were found in all domains except personal care. The NC–MCI–AD group showed higher impairment in memory ( p < 0.001), orientation ( p = 0.006), judgment and problem solving ( p = 0.023), community affairs ( p = 0.036), and home and hobbies ( p = 0.026). These findings suggested that the NC–MCI–AD group exhibited more severe global cognitive and functional impairment at the time of AD dementia diagnosis. Interestingly, MMSE scores were significantly lower in the NC–MCI–AD group compared to the NC–AD group (24.0 vs. 25.0, p = 0.015), which contrasts with the pattern observed during NC, during which the NC–MCI–AD group maintained better MMSE scores. Also, the magnitude of decline in MMSE scores from NC to AD dementia onset appears to be greater in the NC–MCI–AD group (from 29.0 to 24.0) compared to the NC–AD group (from 28.0 to 25.0). This suggested that while the NC–MCI–AD group initially had better cognitive function, they experienced a more rapid decline by the time of AD dementia onset. NPI‐Q and FAQ scores both showed significant differences between the groups at AD dementia onset, with the NC–MCI–AD group demonstrating greater impairment in both domains. The median NPI‐Q score was significantly higher in the NC–MCI–AD group compared to the NC–AD group (3.0 vs. 2.0, p = 0.006), indicating more severe neuropsychiatric symptoms. Similarly, FAQ scores were also significantly higher in the NC–MCI–AD group compared to the NC–AD group (13.0 vs. 8.0, p < 0.001), suggesting greater functional impairment in daily activities. Within‐participant analyses from the first normal visit (Table 2 ) to the first AD visit (Table 3 ) revealed similar patterns of change in health characteristics for both groups. In the NC–AD group, significant increases from NC to AD dementia onset were observed for HTN ( p = 0.003), hypercholesterolemia ( p = 0.008), and depression ( p < 0.001). Similarly, the NC–MCI–AD group showed significant increases in HTN ( p < 0.001), hypercholesterolemia ( p < 0.001), and depression ( p < 0.001). The NC–MCI–AD group also demonstrated a significant increase in cardiovascular disease ( p < 0.001). We further examined the progression patterns of cognitive and functional changes across disease progression using visual representations. As shown in Figure 1 , we used violin plots to compare the distribution of cognitive and functional outcomes over the course of disease progression between the NC–AD group and the NC–MCI–AD group. As disease progressed from NC to AD dementia, the outcome measures got worse in each group. The outcome change at the first AD dementia visit from the last NC visit was substantially larger than the change between the two NC time points. For MMSE and NPI‐Q, the two groups had similar distributions at each time point. On the first AD dementia visit, the NC–MCI–AD group demonstrated more severe impairment in CDR‐SB and FAQ scores than the NC–AD group. Table 4 summarizes the median yearly changes in cognitive and functional assessment scores for the NC–AD group and the NC–MCI–AD group during both NC and AD dementia stages, providing a longitudinal perspective. It is important to note that for CDR‐SB, CDR sub‐scores, NPI‐Q, and FAQ, higher scores indicate greater impairment, while for MMSE, lower scores represent cognitive decline. The CDR‐SB scores showed no significant difference during the NC stage, with both groups demonstrating a median annual change of 0 ( p = 0.261). In the AD dementia stage, both groups exhibited substantial and similar annual increases (NC–AD: 1.451, NC–MCI–AD: 1.399, p = 0.572), despite the NC–MCI–AD group showing significantly higher CDR‐SB scores at AD dementia onset in Table 3 . We also examined the CDR sub‐scores to assess potential differences in specific cognitive domains, although no significant differences were found between groups during either stage. MMSE scores during NC showed no significant difference in median annual change between groups ( p = 0.335), with both groups demonstrating a median change of 0. However, in the dementia stage, both groups showed more pronounced declines. The NC–MCI–AD group demonstrated a larger median annual decrease (–2.002) compared to the NC–AD group (–1.795), though this difference was not statistically significant ( p = 0.443). While this trend suggests the possibility of a more rapid cognitive decline in the NC–MCI–AD group during the dementia stage, further research with larger sample sizes is needed to confirm this observation. The NPI‐Q and FAQ scores showed varying patterns across the NC and dementia stages. During the period of NC, both groups demonstrated a median annual change of 0 for both measures, with no statistically significant differences observed. It was noteworthy, however, that the NC–MCI–AD group displayed some variability in NPI‐Q scores compared to the NC–AD group. In the dementia stage, both groups continued to show a median annual change of 0 for NPI‐Q scores ( p = 0.460), but exhibited substantially wider quartile ranges, indicating considerable heterogeneity in individual trajectories. For FAQ scores in the dementia stage, both groups revealed more substantial changes, with the NC–AD group showing a slightly larger median annual increase (2.994) compared to the NC–MCI–AD group (2.653), although this difference did not reach statistical significance. Given the observed differences in vascular risk factors and BMI distribution between the NC–AD and NC–MCI–AD groups, and their potential role in cognitive decline suggested by previous literature, we stratified our yearly change analysis by obesity status (Table 5 ), and HTN (Table 6 ), to determine whether these factors were associated with varying rates of cognitive decline or functional changes in both the NC–AD and NC–MCI–AD groups. During the NC stage, no statistically significant differences were observed for obesity or HTN in either group across all cognitive and functional measures (all p values > 0.05). However, during the dementia stage, some noteworthy differences emerged. In the obesity analysis, while not statistically significant, non‐obese individuals in the NC–AD group showed slightly larger increases in CDR‐SB scores and FAQ scores compared to obese individuals. Conversely, in the NC–MCI–AD group, obese individuals showed a numerically larger increase in CDR‐SB scores and a larger decrease in MMSE scores. The HTN analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in either group during the dementia stage. These findings suggest that the influence of obesity and HTN on cognitive and functional decline may be complex and potentially differ between the NC–AD and NC–MCI–AD groups, particularly during the dementia stage. Also, the overall lack of consistent statistically significant patterns across these risk factors indicates that larger studies may be needed to confirm these findings. John Wiley & Sons, Ltd. 4 DISCUSSION In this study, we aimed to compare the characteristics and trajectories of individuals who progressed directly from NC to AD and those who transitioned through the MCI stage. We examined health‐related factors and cognitive and functional assessments between these two groups at various time points, including the first and last visits with NC, and the first visit of AD dementia diagnosis. Our findings revealed several notable patterns and differences between the NC–AD group and the NC–MCI–AD group in their cognitive trajectories, functional changes, and health profiles. One of the key findings was the complex pattern of cognitive and functional performance patterns observed in the NC–AD group and the NC–MCI–AD group across different stages of disease progression. While both groups showed minimal changes during NC, the NC–MCI–AD group demonstrated significantly greater impairment at AD onset across multiple measures. The analysis of median yearly changes further illuminated these progression patterns. During the NC stage, both groups showed minimal changes in most measures. In the dementia stage, both groups exhibited more substantial changes, with differences in patterns that, while not statistically significant, could suggest different trajectories. The NC–MCI–AD group showed a larger median annual decrease in MMSE scores, potentially indicating faster cognitive decline, while the NC–AD group exhibited slightly larger annual increases in FAQ scores, which might reflect more rapid functional decline. These observations suggest a potentially complex, non‐linear pattern of decline that warrants further investigation with larger sample sizes. We also observed differences in BMI distribution between the two groups across different time points. While the differences in BMI distribution showed varying levels of statistical significance throughout the study period, the consistent trend of higher proportions of overweight and obese individuals in the NC–AD group suggests a potential association between higher BMI and a more direct progression from NC to AD dementia without an intervening MCI stage, aligning with previous studies linking obesity to increased AD risk. , 27 However, our additional analysis of yearly changes stratified by obesity status did not reveal statistically significant differences between obese and non‐obese individuals in either the NC–AD or NC–MCI–AD groups. Nevertheless, the observed numerical differences warrant further investigation in larger cohorts. Our study has several strengths, including the use of a large, well‐characterized cohort from the NACC database, which allowed for a comprehensive comparison of the NC–AD group and the NC–MCI–AD group across multiple domains and time points. Moreover, the longitudinal nature of the data enabled us to track changes over time, offering a detailed understanding of the dynamics of disease progression that cross‐sectional studies cannot capture. Nevertheless, our study has limitations that should be acknowledged. 28 One limitation was the constrained availability of biomarkers and imaging data within our cohort, including cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) markers and neuroimaging. CSF biomarker data were available for only a subset of participants, which precluded any meaningful comparison of Aβ, phosphorylated tau (p‐tau), or total tau (t‐tau) levels between the groups. Similarly, we were unable to incorporate magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or positron emission tomography (PET) data in our analysis, due to the relatively recent initiation of the Standardized Centralized Alzheimer's and Related Dementias Neuroimaging (SCAN) project, which was funded in May 2020. This limitation highlights the need for future research with access to comprehensive biomarkers and neuroimaging data to offer valuable insights into the underlying pathological processes and potentially identify neurobiological markers that distinguish these distinct progression patterns. While our study examined several medical conditions, NACC did not capture all potential physical illnesses that could impact cognitive decline. In particular, information on episodes of delirium or other acute medical events, which could accelerate cognitive decline, was not available. Future studies should aim to incorporate more detailed medical history data, including information on a wider range of physical illnesses and acute medical events, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of factors influencing AD progression patterns. Additionally, there are important considerations regarding the generalizability and interpretation of our findings. The NACC dataset is not a random or statistically representative sample of the US population, as it consists primarily of participants recruited from ADRCs across the United States, which tend to overrepresent educated, White, and female individuals. This highlights the need for more diverse, community‐based studies to ensure findings are representative of the broader population. It is also essential to increase participant diversity in AD research. NACC data do not adequately capture the potential influence of socioeconomic, cultural, environmental, and health‐care access factors on AD progression patterns. These factors may impact how AD manifests and progresses in different populations. Future research should prioritize the inclusion of underrepresented groups, such as racial and ethnic minorities, individuals from lower socioeconomic backgrounds, and those from rural or underserved areas, to identify disparities in disease mechanisms and outcomes, and to develop equitable diagnostic criteria and treatment protocols. Our study focused on these two specific AD progression patterns, but we acknowledge the importance of mixed and non‐AD pathologies in cognitive decline. For the final 542 patients with AD as the primary pathology, there were 213 patients with other contributing pathology. The mixed pathology group had slightly faster declines in CDR‐SB than the AD pathology for patients in the NC‐AD group once these patients were diagnosed with AD dementia. Our exclusion of participants with multiple transitions was necessary for clear comparison between these two progression patterns, but it limits our ability to capture the full complexity of dementia progression. Future research could benefit from alternative methodological approaches, such as sequence analysis or growth mixture modeling, to better account for the heterogeneity in diagnostic transitions and represent a broader spectrum of patients. Sequence analysis could help identify and characterize a wider variety of diagnostic transition patterns, including those with multiple transitions or reversions that were excluded from our current study, while growth mixture modeling could uncover latent classes of cognitive decline trajectories, including those with mixed or non‐AD pathologies. These methods would provide a more comprehensive understanding of cognitive decline trajectories, potentially revealing subpopulations that may benefit from targeted interventions. Changes in the standardized diagnostic criteria across ADRCs over time may also impact our findings. For example, the criteria for dementia changed across different versions of the UDS. In versions 1.2 and 2, specific diagnostic criteria were not provided for assessing the presence of dementia, although most centers were likely to use Diagnostic and Statistical Manual for Mental Disorders Fourth Edition criteria or similar. However, in version 3, explicit diagnostic criteria for dementia were provided. This evolution could potentially influence the observed progression patterns in our study, particularly for participants whose data spans multiple UDS versions. Moreover, even with standardized criteria, there is potential for some variability in how these criteria are applied across different sites and clinicians, especially in the absence of biomarker data. This limitation emphasizes the need for careful consideration of UDS version effects and potential site‐specific variations in multicenter studies of AD progression. Moreover, while our current study focused on between‐group comparisons of health‐related factors and cognitive/functional assessment scores at specific time points (e.g., the first and last visits with NC and the first visit of AD dementia diagnosis), we recognize the importance of examining within‐participant changes over time. In future research, we plan to use multilevel modeling techniques, which will allow us to leverage all available data points and better manage missing data using appropriate algorithms, rather than relying solely on full‐case analyses. Multilevel modeling will enable the simultaneous modeling of within‐ and between‐person changes, providing insights into individual variability and the impact of various covariates, such as vascular risk factors and obesity, on disease trajectories. This approach also allows us to model discontinuous change trajectories, such as different slopes for cognitive decline before and after dementia onset, thereby allowing us to better understand the trajectory of cognitive and functional decline. When multiple states (e.g., NC, MCI, AD) are the outcome of interest in the statistical analysis model, we will use the competing risks multistate models to analyze data. In this cohort, we have at least four possible states: NC, MCI, AD dementia, and death. The multistate model is able to calculate the probability of transiting between states as disease progresses over time. The event of death will be considered competing state with no reversion. We will investigate the possible reversion from dementia to MCI state. We may include other types of dementia in this future work if the sample size is sufficient, as well as add another state of subjective memory concern. The identified possible risk factors (e.g., obesity and HTN) from this article will be considered covariates. Despite these limitations, our findings have significant implications for both research and clinical practice. The identification of distinct progression patterns from NC to AD dementia suggests the need for more personalized approaches to diagnosis and treatment. Recognizing that patients may progress through different pathways—such as directly to AD dementia or through an intermediate MCI stage—can help tailor monitoring and treatment strategies to the individual, potentially improving patient care. Although the underlying mechanisms driving these different progression patterns remain unclear, our findings suggest that factors such as metabolic or vascular health, genetic predispositions, and lifestyle factors may play a role. Continued research in these areas is needed to uncover these contributors and develop targeted interventions that could slow or modify the course of the disease.

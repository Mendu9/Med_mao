1 INTRODUCTION The establishment of reperfusion therapies, including intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) and endovascular therapy (EVT), has dramatically improved the prognosis of ischemic stroke [ 1 , 2 ]. Furthermore, advances in imaging technology, such as perfusion images on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT) with automated analysis software [ 3 ], have enabled evaluation of the ischemic penumbra, salvageable brain tissue affected by hypoperfusion, greatly expanding the therapeutic window, and heralding a shift from a time‐based to tissue‐based era [ 4 ]. Nevertheless, such technology is limited to specialized facilities, often because of issues including software copyright. Hence, the development of a widely accessible, cost‐effective, and minimally invasive biomarker for the prediction of the penumbra is of urgent need. Several studies have reported multiple blood biomarkers for the diagnosis, or severity evaluation, of ischemic stroke in humans [ 5 ]; however, none have targeted the penumbra. Many biomarkers reported in stroke have so far been in intracellular components reflecting the destruction of brain tissue [ 6 , 7 , 8 ]. However, secretory peptides reacting specifically to brain ischemia could serve as accurate markers in the evaluation of the penumbra. A previous experimental study in mice showed several genes expressed in response to cerebral hypoperfusion because of bilateral common carotid artery stenosis [ 9 ], which recapitulated low perfusion within the penumbra [ 10 , 11 ]. Adrenomedullin (ADM) was the second‐most highly upregulated among >44,000 genes and the highest among those coding secretary proteins, emphasizing ADM as an optimal biomarker for the penumbra. ADM is a 52‐amino acid peptide that participates in various physiological functions such as vasodilation, hormone regulation, and angiogenesis [ 12 , 13 ]. Plasma ADM levels increase in acute ischemic stroke [ 14 ], probably because of a defensive or compensatory mechanism against brain ischemia. The neuroprotective properties of ADM have been demonstrated in several rodent models [ 15 , 16 , 17 ]. ADM mRNA is induced as early as 1 h after occlusion of the middle cerebral artery (MCA) in the rat cortex, which precedes obvious progression of infarction [ 18 , 19 ], further supporting the notion that ADM could act as a biomarker for the penumbra. Although reliable measurement of circulating ADM is difficult because of its short half‐life [ 20 ], mid‐regional pro‐ADM (MR‐proADM), a stable peptide fragment of ADM precursor, can be accurately measured as a surrogate marker for ADM [ 21 ]. However, previous reports using blood samples, obtained within 24 h [ 22 ] or 7 days [ 23 ] after the onset of acute ischemic stroke, have shown a correlation between plasma MR‐proADM elevation and unfavorable outcomes and worsening symptoms. In the current Determination of Early Predictor of Ischemic Stroke: Adrenomedullin (DEPRISA) study, we therefore evaluated the role of plasma MR‐proADM in the prediction of the ischemic penumbra in hyperacute ischemic stroke (HAIS) patients. 2 METHODS 2.1 Study population We prospectively included consecutive patients with HAIS who presented to the National Cerebral and Cardiovascular Center (NCVC), and Shiga University of Medical Science within 4.5 h from the last known well (LKW), or with onset between July 2017 and March 2019. We excluded patients without blood examination for plasma MR‐proADM on admission, and those with acute infection, inflammatory disease and end‐stage kidney disease, defined as estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <15 ml/min/1.73 m 2 [ 24 ]. All subjects provided written informed consent and the study was approved by the NCVC research ethics committee (approval number: M28‐075‐3, R21013 ). We also included 1298 participants with the evaluation for plasma MR‐proADM levels as healthy control, who were enrolled in the second survey of the Japan Multi‐Institutional Collaborative Cohort (J‐MICC) Study in the Kyoto area from 2013 to 2017 [ 25 ]. All participants provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the institutional Ethics Committee of Kyoto Prefectural University of Medicine (approval number: ERB‐E‐36‐8). 2.2 Clinical assessment The following clinical information was obtained in HAIS patients: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), which was calculated as weight (kg) divided by the square of the height in meters (m 2 ), systolic and diastolic blood pressure, smoking, drinking, past medical history, including hypertension (systolic/diastolic blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg during hospitalization, and/or use of antihypertensive drugs), diabetes (glycated hemoglobin level ≥6.5% and blood sugar ≥ 126 mg/dl, and/or use of hypoglycemic agents or insulin), dyslipidemia (low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥140 mg/dl and/or high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol <40 mg/dl and/or use of antihyperlipidemic drugs), atrial fibrillation (AF), old cerebral infarction and hemorrhage, and ischemic heart disease, LKW/onset to door time (ODT), blood collection to image time, National Institute of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), and presence of reperfusion therapy including IVT and EVT. Subtypes of stroke were classified according to TOAST criteria [ 26 ]. Large vessel occlusion was defined as occlusion of extra and intracranial internal carotid artery (ICA), M1–2 segments of the MCA, A2‐segment of the anterior cerebral artery (ACA), P1–2 segment of the posterior cerebral artery (PCA), and basilar artery. GFR‐estimating equations for Japanese were used in the calculation of eGFR using blood samples on admission [ 27 ]. In healthy controls, the following medical information was analyzed from self‐administered questionaries in the J‐MICC study: age, sex, BMI, smoking, drinking, past medical history including hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, old cerebral infarction and hemorrhage, and ischemic heart disease. We calculated eGFR in a similar manner to HAIS patients. 2.3 Measurement of plasma MR‐proADM levels and routine blood examination In HAIS, peripheral blood was collected from all patients immediately after admission. Blood samples were also used for routine blood examination tests such as serum chemistry and glucose metabolism. Collected blood samples were dispensed to normal ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid tubes for measurement of MR‐proADM. Each sample was agitated and centrifuged at 3400 g for 6 min at 25°C immediately after collection. The plasma samples were collected and preserved at −80°C until measurement for MR‐proADM. Plasma MR‐proADM concentrations (nmol/L) were measured by an automated KRYPTOR analyzer, using a time‐resolved amplified cryptate emission technology assay (Thermo Fisher Diagnostics K.K., Japan) [ 21 ]. 2.4 Imaging analysis We performed brain MR imaging or CT perfusion analysis and angiography on admission in HAIS. MR imaging was performed with either 1.5 tesla (Magnetom Sonata; Siemens Medical Solutions, Erlangen, Germany, or SIGNA HDxt or Optima450W; GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, USA) or 3.0 tesla (Magnetom Verio or Spectra; Siemens Medical Solutions, Erlangen, Germany, or SIGNA HDxt or Discovery 750W, Milwaukee, USA) scanners. The sequence parameters of diffusion‐weighted image were as follows: slice thickness, 4.0 or 5.0 (1.5 tesla) or 5.0 (3.0 tesla) mm; repetition time/ echo time, 3000/72 or 5000/71 (1.5 tesla), 5000/82, 7300/83, or 8000/72 (3.0 tesla) ms; matrix, 128 × 128; field of view, 230 × 230 mm; b = 0, 1000/mm 3 . MR perfusion imaging was acquired by a 3.0 tesla scanner using a gradient‐echo echo‐planar imaging sequence after a bolus injection of 0.2 ml/kg of gadoterate meglumine (Magnescope, Guerbet, Tokyo, Japan) at a rate of 3 ml/s, followed by a 30 ml bolus of saline flush at the same rate. The parameters of MR perfusion imaging were as follows: slice thickness, 5.0 mm; repetition time/echo time, 2000/40 ms; matrix, 128 × 128; field of view, 240 × 240 mm; measurements 60. CT imaging was obtained using 320‐slice multidetector CT scanner (Aquilion ONE; Toshiba Medical Systems, Otawara, Japan) with 320 × 0.5 mm detectors, tube voltage setting of 80 kVp, and tube‐current settings of 120 mA. CT perfusion imaging was acquired using a bolus injection of 40 ml of iohexol (Iopaque, 300 mg/dl of iodine; Fuji Pharma, Tokyo, Japan) at a rate of 4 ml/s, followed by a 30 ml bolus of saline flush at the same rate. Ischemic core volume was calculated by an automated image postprocessing system, RAPID (iSchemaView Inc., Menlo Park, CA, USA), which was determined by both the apparent diffusion coefficient <620 × 10 −3 mm 2 /s on b0/b1000 image in MR imaging and cerebral blood flow <30%, in comparison to contralateral hemisphere on CT perfusion [ 4 , 28 ]. The volume of salvageable tissue was evaluated in patients with a clinical indication for reperfusion therapy, using the T max perfusion parameter of >6 s threshold on both MR perfusion imaging and CT perfusion, then analyzed by RAPID software [ 3 , 4 ]. 2.5 Evaluation of the ischemic penumbra The presence of the ischemic penumbra was estimated by two different models in HAIS patients: (1) clinical‐diffusion mismatch (CDM), which was defined as NIHSS ≥8 and diffusion ischemic core volume ≤25 ml, excluding lacunar infarction and brainstem stroke [ 29 ]; (2) MR angiography‐diffusion‐weighted imaging mismatch (MDM) as a purely imaging based criterion, which fulfills the following criteria: (a) NIHSS ≥5 and (b‐1) ICA/M1 occlusion with diffusion core volume ≤25 ml, or (b‐2) signal intensity decrease of ICA/M1 or occlusion of M2/ACA/PCA with diffusion core volume ≤15 ml based on the previous criteria [ 30 ]. Ischemic penumbra volume was calculated by subtracting the ischemic core volume from T max > 6 s volume and evaluated using RAPID software, where MR or CT perfusion imaging was available. 2.6 Case–control analysis In the case–control analysis, we compared plasma MR‐proADM levels between HAIS and healthy control. HAIS with LKW/ODT within 1 h was defined as time‐based HAIS, and tissue‐based HAIS, with the ischemic core volume of 0. We compared the MR‐proADM levels in each group with a healthy control. 2.7 Statistical analysis Continuous variables were presented as the median and interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables were recorded as absolute number and frequency counts. Variables were compared using Wilcoxon's test for continuous variables, and the two‐sided Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Spearman's rank‐order correlation analysis was used to investigate the correlation between the two nonparametric continuous variables. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to determine the most appropriate cut‐off values, which were calculated based on the Youden index. In the case–control analysis, multiple logistic regression analysis was performed, adjusting for the covariates with significant differences between the two groups. We dichotomized the MR‐proADM levels according to the cut‐off value. Multiple logistic regression analysis was also performed to investigate the relation between plasma MR‐proADM levels, and the presence of ischemic penumbra estimated by CDM or MDM after adjustment for age, sex and the covariates associated with MR‐proADM levels (hypertension, cardiogenic stroke, and eGFR), and odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals calculated. We dichotomized age and MR‐proADM levels, calculated using logistic regression analysis according to the median and cut‐off value, respectively. A p ‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R version 4.1.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). 3 RESULTS Among 866 patients with acute ischemic stroke, informed consent was obtained from 143 patients transferred to the hospital within 4.5 h of the onset. MR‐proADM levels were available in 128 on admission, except 15 with insufficient or poor‐quality blood samples. Among these patients, none had an acute infection or inflammatory disease, but three were excluded because of end‐stage kidney disease. Head MRI was not performed in six patients. Subsequently, 119 HAIS patients (median age, 77 years [IQR, 70–85]; male, 59.2%) (Figure 1 ) and 1298 controls (median age, 58 years [IQR, 49–67]; male, 33.2%) were included. In patients with HAIS, the median LKW/ODT was 69 min (IQR, 46–141), and the time from blood collection to imaging was 13 (IQR, 8 – 19) min. The median NIHSS on admission was 6 (IQR, 3–17). Cardioembolic stroke was determined in 50 (42.0%) patients. Large vessel occlusion was detected in 58 (48.7%), including 17 (14.3%) in ICA and 20 (16.8%) in M1. Plasma MR‐proADM levels ≥0.54 nmol/L were also significant predictor of both time‐ and tissue‐based HAIS after adjustment (odds ratio 8.62, p < 0.001, and 6.22, p < 0.001, respectively) (Figure S1 B,C). We also evaluated correlation between MR‐proADM levels and ischemic penumbra volume. MR or CT perfusion imaging was available in 7 of 125 patients with HAIS diagnosed by head MR or CT. Nevertheless, plasma MR‐proADM levels showed a significant positive correlation with the volume of the ischemic penumbra ( r s = 0.79, p = 0.036) (Figure S1 ). 4 DISCUSSION In the current DEPRISA study, we demonstrated higher plasma MR‐proADM levels in HAIS compared with healthy control, even after the baseline adjustment, in addition to both HAIS within 1 h of onset and HAIS without ischemic core. We also found that large vessel occlusion, but not ischemic core volume, was correlated with increased MR‐proADM levels. Importantly, elevated MR‐proADM levels were associated with the presence of both CDM and MDM, which was further confirmed by a significant correlation of the MR‐proADM levels with the RAPID‐evaluated penumbra volume. A large number of studies have identified various peptides or genes as potential biomarkers for the diagnosis of stroke; however, none have been applied in a clinical setting [ 5 , 31 ]. While these biomarkers may reflect brain tissue injury, thrombogenesis, or inflammation, it is more applicable to focus on biomarkers specific to cerebral hypoxia or hypoperfusion, the principal pathology of ischemic stroke, in the current context. In addition, from the perspective of ischemic stroke therapy, the most useful biomarker should reflect salvageable brain tissue, namely, the ischemic penumbra, instead of stroked‐out lesions. However, several studies have shown that ADM is a predictor of severity or long‐term outcomes [ 14 , 32 ], poor functional consequences [ 22 ], and worsening symptoms [ 23 ] in acute stroke patients using blood samples obtained within 24–168 h after onset, which is beyond the therapeutic time window and reperfusion therapy period [ 2 ]. Multiple biomarkers, including S100B, glial fibrillary acidic protein, neuron‐specific enolase, and matrix metalloproteinase‐9 were measured in HAIS within 3–6 h after the onset, which can differentiate ischemic stroke from cerebral hemorrhage and predict the outcomes including hemorrhagic complications after IVT [ 7 , 8 , 33 , 34 ]. Nevertheless, these intracellular proteins were positively correlated with infarct size [ 35 , 36 ], and elevated in hemorrhagic stroke compared with ischemic stroke [ 6 , 7 , 8 ], likely reflecting the destruction of brain tissue, and thus unlikely to be reliable biomarkers in the treatment of stroke. In the current study, we showed elevated MR‐proADM levels in HAIS within 4.5 h, even within 1 h after onset, reflecting the rapid time‐based reaction to the brain ischemia. Moreover, MR‐proADM levels increased in HAIS without ischemic core, which indicated a highly sensitive tissue‐based reaction to brain ischemia. These results were consistent with previous studies using the MCA occlusion rodent models, which showed early ADM mRNA upregulation in the cortex before obvious infarct development [ 18 , 19 ]. A prospective study showed that circulating ADM levels were significantly increased in acute cerebral hemorrhage, compared to healthy volunteers [ 37 ], but the increase was milder than ischemic stroke with a similar severity [ 32 ], supporting MR‐proADM as a reliable candidate biomarker accurately reflecting ischemia. HAIS with ICA/M1 occlusion, which one may expect to induce a larger ischemic core, showed higher plasma MR‐proADM levels than those without, but ischemic core volume did not. These results support the notion that increased MR‐proADM levels reflect the ischemic penumbra rather than brain infarction. Indeed, we demonstrated a positive association of plasma MR‐proADM levels with the penumbra, assessed with both qualitative evaluations, including CDM and MDM, and quantitatively using automatic application (RAPID software). The most relevant evaluation for the penumbra is estimated by a mismatch between the volume of infarct and clinical severity [ 28 , 29 ], or brain tissue with delayed tracer agent on perfusion imaging [ 4 ]. It is also useful to predict the penumbra by a mismatch between MR angiography findings and ischemic core volume, which can identify stroke patients likely to benefit from reperfusion therapy [ 30 ]. With the exception of this imaging technique, only proteomic analysis in rats with focal cerebral ischemia have offered serum RHOA and CDC42 as potential biomarkers for the ischemic penumbra [ 38 ]. To our knowledge, the current study is the first to demonstrate a reliable blood biomarker in the prediction of the penumbra in humans. It is necessary to discuss the mechanism accounting for the correlation between MR‐proADM levels and the penumbra. ADM is biosynthesized and secreted from vascular endothelial cells in response to hypoxia, inflammation, and shear stress [ 39 ]. In the ischemic core, as the vascular endothelial cells are severely compromised [ 40 ], ADM secretion is presumed to be almost absent, corresponding to no correlation between the infarct size and the MR‐proADM levels in our study. Moreover, a rodent MCA occlusion model study showed elevated ADM mRNA levels in the ischemic cortex even before the development of brain infarction [ 18 , 19 ]. Therefore, it is reasonable to assume that ADM is mainly secreted from ischemic, but not yet infarcted, region of brain tissue, namely the ischemic penumbra. ADM exerts neuroprotective effects by several physiological modes of action: vascular smooth muscle cell‐mediated vasodilation, angiogenesis by acting on vascular endothelium, oligodendrogenesis, and anti‐inflammatory effects by modulating immune cells [ 41 ]. Indeed, in the MCA occlusion model mice, infarct size was reduced by exogenous ADM administration [ 16 ], and conversely, ischemic tolerance was decreased in ADM knock‐out mice [ 42 ]. Moreover, in the bilateral common carotid artery occlusion rat model, ADM suppressed neurodegeneration and reactive oxygen species [ 43 ]. These findings suggest that ADM is continuously secreted from the ischemic penumbra and maintains the microenvironment around the ischemic region. MR‐proADM thus holds potential as a key marker in the prediction of the ischemic penumbra. It is also essential to discuss factors associated with elevated plasma MR‐proADM levels other than brain ischemia. In the current study, aging, hypertension, renal dysfunction, and CES were associated with increased MR‐proADM levels in HAIS. A positive correlation between MR‐proADM levels and age was reported in a general population of a large cohort study [ 44 ]. Plasma ADM levels also increased in patients with hypertension, indicating a counteractive hypotensive effect [ 45 ]. Furthermore, the positive association between MR‐proADM and cardiac diseases, such as AF and heart failure, were reported [ 46 , 47 ]. In contrast, elevated MR‐proADM levels negatively correlated with eGFR [ 48 ]. Hence, we adjusted these variables when MR‐proADM levels were compared between HAIS and control, and when multivariate analyses in HAIS were conducted to evaluate the ischemic penumbra. Because circulating ADM levels are also increased in severe infectious or inflammatory disease [ 49 ], patients with infection or inflammatory disease were excluded from the current study. This study has limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small. Second, we did not evaluate whether MR‐proADM levels could differentiate ischemic stroke from the disease control such as intracerebral hemorrhage, epilepsy, or trauma. Third, as we did not directly compare other biomarkers previously reported, it is unclear whether they perform to as effectively as MR‐proADM. Finally, though we investigated the association between MR‐proADM levels and penumbra volume using the T max perfusion parameter of >6 s, further analysis of other thresholds could yield intriguing findings [ 50 ]. 5 CONCLUSIONS Blood MR‐proADM is elevated in HAIS, especially within the ischemic penumbra and could thus serve as a potential biomarker for the determination of this region.

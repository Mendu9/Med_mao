Nonstandard Abbreviations and Acronyms ASPECTS Alberta Stroke Program Early CT [Computed Tomography] Score EVT endovascular therapy FIV final infarct volume ICH intracranial hemorrhage IGR infarct growth rate LVO large‐vessel occlusion mRS modified Rankin scale mTICI modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Ischemia NCCT noncontrast computed tomography NIHSS National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale Clinical Perspective What Is New? Identification of clinicoradiological predictors of patients with fast infarct growth rate at admission is poorly understood. This study found that for each 1‐point increase in National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score at admission, the likelihood of being a fast progressor increased by 12%. Moreover, for each 1‐point increase in Alberta Stroke Program Early CT [Computed Tomography] Score, the likelihood of being a fast progressor decreased by 44%. In the early window, all patients with Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score <7 were identified as fast progressors. What Are the Clinical Implications? A neurological assessment and early ischemic changes findings in noncontrast computed tomography at admission might help in the identification of patients with fast infarct growth rate. Early identification of this high‐risk population is crucial for developing novel treatments and triage strategies to improve outcomes. Endovascular therapy (EVT) has become the standard of care for patients with acute ischemic stroke attributable to anterior circulation large‐vessel occlusion (LVO). Rates of successful reperfusion have consistently improved since the first pivotal trials. , 1 , 2 However, despite these advances, <50% of patients regain functional independence. 3 4 The dynamic relationship between the ischemic core (irreversible infarct) and penumbra (potentially salvageable tissue) represents the essential pathophysiological principle underlying acute LVO ischemic stroke. , 5 , 6 The infarct growth rate (IGR) reflects the balance between collateral blood flow and cerebral ischemic tolerance. 7 Both linear and logarithmic patterns of IGR have been previously reported, 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 and factors such as blood pressure (BP) and collateral blood vessel status may influence this dynamic relationship. A high IGR leads to greater infarct volumes and higher rates of poor outcomes, despite successful reperfusion. 12 13 Low IGR is associated with a greater degree of ischemic tolerance, which translates to a larger salvageable penumbra over time. Because of the inclusion of a greater proportion of slow progressors, the late window (6–24 hours) trials observed similar good outcomes when compared with early window (≤6 hours) trials. 14 , 1 , 2 In contrast, fast progressors have larger final infarct volumes (FIVs) and worse functional outcomes, despite successful reperfusion. 3 Early identification of this patient population is crucial to optimize triage, expedite reperfusion, and improve clinical outcomes. 14 Herein, we aimed to evaluate the association of presentation National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score and Alberta Stroke Program Early CT [Computed Tomography] Score (ASPECTS) with fast infarct progression during the early and late window. In addition, we studied the association between IGR and functional outcomes. Methods The authors declare that all supporting data are available within the article. Study Population This is a retrospective observational study of prospectively collected patients with stroke from 2 academic comprehensive stroke centers from 2014 to 2019. We included all consecutive adult patients with acute ischemic stroke with anterior circulation proximal LVO (intracranial internal carotid artery, M1, and proximal M2) who underwent EVT and achieved successful reperfusion (modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Ischemia [mTICI]≥2b). Patients had a defined onset time (witnessed stroke onset or unwitnessed onset when symptom discovery was within 1 hour from last known normal), no significant prestroke disability (modified Rankin scale [mRS] score≤1) and 24‐hour neuroimaging after EVT. Patients with procedural complications, patients with reocclusion, or those requiring rescue stenting after EVT were excluded because of inaccuracy of the FIV estimation. Stroke management decisions, including the delivery of thrombolytic drugs and hemodynamic management, were made by the patients’ attending providers and clinical care team following then‐current American Heart Association guidelines. All patients were admitted to an intensive care unit after the procedure. Similar BP institutional protocols were performed in both institutions, with BP goals of <180/105 mm Hg if tPA (tissue‐type plasminogen activator) was administered and BP goals of <220/120 mm Hg when no tPA was given before EVT, followed by a systolic BP goal of 100 to 160 mm Hg for the first 24 hours after EVT. 15 Clinical data included the patient's medical history, demographics, neuroimaging data, angiographic information, and functional outcomes. The neurological examination was assessed by the NIHSS at admission in all patients. The study was approved under the waiver of informed consent by the local institutional review board at both centers. It is reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines. 16 Neuroimaging Biomarkers Neuroimaging parameters, including ASPECTS on noncontrast CT (NCCT) head before EVT and mTICI score, were adjudicated by independent reviewers blinded to the clinical data and 90‐day clinical outcome at each institution (D.Q.O. and N.P.). FIV on diffusion‐weighted imaging–magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (or NCCT when MRI was not obtained) within 24 hours after EVT was collected as part of a routine prospectively collected data element in both centers. FIV was calculated by automatic or manual delineation of the region of interest in the affected areas on diffusion‐weighted imaging–MRI (FIV was measured on NCCT in 6% [N = 13] of patients who could not get MRI). We worked under the assumption that there would not be a significant increase in the FIV after successful reperfusion. IGR was defined as follows: IGR = final infarct volume mL stroke onset to reperfusion time h Collateral blood flow was evaluated with the hypoperfusion intensity ratio, which was defined as the tissue volume with time to maximum >10 seconds divided by the tissue volume with time to maximum >6 seconds on perfusion imaging using automated software (RAPIDAI‐iSchemaView, Inc). Hypoperfusion intensity ratio <0.4 identified patients with good collaterals. 17 Hypoperfusion intensity ratio was used as an automated and quantitative alternative to CT angiography (CTA) collaterals to avoid interrater disagreement from 2 different stroke centers. 18 19 Clinical Outcomes The primary functional outcome was evaluated using the mRS by board‐certified vascular neurologists at each hospital at 90 days after EVT, and it was documented in the clinical chart. Poor functional outcome was defined as mRS score of 3 to 6. Secondary outcomes included mortality and intracranial hemorrhage (ICH). Symptomatic ICH was defined according to the European Cooperative Acute Stroke Study III trial as any ICH causing neurologic deterioration (increase of ≥4 points in the NIHSS). 20 Asymptomatic ICH was defined as any ICH that did not meet symptomatic ICH criteria. 21 Statistical Analysis We reported categorical variables as counts and percentages and continuous variables as means (SD) or medians (interquartile range [IQR]). For the univariate analysis, we used Student t ‐tests or Wilcoxon rank‐sum test for continuous variables and χ 2 or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, as needed. IGR optimal cutoff was identified by using Youden J index (maximum [sensitivity+specificity−1]). Patients were classified as fast or slow progressors if they had an IGR above or below the IGR cutoff value, respectively. The association of presentation NIHSS score and ASPECTS with fast IGR was assessed using multivariable binary logistic regression, adjusting for potential confounders from the univariate analysis. We also analyzed the distribution of functional outcome over the entire range of the mRS scale among fast and slow progressors (mRS shift analysis) using ordinal logistic regression analysis. 22 In addition, we evaluated the association between initial CT‐ASPECTS and IGR stratified by time from stroke onset to pre‐EVT NCCT. Moreover, we assessed the statistical association between NIHSS score at admission and IGR over time in patients with ASPECTS 7 to 10 within 6 hours from stroke onset using a linear regression model. Finally, we performed a sensitivity analysis to identify the Youden J index IGR cutoff and classify fast and slow progressors in specific subgroups. First, we evaluated patients with mTICI 3 treated up to 24 hours from stroke onset. Second, we assessed patients with mTICI 2b to 3 treated in the early window and evaluated the association between their IGR cutoff with CT‐ASPECTS and NIHSS score at admission. Last, we compared the diagnostic accuracy of our IGR cutoffs with previously published studies to predict poor outcome. Statistical significance was set at P <0.05. All the analyses were performed using R version 3.3.0. Using the ss function from the R package “npreg,” plots were generated by fitting a smooth cubic spline with 92 knots. Time from stroke onset to reperfusion was ≤6 hours in 133 patients (62.7%) and >6 hours to 24 hours in 79 patients (37.3%). ASPECTS and hypoperfusion intensity ratio showed only a weak correlation with IGR ( r =−0.34 and r =0.31, respectively) and were therefore included in the multivariable logistic regression. After adjusting for confounders, the odds of shifting toward a higher (worse) score on the mRS increased by 2.5 fold among fast progressors compared with slow progressors (adjusted OR, 2.5; 95% CI, 1.35–4.62; P =0.003) (Figure S1 in the Data Supplement). Fast progressors had the highest odds for a poor functional outcome (mRS score 3–6) after adjusting for confounders (adjusted OR, 2.74; 95% CI, 1.26–6.12; P =0.012) (Table S3 in the Data Supplement). The overall mortality at 90 days was 15.5% in patients who received successful EVT. Fast progressors had a higher mortality rate than the slow progressors group (21% versus 5%, respectively; P =0.002). Among all variables in the logistic regression model, fast progressor status had the highest odds for predicting 90‐day mortality (adjusted OR, 5.11; 95% CI, 1.7–19) (Table S4 in the Data Supplement). In the univariate analysis, fast progressors had higher rates of asymptomatic ICH (45% versus 23%; P =0.001) (Table 1 ). Impact of IGR on ASPECTS and NIHSS Score in the Early Window An ASPECTS <7 at admission NCCT obtained within 6 hours of symptom onset was only seen in fast progressors, and half of all fast progressors presented with an ASPECTS <7 (Figure 3A ). Patients with ASPECTS 9 to 10 were 50% less likely to be fast progressors, whereas patients with ASPECTS 7 to 8 had similar odds of being either fast or slow progressors (Figure 3B ). When we analyzed the NIHSS scores in patients with ASPECTS 7 to 10 within 6 hours from stroke onset, we found significantly higher mean NIHSS scores in fast progressors over time ( P <0.001). In addition, the difference in the overall marginal mean NIHSS score during the first 6 hours between fast and slow progressors was significant (17±6 versus 13±8; P <0.001). Moreover, the univariable analysis did not show differences in the left stroke location between fast and slow progressors (44% versus 50%; P =0.588) in this subanalysis. Adjusting for the time from stroke onset to NCCT, the estimate of the difference in NIHSS score means between fast and slow progressors was about 5 NIHSS units (estimate, 4.910; P <0.001) (Figure 3C ). Sensitivity IGR Cutoff Analysis Our IGR cutoff analysis showed 3.2 mL/h had a higher sensitivity (76%) to predict poor outcome, and therefore a higher proportion of fast progressors (135/212 [63.6%]) were identified. The previously reported IGR cutoff of 10 mL/h had a higher specificity, and a lower proportion of fast progressors (68/212 [32%]) were identified within 24 hours from stroke onset to recanalization. 23 Patients with mTICI 3 treated up to 24 hours had a Youden J index IGR cutoff of 3.4 mL/h. In patients presenting within 6 hours from stroke onset to recanalization, the optimal cutoff was 14 mL/h. This cutoff had a high specificity (0.84) but low sensitivity (0.37), identifying a lower proportion of fast progressors (34/132 [25.7%]) (Table 2 ). Moreover, an ASPECTS <7 at admission NCCT obtained within 6 hours of symptom onset was seen in >50% of fast progressors (Figure 4A ). In addition, patients with ASPECTS 7 to 8 had higher odds of being fast progressors (Figure 4B ). Discussion This study shows the significant association of presentation stroke severity and early ischemic changes with fast infarct progression in patients receiving EVT who were treated within 24 hours. It also found that an ASPECTS <7 within the early window was only seen in patients with high IGR, which may help to early identify fast progressors without the need for advanced imaging. In addition, in patients with ASPECTS 7 to 10 on admission NCCT, fast progressors scored a mean of 5 points higher in the NIHSS score at admission within the first 6 hours after symptom onset. The IGR characterizes the dynamic process of stroke evolution over time influenced by the collateral circulation. It is an important and potentially modifiable parameter for determining clinical outcomes. Early identification of time‐sensitive fast progressors is paramount to maximize efforts to sustain the ischemic penumbra and decrease reperfusion times. In addition, early calculations of IGR based on pretreatment imaging represent an important factor to estimate FIVs. 24 Our study used stroke onset instead of last known normal time to optimize the accuracy of the IGR calculation. We also modified the traditional approach of previous studies where diffusion‐weighted imaging–MRI or CT perfusion before EVT was used to estimate the core volume, and we calculated the IGR from stroke onset to reperfusion instead. 25 , 11 , 12 , 26 Although our method could not be used to calculate IGR before EVT because we used FIV to compute IGR, we intended to maximize accuracy when exploring associations with the clinical and neuroimaging admission parameters. 27 Our IGR cutoff for classifying fast progressors was lower than the threshold reported in a previous study, which is because we included more late‐window patients reflecting a real‐world scenario. A sensitivity analysis of patients with TICI 3 showed a similar IGR cutoff in comparison to our overall population. Therefore, despite the fact that patients with TICI 2b might have further infarct progression after reperfusion, the Youden J index IGR cutoff did not change drastically. Patients treated up to 6 hours after stroke onset showed a higher IGR cutoff of 14 mL/h attributable to a higher proportion of fast progressors in the early window. 23 Thus, higher IGR cutoffs should be used to classify slow and fast progressors in the early window. Finally, our Youden J index cutoff of 3.2 mL/h had a high sensitivity, which would allow maximizing the identification of fast progressors from outside hospital to directly transfer to angiosuite. 28 Current limitations in incorporating IGR as a risk stratification tool to differentiate fast from slow progressors arise from the inability to accurately calculate core infarct before EVT. Although studies have used CTP as a surrogate of core infarct, evidence has shown that the absolute threshold of cerebral blood flow may be inaccurate during the early window (<3 hours) and might result in EVT undertreatment of eligible patients. , 29 In addition, pre‐EVT MRI and high‐quality quantitative CTP in transferring center are limited by its resource‐intensive nature and may not always be available. Thus, we explored the phenotypic patterns of fast progressors based on stroke severity at presentation and ischemic signs on NCCT measured by ASPECTS, the most widely used parameters for EVT patient selection ≤6 hours. 30 Our analysis showed that the likelihood of being a fast progressor in our cohort was 100% when patients exhibited an ASPECTS <7 on NCCT. Patients with ASPECTS 9 to 10 were 50% more likely to be slow progressors, whereas a similar proportion of fast and slow progressors could present an ASPECTS 7 to 8. This finding might be explained by the fact that hyperacute ischemic changes (<100 minutes from stroke onset to imaging) are subtle on NCCT, demonstrated as a reduced interrater reliability for ASPECTS. 15 , 31 In addition, basal ganglia regions could equally be susceptible to ischemic changes early on because of the lack of collaterals in the anterior and medial lenticulostriate circulation. 32 Sensitivity analysis using an IGR 14 mL/h identified higher odds of being a fast progressor in patients with ASPECTS 7 to 8, which supports the use of NCCT in the early window for early identification of fast progressors. 33 Our analysis also found that each 1‐point increase in NIHSS score increased a patient's likelihood of being a fast progressor by 12%, whereas each 1‐point increase in ASPECTS decreased the likelihood by 44%. Previous studies also reported slow progressors have lower NIHSS scores than fast progressors, , 23 , 34 , 35 reflecting an increased ischemic tolerance attributable to better collaterals. 36 Moreover, admission NIHSS score has been demonstrate to be an alternative to calculate hypoperfused tissue volume for patients receiving EVT. 18 Finally, a recent study showed that NCCT alone may be used as an alternative to advanced imaging in selecting patients receiving late window EVT. 37 Hence, admission ASPECTS and NIHSS score can be useful tools in helping to identify fast progressors. 38 The odds of shifting toward a higher (worse) score on the mRS increased by 2.5 fold in fast progressors. We observed a more significant increase in the percentage of patients with poor functional outcome over time among fast progressors compared with slow progressors (54% 0–6 hours to 77% >6 hours for fast progressors [ P =0.008] versus 33% 0–6 hours to 34% >6 hours for slow progressors [ P =0.9]), emphasizing the negative impact of time delays among patients with rapid infarct progression. Early identification of this high‐risk group could facilitate the triage and reperfusion strategies, such as direct transfer to the angiosuite. With direct transfer to the angiosuite, workflow times in the transfer of patients with LVO can be significantly reduced, translating to better functional outcomes. , 39 , 40 , 41 Furthermore, fast progressors are most likely to benefit from treatment strategies to sustain the ischemic penumbra, such as BP augmentation, until reperfusion can be achieved. 42 13 Strengths and Limitations Our data were collected from 2 large academic stroke centers with patients treated for up to 24 hours and independent blinded reviewers at each institution performing the neuroimaging measurements. We used the diffusion‐weighted imaging–MRI (94% of cases) to calculate the IGR because of its greater accuracy than CTP to identify ischemic core volume, , 26 and the time to reperfusion was calculated on the basis of the stroke onset and not based on last known normal. Although we only included patients with successful EVT to study the IGR, our overall IGR (mean, 11.3±17.8 mL/h; median, 5 mL/h [IQR, 2–14 mL/h]) was comparable to previous reports of patients with stroke who received 27 or did not receive 10 (mean, 12±13.8 mL/h; median, 5.2 mL/h) thrombolytics or EVT. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis with patients with TICI 3 showed a similar IGR cutoff in comparison to our study population. 43 This multicenter study has the limitations inherent to a retrospective design. We have demonstrated that drops in BP have been associated with an increase in the infarct growth in a previous work, which could act as an unmeasured confounder. However, BP at admission in our study population did not differ between fast and slow progressors. We calculated the IGR based on single imaging after EVT assuming a linear infarct growth 13 , 11 instead of a logarithmic growth, as shown in previous studies. 12 , 9 We only treated patients beyond 6 hours after the results of DWI or CTP Assessment with Clinical Mismatch in the Triage of Wake‐Up and Late Presenting Strokes Undergoing Neurointervention with Trevo (DAWN) and Endovascular Tehrapy Following Imaging Evaluation for Ischemic Stroke (DEFUSE 3) were published, incorporating a heterogeneity on the distribution of slow progressors, which might have decreased the IGR values in our study. Because of this, we did a sensitivity analysis identifying a higher IGR cutoff in the early window as well as different NCCT phenotypes. Thus, a higher cutoff might be used to classify slow and fast progressors in the early window. 10 Conclusions High NIHSS score and low ASPECTS are main predictors of fast infarct progression in patients with LVO. Early identification of this high‐risk population is crucial for developing novel treatments and triage strategies to sustain the ischemic tissue and facilitate reperfusion. Further research is needed to improve outcomes in patients with fast infarct progression. Sources of Funding None.

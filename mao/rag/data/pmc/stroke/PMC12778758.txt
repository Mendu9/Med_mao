Nonstandard Abbreviations and Acronyms EVT endovascular thrombectomy LVO large‐vessel occlusion OAC oral anticoagulant VKA vitamin K antagonist Clinical Perspective In patients with large‐vessel occlusion who are treated with endovascular thrombectomy: One in 5 (22.3%) patients had indications for oral anticoagulant therapy but were inadequately anticoagulated before stroke. Adequate anticoagulation in patients with atrial fibrillation was associated with reduced risk of internal carotid artery occlusion and better clinical outcome at 3 months. A substantial number of endovascular thrombectomy procedures may have been avoided if patients with indications for oral anticoagulants had been adequately anticoagulated. Endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) has revolutionized the treatment of stroke in patients with large‐vessel occlusion (LVO). Cardioembolism is one of the leading causes of LVO, with most cases caused by atrial fibrillation (AF), which is seen in 28% to 54% of all patients treated with EVT. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Other important sources of cardioembolism include mechanical heart valves, severe cardiomyopathy, left ventricular thrombus, infective endocarditis, and patent foramen ovale. 7 , 7 Cardioembolism, and in particular AF, is associated with greater baseline stroke severity, and higher rates of disability, mortality, and stroke recurrence. 8 , 6 , 7 , 9 , 10 11 In patients with AF, oral anticoagulants (OACs) are effective for both primary and secondary stroke prevention. Patients with AF taking OACs at the time of stroke have reduced stroke severity at onset and reduced infarction volumes. 12 , 9 , 13 In patients with mechanical heart valves, vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) such as warfarin reduce stroke risk by 75%. 14 Despite clear evidence of benefit and guideline recommendations, inadequate anticoagulation continues to be a major problem in patients with AF, mechanical heart valves, and other potential sources of emboli. 15 , 9 , 16 , 17 A study in patients with EVT has shown that 72% of patients with known AF were not effectively anticoagulated at the time of stroke. 18 In patients with mechanical heart valves, studies have shown that only 65% of patients consistently adhere to VKAs, which is reduced to 12% to 17% in resource‐limited settings. 18 19 We hypothesized that inadequate anticoagulation therapy is a major cause of cardioembolic LVO and is associated with worse stroke outcomes in patients treated with EVT. Methods Study Population The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. This study was approved by the regional ethics committee, and informed consent was not required. The study was performed at a comprehensive stroke center that provides a hub‐and‐spoke EVT service to a population of 2.8 million people across 11 primary stroke centers. Consecutive patients enrolled into a prospective EVT registry between January 2015 and December 2020, with a cardioembolic stroke cause defined according to the Trial of ORG‐10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment classification, were included. The registry contains data regarding patient demographics, medical comorbidities, clinical and imaging features of the stroke, treatment details, and patient outcomes, as previously described. 8 , 20 , 21 22 Stroke onset was defined as the time the patient was last known normal. Baseline and 24‐hour stroke severity was assessed with the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale by the attending stroke team. The Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score was used to assess the size of baseline infarct. Successful recanalization was defined as a modified thrombolysis in cerebral infarction score of 2b to 3. 23 Modified Rankin Scale scores were determined at 3 months. The primary outcome was the proportion of patients achieving functional independence, defined by a modified Rankin Scale score of 0, 1, or 2 at 3 months. Secondary outcomes included early neurological recovery, defined as a reduction in National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score of ≥8 points from baseline to 24 hours, or a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score of 0 or 1 at 24 hours; ordinal shift of modified Rankin Scale scores at 3 months; symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage 24 ; mortality at 7 days; and mortality at 3 months. 25 Cause of Cardioembolism and Anticoagulation Status AF was defined according to the American Heart Association guidelines. Prevalent AF was defined as documented AF on ECG before admission. Incident AF was defined as newly diagnosed AF during hospitalization or up to 90 days after the stroke. 6 For all patients with prevalent AF, a CHA 2 DS 2 ‐VASc score was calculated. Patients were dichotomized into those with adequate or inadequate anticoagulation. Patients were defined as having inadequate anticoagulation if there were clear pre‐stroke indications for anticoagulation, including prevalent AF with CHA 2 DS 2 ‐VASc score ≥2, mechanical heart valves, and prevalent left ventricular thrombus, , 6 , 12 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 who had never been prescribed an OAC, had documented OAC nonadherence, had documented reasons for not taking OACs, or had subtherapeutic VKA with international normalized ratio (INR) below guideline recommendations (INR <2.0 for AF with CHA 31 2 DS 2 ‐VASc score ≥2; INR <2.0 for prevalent left ventricular thrombus; INR threshold as per manufacturer's guideline for specific mechanical heart valve type). , 12 , 27 29 Patients with documented reasons for not taking OACs were included as having inadequate anticoagulation, consistent with earlier studies. , 9 , 17 The rationale for this is as follows: (1) Currently, there is no consensus on definitions of absolute and relative contraindications to OACs; (2) the benefits of stroke prevention generally outweigh the risks of previous bleeding, high bleeding risk, age, and frailty; and (3) treatment decisions regarding OACs are more likely to be driven by perceived risks rather than perceived benefits. 18 , 32 Patients were defined as adequately anticoagulated when there was documented adherence with OACs, and INRs were in the therapeutic range if the patients were taking a VKA. 33 Statistical Analysis Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, New York). Results are reported as mean±SD or median and interquartile range. Univariate comparisons of continuous and categorical data were performed using the Mann‐Whitney U test and Fisher's exact test, respectively. Propensity‐score adjusted logistic or ordinal regression were performed to assess the relationship between the presence of adequate anticoagulation and outcome measures, with propensity scores being constructed using logit modeling incorporating relevant confounding variables with a univariate association threshold of P <0.15. All tests were 2‐tailed, and P <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results There were 784 patients treated with EVT between January 2015 and December 2020, of whom 416 (53.1%; 231 [55.5%] men, mean±SD age, 67.1±15.9 years) were assessed as having cardioembolic LVO and are the subject of analysis (Table 1 ). Of the 416 patients, 190 (45.7%) were treated with intravenous thrombolysis with a median (interquartile range) last known normal to needle time of 130 (98–165) minutes. Median (interquartile range) last known normal to EVT puncture time was 235 (170–340) minutes, and 370 (89.2%) patients had successful recanalization. A total of 206 (50.2%) patients had early neurological recovery. At 3 months, 243 (58.4%) achieved functional independence, and 57 (13.7%) had died. Cause of Cardioembolism and Anticoagulation Status Of the 416 cardioembolic patients, 320 (76.9%) had LVO assessed as being secondary to AF; prevalent AF in 221 (53.1%) and incident AF in 99 (23.8%) patients. There were 48 (11.5%) patients with LVO caused by mechanical heart valves, 10 (2.4%) with left ventricular thrombus (prevalent thrombus in 3 patients), and 38 (9.1%) with other cardioembolic causes (Figure 1 ). A total of 242 of the 416 patients with cardioembolism were identified as having a pre‐stroke indication for OACs, including 191 patients with prevalent AF and a CHA 2 DS 2 ‐VASc score ≥2, 48 patients with mechanical heart valves, and 3 patients with a prevalent left ventricular thrombus. Of the 242 patients with a pre‐stroke indication for OACs, 175 (72.3%) had inadequate anticoagulation at the time of LVO (Table 2 ). Thus, of all 784 patients of any cause treated with EVT, 175 (22.3%) had pre‐stroke indications for OACs, but were inadequately anticoagulated at the time of LVO. Anticoagulation Status in Patients With AF With CHA 2 DS 2 ‐VASc Score ≥2 There were 191 patients with prevalent AF and a CHA 2 DS 2 ‐VASc score ≥2; 84 (44.0%) had never been prescribed or had documented reasons for not taking OACs, 71 (37.2%) had been prescribed dabigatran, 21 (11.0%) had been prescribed VKA, and 15 (7.9%) had been prescribed rivaroxaban. There were 59 of 191 (30.9%) patients with prevalent AF with CHA 2 DS 2 ‐VASc score ≥2 deemed as having adequate anticoagulation, and these patients were more likely to be male (69.5% versus 43.2%; P =0.001) with previous stroke (28.8% versus 15.2%; P =0.03), and less likely to have extra‐ or intracranial internal carotid artery LVO (6.8% versus 18.9%; P =0.03). Propensity‐score adjusted logistic regression demonstrated that in patients with AF with CHA 2 DS 2 ‐VASc scores ≥2, adequate anticoagulation was associated with improved 3‐month functional independence (odds ratio, 1.94; [95% CI, 1.02–3.78]; P =0.04) but not 3‐month mortality (Table 4 , Figure 2 ). Discussion Cardioembolism was determined to be the likely stroke mechanism in just over half of all patients treated with EVT. One of the major findings of this study is that 1 in 5 (22.3%) patients with EVT were inadequately anticoagulated despite an indication at the time of LVO, suggesting that many of these strokes were potentially preventable. Adequate anticoagulation was not associated with outcome across all of the patients with cardioembolism with OAC indications. However, adequate anticoagulation in patients with AF with CHA 2 DS 2 ‐VASc scores ≥2 was associated with lower rates of internal carotid artery occlusion and 2‐fold higher rates of 3‐month functional independence. These findings are in keeping with previous studies showing that therapeutic anticoagulation reduces clot size and burden, the risk of proximal LVO, and infarct size. , 9 , 13 Direct OAC use in patients with AF was associated with lower odds of occlusion at proximal artery sites and smaller infarct volumes compared with patients not taking OACs. 14 Another study of patients with AF found that stroke severity was lower in those taking direct OACs and VKAs with therapeutic INRs, and direct OAC use was associated with favorable 3‐month outcome after adjusting for prognostic confounders. 13 This earlier study also reported that the rates of EVT were no different among anticoagulation and control groups but did not specifically look at outcomes in the patients treated with EVT. 34 There was an association between anticoagulation status and EVT outcomes in patients with AF where OACs were indicated before stroke. However, when the analysis was expanded to include other patients with cardioembolic LVO with indications for OACs, there was no association between anticoagulation status and EVT outcomes. Possible explanations for this loss of association in the expanded group include the younger age, the different pathophysiology of cardioembolism, and the absence of an association between anticoagulation status and site of LVO in the patients with mechanical heart valves. Nonadherence was the major reason for inadequate anticoagulation in the patients with mechanical heart valves and has been cited as a major risk factor for adverse events in patients treated with VKAs. 35 A total of 69.1% of those with prevalent AF and OAC indications were inadequately anticoagulated at the time of LVO, a finding not unique to our study. , 9 , 17 In a North American study of 94 474 patients with ischemic stroke, 84% of patients with prevalent AF and a pre‐stroke CHA 18 2 DS 2 ‐VASc score ≥2 were inadequately anticoagulated. The high rates of underuse of and nonadherence with OACs represent missed opportunities for stroke prevention. A systematic review of 28 randomized trials has shown that self‐monitoring and self‐management programs in OACs halve thromboembolic events. 9 Physician education to improve prescribing of OACs, including effective risk communication strategies and patient self‐management programs to improve adherence with OACs, is warranted. 36 37 A number of methodological limitations need to be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. The observational nature precludes the inference of causality. The single‐center retrospective design and inclusion of only patients treated with EVT with a relatively small sample size has the potential to introduce selection bias and limits the generalizability of the findings. It is possible that unmeasured and residual confounding might have contributed to the results. We did not evaluate stroke recurrence rates or anticoagulation adequacy following LVO. Lack of a control cohort limits the assessment of potential benefits of adequate anticoagulation therapy. This study also has a number of strengths. This report is of a homogeneous cohort in that all patients were independent at the time of stroke, all had an LVO, and all had comprehensive clinical and radiological evaluation as part of EVT assessment. In summary, cardioembolism was the leading cause of LVO in patients treated with EVT. In patients with prevalent AF and where OACs were indicated before stroke, adequate anticoagulation was associated with a reduced risk of internal carotid artery occlusion and increased functional independence. One in five (22.3%) of all EVT cases of any cause had pre‐stroke indications for OACs but had inadequate anticoagulation at the time of stroke. OACs reduce stroke risk by 60% to 70% in people with AF and mechanical heart valves. , 12 These figures suggest that a substantial number of EVT procedures could have been avoided if patients with indications for OACs had been adequately anticoagulated. 15 Funding The Neurological Foundation of New Zealand funds a Clinical Fellowship for J.

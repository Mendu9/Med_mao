Introduction Computed tomography (CT) perfusion provides both hemodynamic and physiologic information and is now commonly used to triage patients with acute ischemic stroke ( 1 ). According to the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association guidelines, CT perfusion can be used to select candidates for mechanical thrombectomy within 6–24 hours of symptom onset ( 2 - 4 ). Recently, the estimation of ischemic core volume has been utilized as an imaging criterion in the ANGEL-ASPECT and SELECT-2 trials to facilitate the selection of patients with large ischemic cores for endovascular thrombectomy ( 5 , 6 ). Therefore, accurate quantification of the ischemic core volume is critical for making appropriate treatment decisions. However, there is currently no global standardization of CT perfusion acquisition protocols, imaging parameters, or threshold values used to estimate ischemic core volume. In terms of parameters and thresholds, cerebral blood flow (CBF) has been established as the most reliable parameter for quantifying ischemic core volume, with proposed thresholds ranging from 30% to 45%, depending on the deconvolution algorithm and post-processing software used ( 7 - 10 ). Regarding acquisition protocols, current recommendations aim to minimize both iodine contrast load and radiation dose by using a low tube voltage of 70–80 kVp and administering 35–45 mL of high concentration iodine contrast media (typically 350–370 mgI/mL) at an injection rate of 4–7 mL/s, with an acquisition time of at least 60 seconds ( 11 - 13 ). Although a lower tube voltage enhances CT attenuation due to the k-edge effect of iodine, it may also result in increased image noise. This is particularly relevant because singular value decomposition, the most commonly used deconvolution method, is highly sensitive to image noise ( 11 , 14 ). CT image quality primarily depends on image noise, spatial resolution, and contrast. Notably, image noise is inversely proportional to the square root of the slice thickness ( 15 ). With advancements in CT technology, particularly multidetector-row CT, imaging at very thin slice thicknesses, such as 0.5 and 0.625 mm, has become feasible. However, in brain CT perfusion, thicker slice thicknesses (typically 5 or 10 mm) are often employed to improve the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) ( 16 ). While increased slice thickness reduces image noise and enhances SNR, it also amplifies the partial volume effect and may reduce diagnostic accuracy, particularly for small lesions ( 17 ). Optimizing CT slice thickness in acute stroke imaging may improve the accuracy of ischemic core estimation, potentially guiding more effective treatment decisions and enhancing patient outcomes. To our knowledge, no prior studies have specifically evaluated the effect of slice thickness on the accuracy of the ischemic core volume estimation using CT perfusion. Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine the optimal slice thickness for quantifying CT perfusion-derived ischemic core volume in patients with acute ischemic stroke, using diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI)-derived ischemic core volume as the reference standard. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-aw-2390/rc ). Methods Patients The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by institutional review board of Seoul National University Hospital (No. H-2304-055-1421) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived. We retrospectively reviewed the radiology database to identify patients with acute ischemic stroke. Inclusion criteria were patients aged over 18 years who underwent both CT perfusion and DWI. Exclusion criteria included: absence of occlusion in the large vessel of the anterior circulation, time from onset exceeding 24 hours, DWI obtained more than 90 minutes after CT perfusion, and inadequate image quality of either CT perfusion and DWI due to severe motion artifact and incomplete raw data ( ). Image acquisition All CT examinations were performed using a CT scanner (Aquilion ONE, Canon Medical Systems Corp., Otawara, Japan and Revolution Apex, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA). Scanning parameters were as follows: tube voltage of 80 kVp, tube current of 150 mA, rotation of 0.5 seconds, field of view 240 mm, slice thickness of 0.5 (Canon) and 0.625 (GE) mm, with z-axis coverage of 16 and 14 cm, respectively. The total acquisition time was 64 seconds. A bolus of 50 mL of non-ionic contrast media Iomeron 400 (400 mgI/mL, Bracco, Milan, Italy) was administrated at a flow rate of 5 mL/s, followed by a 30 mL saline flush at the same rate, using a power injector. All images were reconstructed using adaptive iterative dose reduction 3-D (AIDR-3D; Canon Medical Systems Corp.) and 80% adaptive statistical iterative reconstruction (ASIR, GE Healthcare). The reconstructed datasets were resampled at slice thicknesses of 1, 3, 5, 7, and 10 mm using Cercare Medical Neurosuite (Cercare Medical, Aarhus, Denmark). DWI and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps were obtained using a 3T magnetic resonance imaging scanner with the following parameters: repetition time, 6,000 ms; echo time, 73 ms; field of view, 240×240 mm 2 ; 38 slices; matrix size, 160×160; and slice thickness, 3 mm with a 1 mm interslice gap. Post-processing analysis CT perfusion maps were processed using fully automated perfusion software (RAPID, iSchema View, Menlo Park, CA, USA). Parameters including the cerebral blood volume (CBV), CBF, mean transit time (MTT), time to peak (TTP), and time to maximum (Tmax) were generated following automated registration, segmentation, and motion correction. The CT perfusion-derived ischemic core volume was defined based on a relative CBF (rCBF) <20–38% and a relative CBV (rCBV) threshold of <34–42%. DWI-derived ischemic core volume was automatically calculated using an ADC threshold of 620×10 −6 mm 2 /s with Olea Sphere software (Olea Medical, La Ciotat, France) ( 18 ). Semi-automatic analysis of the DWI-derived ischemic core volume was performed when necessary. Statistical analysis Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests. The mean absolute error (MAE) was calculated as the absolute difference between DWI-derived and CT perfusion-derived ischemic core volumes. Agreement between two modalities was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement (ICC >0.9, excellent; >0.75–0.9, good; 0.5–0.75, moderate; <0.5, poor) ( 19 ), along with Bland-Altman analysis to determine the bias and 95% limits of agreement (LoA). The optimal slice thickness for quantifying CT perfusion-derived ischemic core volume was defined as the slice thickness that demonstrated the lowest MAE, the smallest bias and narrowest 95% LoA, and the highest ICC when compared to DWI-derived ischemic core volume. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS statistical software ver 25.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). A P value <0.05 was statistically significant. Among the 18 patients with large ischemic core volumes on DWI (≥70 mL), the proportion of patients classified as having large ischemic cores on CT perfusion varied according to rCBF threshold and slice thickness. Using an rCBF threshold of <30%, classification rates were 61.1% (11/18) at 1 and 3 mm slice thickness, 66.7% (12/18) at 5 and 7 mm slice thickness, and decreased to 44.4% (8/18) at 10 mm slice thickness. In contrast, using an rCBF threshold of <38% resulted in higher classification rates across all slice thicknesses: 72.2% (13/18) at 1 mm, 77.8% (14/18) at 3 mm, 88.9% (16/18) at 5 mm, 77.8% (14/18) at 7 mm, and 66.7% (12/18) at 10 mm. Overall, rCBF <38% consistently identified a greater proportion of patients with large ischemic cores than rCBF <30%. Discussion This study aimed to investigate the effect of image slice thickness on the quantification of ischemic core volume derived from CT perfusion in patients with acute ischemic stroke. To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive study evaluating how varying slice thicknesses affect ischemic core volume estimation based on perfusion parameters and thresholds. Our findings demonstrated the 3, 5, 7 mm—yielded the lowest MAEs, smallest biases and 95% LoA, and the strongest correlations with DWI-derived ischemic core volumes. These findings suggest that excessively thin (1 mm) or thick (10 mm) slices may compromise the accuracy of ischemic core volume quantification. Several major clinical trials have used CT perfusion-derived ischemic core volume with an rCBF <30% threshold to select patients eligible for endovascular thrombectomy in the extended time window (6–24 hours after onset) ( 3 , 4 , 20 , 21 ). As a result, this threshold has become the most widely adopted criterion for ischemic core estimation in clinical practice. However, accumulating evidence suggests that rCBF threshold of <38% may provide a more accurate estimation of ischemic core ( 7 , 8 , 22 ). The present study supports this notion, demonstrating that rCBF <38% threshold yielded lower MAEs, higher ICCs, and narrower 95% LoA across all slice thicknesses (1, 3, 5, 7, and 10 mm) when compared with DWI-derived ischemic core volumes. Although prior studies ( 7 , 8 ) have reported that rCBF threshold of <38% may reduce ischemic core overestimation compared with <30%, our results showed that, across all slice thicknesses, rCBF <38% yielded smaller absolute errors relative to DWI-derived ischemic core volumes and identified a greater proportion of patients with large ischemic cores than rCBF <30%. However, the potential for overestimation using an rCBF <38% threshold in patients with large ischemic core volumes requires further investigation. Additionally, prior studies have investigated volumetric agreement between CT perfusion- and DWI-derived ischemic core volumes within 24 hours after CT perfusion ( 23 - 25 ). A key strength of this study is the short mean interval of only 37 minutes between CT perfusion and DWI, particularly in the pre-thrombectomy settings. This brief time frame minimizes the effect of infarct evolution, thereby improving the accuracy of volume comparisons and strengthening the reliability of our findings. Image noise in CT is influenced by voxel size, which is determined by slice thickness, field of view, and matrix size ( 26 ). The standard clinical matrix size is 512×512, and the field of view typically corresponds to patient size. However, CT perfusion protocols vary considerably in slice thickness, lacking a standardized approach. While thinner slices, with smaller voxel sizes, offer higher spatial resolution, better anatomical detail, and reduced partial volume effects, they are associated with increased image noise ( 27 ). A previous study by van der Schaaf et al. examined the influence of partial volume effects on the quantification of the arterial input function and venous output function in CT perfusion parameters—specifically CBV, CBF, and MTT—using slice thicknesses of 3, 6, and 12 mm ( 28 ). They reported that slice thicknesses of 6 mm or greater significantly impacted arterial input function and venous output function, leading to overestimation of CBV and CBF. Consequently, they recommended the use of 3 mm slices ( 28 ). However, their study did not assess ischemic core volume quantification. In our study, the mean CT perfusion-derived ischemic core volume using rCBF <38% threshold was 28.85 mL (1 mm), 30.38 mL (3 mm), 30.78 mL (5 mm), 30.65 mL (7 mm), and 26.33 mL (10 mm), compared to 35.17 mL in DWI. Although thicker slices are associated with reduced image noise, the 10 mm slice thickness resulted in lower ICC (0.905) and higher MAE (15.97 mL), wider Bland-Altman limits (bias =9.12 mL), likely due to partial volume effects. In contrast, while 1 mm slices offered higher spatial resolution, the increased image noise may have compromised volumetric accuracy. The best overall performance—lowest MAE, narrowest LoA, and highest ICC—was achieved at 3, 5, and 7 mm slice thicknesses, indicating an optimal trade-off between resolution and noise. Moreover, post-processing time is also affected by slice thickness. Campbell et al. reported that automatic post-processing time for CT perfusion ranged from 3 to 10 minutes depending on the scanner and acquisition parameters, including slice thickness ( 16 ). Thinner slices (1 mm) may increase computational load, thereby prolonging post-processing. Our findings suggest that slice thicknesses of 3, 5, 7 mm not only optimize ischemic core quantification but also contribute to more efficient image processing—an important consideration in the acute stroke setting. This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center, retrospective study, which may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. Second, the DWI-derived ischemic core volume obtained within 90 minutes after CT perfusion was used as the reference standard, rather than the final ischemic volume measured on follow-up FLAIR imaging at 24–72 hours. However, in real-world clinical practice, patient eligibility for endovascular thrombectomy is primarily determined based on CT perfusion-derived ischemic core volume obtained prior to the procedure. Therefore, comparisons against short-interval DWI—performed before endovascular treatment—was chosen to assess the accuracy of CT perfusion in estimating ischemic core volume while minimizing the potential confounding effects of ischemic growth. The study only evaluated ischemic core volume using automated software with fixed thresholds. Future investigations involving larger patient populations and multicenter settings are necessary to validate the generalizability of our findings and to evaluate clinical outcomes in relation to ischemic core volume estimations across different automated CT perfusion post-processing platforms. Conclusions CT perfusion data reconstructed with slice thicknesses of 3–7 mm provided the most reliable estimation of ischemic core volume—at both commonly used and optimal thresholds—compared to thinner (1 mm) or thicker (10 mm) slice thicknesses.

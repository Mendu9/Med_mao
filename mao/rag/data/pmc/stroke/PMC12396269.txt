Introduction Stroke mimics can account for up to half of suspected stroke cases, with epileptic seizures representing around 20% of these mimics [ 1 ]. Given the time-sensitive nature of stroke treatment, accurate diagnosis and prompt decision-making are crucial. Failure to differentiate between stroke and its mimics can result in administering thrombolysis therapy to patients with conditions other than ischemic stroke. Although this carries a reasonably low risk of complications, it can still possibly be avoided [ 2 ]. One of the most challenging stroke mimics to differentiate is Todd’s paralysis, a transient postictal neurological deficit first described by Robert Bentley Todd in 1849 [ 3 ]. This condition is particularly difficult to identify when the preceding seizure has not been witnessed. Furthermore, seizures can occur at the onset of a true stroke, making it even more challenging to distinguish [ [4] , [5] ]. Cerebral computed tomography perfusion (CTP) has become a widely used imaging modality for assessing acute ischemic stroke. It is associated with increased diagnostic accuracy, aiding in the appropriate selection for reperfusion therapy and, to some extent, distinguishing stroke mimics [ 6 ]. The concept of perfusion imaging uses an intravascular tracer and sequential imaging to measure blood flow within the brain parenchyma and calculate various hemodynamic parameters, including cerebral blood volume (CBV), cerebral blood flow (CBF), and mean transit time (MTT) [ 6 , 7 ]. In clinical contexts, the primary aim often involves identifying 2 distinct regions: the penumbra, which represents tissue experiencing significant hypoperfusion at risk of infarction without reperfusion, and the ischemic core, which denotes tissue identified as irreversibly infarcted [ 6 ]. Perfusion abnormalities detected through CTP offer potential utility in differentiating strokes from mimics and guiding treatment decisions. However, like ischemic strokes, epileptic seizures can cause changes in cerebral perfusion, with focal hyperperfusion during the ictal phase and hypoperfusion in the postictal phase [ 8 , 9 ]. The existing literature on the role of CTP in distinguishing Todd’s paralysis from ischemic stroke primarily consists of case reports and case series. This small case series examines perfusion abnormalities in postictal Todd’s paralysis, aiming to improve understanding of diagnostic challenges and their clinical implications in acute stroke care. Materials and methods In this retrospective case series conducted at a tertiary care facility providing stroke services, we focused on stroke mimickers identified through CTP within the 24-hour window following the local hyperacute stroke protocol initiated in January 2023. All included patients underwent CT (Siemens Force, Germany) using a hyperacute stroke protocol that encompasses noncontrast head CT, Computed Tomography Angiogram (CTA), and CTP. The images were analyzed using the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) (Synapse PACS, Fujifilm Worldwide, version 5.7.102, Japan). Clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records. The study adhered to ethical standards, obtaining verbal consent from all patients and approval from the institutional review board, with a commitment to maintaining strict patient confidentiality throughout the research process. The data collection period spans from the inception of the updated protocol in January 2023 to January 2024. For data analysis, descriptive statistics will summarize patient characteristics, clinical presentations, and imaging findings. Given the retrospective nature and limited sample size inherent in a case series, inferential statistics may not apply. The analysis will focus on identifying patterns and trends in the diagnostic challenges posed by stroke mimickers within the acute stroke care context. Case 1 A 40-year-old male with a history of childhood epilepsy (last seizure was at 12 years old), diabetes, hypothyroidism, and chronic smoking was brought to the emergency department (ED) with sudden-onset right-sided weakness. Upon arrival, he exhibited expressive aphasia and right-sided hemiparesis. During the evaluation in the ED, he experienced a generalized tonic-clonic seizure that lasted less than 2 minutes, followed by postictal agitation. His National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score postseizure was 13. He was not on any antiseizure medication at the time of presentation. His regular medications included metformin and levothyroxine. Blood tests showed normal hemoglobin (15.0 g/dL), white blood cell (WBC) (7.5 ×10⁹/L) and platelet counts (314 ×10⁹/L), and normal renal function (creatinine: 76 µmol/L; eGFR >90 mL/min). Random blood glucose was high at 20 mmol/L. Electrolytes were within normal range (Na: 137 mmol/L; K: 3.6 mmol/L, Mg: 0.72 mmol/L). Serum Lactate was mildly elevated at 1.9. Liver function tests showed a mild isolated elevation in ALT (57 U/L). Calcium (2.36 mmol/L) and phosphate levels (0.94 mmol/L) were within the normal range. A noncontrast head CT showed atrophy of the left parietal lobe. A CTA of the head and neck revealed no large vessel occlusion or significant intracranial or extracranial arterial stenosis. However, perfusion imaging indicated reduced CBF in the left parietal and temporal lobes with normal CBV, suggestive of a perfusion mismatch. MTT was prolonged in the same region ( Fig. 1 ). Considering the clinical exam and CTP findings, the decision was made to administer intravenous thrombolysis 3.5 hours from the onset of symptoms. In addition, he was loaded with antiseizure medication (levetiracetam), and hyperglycemia was treated with insulin. He achieved a complete recovery of symptoms the following day. Initial MRI of the brain revealed no infarction but left focal parietal cortical atrophy. Although a transient ischemic attack (TIA) with early recanalization cannot be entirely excluded, the absence of arterial occlusion on CTA, absence of diffusion restriction sequence on MRI brain, and the patient’s history of epilepsy support the diagnosis of a seizure-related postictal neurological deficit. On follow-up, he underwent a conventional cerebral angiogram to investigate the possibility of a developmental venous anomaly suspected on a repeated MRI of the brain with gadolinium. The angiogram showed multiple tortuous vessels in the left parieto-temporal lobe, suggestive of a developmental venous anomaly (DVA). Fig 1 Case 2 A 43-year-old female with a history of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and chronic kidney disease presented to the ED with an episode of generalized tonic-clonic seizure lasting 5 minutes, followed by postictal confusion and slurred speech. She reported feeling unwell for 1 week, with complaints of generalized fatigue and lower limb edema. A day prior to presentation, she experienced recurrent jerky movements in her right lower limb, around 4 episodes in the last 24 hours, without impaired awareness. On clinical evaluation, she was found to be hypertensive and hyperglycemic; however, she did not meet the criteria for diabetic ketoacidosis. A thorough clinical assessment revealed a normal neurological examination, except for mild dysarthria. She was on biphasic insulin (NovoMix 30, 12 units twice daily) for diabetes. She had previously been advised to use irbesartan and carvedilol for her hypertension and chronic kidney disease but was noncompliant with both. Initial blood investigations revealed anion gap of 11 mmol/L, mild hyponatremia (Na: 131 mmol/L), metabolic acidosis (bicarbonate: 13 mmol/L), normal urea (2.1 mmol/L), and normal creatinine (70 µmol/L). He has mild anemia with hemoglobin of 11.5 g/dL, elevated WBC count (13.2 ×10⁹/L), and platelet count of 454 ×10⁹/L. The management plan aimed at controlling hypertension, optimizing glycemic control, and initiating antiseizure medications. She underwent CTP due to new-onset seizures and dysarthria to evaluate any possible acute ischemic insult. A noncontrast head CT showed preserved gray and white matter differentiation. The CTA of the head and neck revealed no large vessel occlusion nor significant intracranial or extracranial arterial stenosis. CTP imaging revealed a predominant defect in the left occipital lobe, marked by an increased MTT with a matched mild reduction in CBF and CBV ( Fig. 2 ). There was also reduced CBF and prolonged MTT in the left parietal lobe. The patient improved clinically after correcting hyperglycemia, leading to a return to normal speech. She was managed for a provoked seizure secondary to metabolic derangement and discharged without any antiseizure medication. Fig 2 Case 3 A 68-year-old female with a background of diabetes mellitus, schizoaffective disorder, and recent ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt surgery for normal pressure hydrocephalus 1 month prior, was brought by her family to the ED with a 1-day history of slurred speech and right-side weakness, followed by a tonic-clonic seizure upon arrival at the emergency room. Her regular medications include venlafaxine, risperidone and metformin. Her has normal hemoglobin of 13.0 g/dL, elevated WBC of 14.8 ×10⁹/L and normal platelet count of 237 ×10⁹/L. Metabolic blood investigations revealed an elevated anion gap of 18 mmol/L, normal Serum sodium (Na: 142 mmol/L), mild metabolic acidosis (bicarbonate: 21 mmol/L), normal urea (3.7 mmol/L), and low creatinine (32 µmol/L). Her Creatine Kinase (CK) levels were 225 µ/L (26-192 µ/l). C-reactive protein was mildly elevated (10 mg/L). Due to the initial rise in inflammatory markers, she underwent septic workup, including blood cultures and lumbar puncture, which did not reveal any microorganism later on. The patient required intubation for airway protection and was shifted to the intensive care unit. During neurological evaluation, she was intubated and sedated; her pupils were 3 mm on the right and 2.5 mm on the left, both reacting well to light. There were minimal movements in her limbs in response to pain, but it was difficult to judge lateralization. The plantar response was extensor on the right side and flexor on the left. Noncontrast head CT showed a right parietal VP shunt and bilateral subacute small subdural hematomas, more prominent on the left side. CTP demonstrated decreased CBF, normal CBV, and mildly prolonged MTT in the left frontal and parietal lobes ( Fig. 3 ). The brain MRI showed no acute infarct but bilateral subdural fluid and dural thickening. There were T2/FLAIR signal hyperintensities around the VP shunt tube and old hemorrhage along the shunt tract in the right parietal lobe. The seizure was managed in the ICU with levetiracetam and valproic acid. She received empiric antimicrobials (meropenem, vancomycin and acyclovir), which were later discontinued after negative cultures and negative CSF virology. She was extubated after a couple of days and showed clinical improvement with no focal neurological deficit. Fig 3 Case 4 A 67-year-old female with a history of diabetes, hypertension, migraine headaches, and scar epilepsy from the right occipital lobe (based on abnormal EEG suggestive of focal seizures arising from the right occipital region), attributed to a previously treated right occipital arteriovenous malformation (AVM). She was compliant with her regular medications that included levetiracetam, metformin, sitagliptin, glimepiride, olmesartan and amitriptyline. She was admitted via ED with complaints of incoherent, nonsensical speech and repetition of words. This was soon followed by left-sided weakness while trying to ambulate. This event was preceded by a prodromal feeling that she would have a seizure, and she requested to be shifted to a dark room. Upon arrival, she was found to be disoriented and developed a generalized tonic-clonic seizure that lasted 1-2 minutes, associated with versive deviation of her head and eyes to the left side and left-sided hemiparesis. She has low hemoglobin of 10.7 g/dL, normal WBC of 3.8 ×10⁹/L and normal platelet count of 235 ×10⁹/L. Metabolic blood investigations revealed an elevated anion gap of 21 mmol/L, hyponatremia (Na: 124 mmol/L), metabolic acidosis (bicarbonate:17 mmol/L), normal urea (3.7 mmol/L), and normal creatinine (82 µmol/L). C-reactive protein was normal (3 mg/L). A noncontrast head CT, along with a CTA of the head and neck, showed stable right occipital lobe gliosis with cortical calcification and ex vacuo dilatation of the posterior horn of the right lateral ventricle due to a previously treated AVM, with no extracranial or proximal intracranial arterial occlusion. However, perfusion studies demonstrated increased MTT, reduced CBF, and almost normal CBV in the right occipitotemporal lobe, suggesting a perfusion mismatch ( Fig. 4 ). However, considering the patient's background and clinical presentation, seizure-related perfusion defects were favored. Another antiseizure medication was added (valproic acid), and her serum sodium was corrected, followed by complete clinical improvement the following day. One year later, she presented to the ED with a breakthrough seizure and postictal left-sided weakness in the context of hyponatremia (Na 117 mmol/L). A noncontrast head CT and CTA of the head and neck revealed no new findings. She recovered the following day. Her serum sodium was corrected and she was treated for the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion with free water restriction. All of these patients presented with clinical seizures. The focal weakness resolved within 48 hours following the initiation of antiepileptic medication. All CTPs were performed within a 30-minute window from presentation to the ED, and none were associated with clinical seizures during CTP scanning. Therefore, it is plausible that we scanned patients during the postictal period. The CTP patterns in all patients revealed reduced CBF. There was normal CBV in 2 out of 4 patients with a CBF/CBV mismatch, which can be confused with an ischemic penumbra. The other 2 cases (case 2 and case 4) had a reduction in both CBF and CBV resembling the core in an ischemic infarct, with 1 of them (case 4) having an underlying focal lesion. Interestingly, the patient in case 1 underwent a follow-up conventional cerebral angiogram, which showed a DVA in the left parietotemporal region, corresponding to the area of cortical atrophy seen on the initial MRI of the brain. The CTP pattern in DVA is variable but includes an increase in MTT and usually does not include a reduction in CBF. In a small case series (3 patients) looking at typical and atypical CTP patterns in cerebral DVA, increased CBF and CBV with increased MTT were seen [ 10 ]. Another study examined CTP patterns in 5 patients with DVA compared with controls and showed similar findings [ 11 ]. Therefore, the changes seen in our case are likely related to the postictal state. In case 2, a common scenario of seizure triggered by metabolic derangement with no pre-existing focal brain lesion, as opposed to case 4, yet shares a similar CTP parameter pattern. Several case reports and series have examined CTP patterns in the context of ictal and postictal states. A case-control study included 67 cases of postictal focal neurological deficits; 64% showed a normal perfusion pattern on CTP, 21% showed a hypoperfusion pattern, and 14% showed a hyperperfusion pattern [ 12 ]. In this particular study, the variability in postictal CTP findings was attributed to a longer time gap between the ictus and CTP in two-thirds of the subjects [ 12 ]. In the same study, another 27 patients underwent CTP during the ictal state, which showed significant hyperperfusion compared to the patients in the postictal state. In another study, 3 subjects with postictal Todd's paralysis had regional hypoperfusion abnormalities compared to symmetric CTP parameters in 7 subjects in the postictal state without focal deficits [ 13 ]. Furthermore, nonconvulsive status epilepticus was associated with an increased regional hyperperfusion pattern in 9 subjects [ 13 ]. The CTP patterns in our case series involve both cortical and subcortical regions, corresponding to the presenting focal neurological deficit. The perfusion maps showed prolonged MTT in all subjects, similar to prior studies [ 9 , 12 ]. One study suggested that the increment in MTT during acute ischemic stroke is relatively larger compared to the increment in MTT during postictal Todd's paralysis [ 14 ]. Reduced CBF was present in all 4 patients in this series without significant reduction of CBV, except in patient 2, who had a mild matched reduction of CBV. Interestingly, in 2 out of 4 subjects (case 1 and case 3), there was a clear CBF/CBV mismatch, which makes it difficult to differentiate it from acute ischemic stroke in the absence of arterial occlusion on CTA. The variability in CTP patterns seen in our study may be due to pre-existing brain abnormalities. Another factor to consider is the duration of the ictus, as prolonged seizure may result in MRI changes [ 15 ]. Furthermore, it’s not known if seizure triggers, like hyperglycemia in case 1 and hyponatremia in case 3, for example, affect the CTP pattern differently. Our study is limited by its retrospective design and descriptive nature. We did not include controls, and the small sample size precludes any form of comparison or statistical analysis.

Introduction It is unclear whether renal function impacts stroke outcomes in patients treated with EVT. Renal dysfunction (an estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] of < 60 mL/min/1.73 m 2 ) is common among patients with acute ischaemic stroke, with reported prevalence rates ranging from 20% to 35%. In patients treated with intravenous thrombolytic therapy, poor renal function is associated with worse functional outcomes and a higher risk of symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage (sICH). 1-4 Whether this association also exists in patients treated with EVT remains uncertain. Previous studies report conflicting results, in which some studies have identified an independent association between renal dysfunction and poor outcomes after EVT, whereas others have attributed these findings to confounding factors such as the high prevalence of comorbid conditions within this patient population. 1-5 Renal dysfunction may lead to worse outcomes after EVT by promoting systemic vascular inflammation and endothelial dysfunction. 6-12 13 , Additionally, it could increase susceptibility to peri-procedural complications such as infections and haemorrhage, resulting in poorer functional recovery. 14 13 , Better understanding of the interactions between renal function and EVT outcome contributes to accurate prognostication and refined risk stratification, particularly given the increasing number of elderly patients undergoing stroke intervention. We aim to investigate functional outcome and complication rates in patients with renal dysfunction treated with EVT from a large Dutch national registry. 14 15 Patients and methods Study design The MR CLEAN Registry was a multicentre, nationwide, prospective, consecutive database of patients treated with EVT for acute ischaemic stroke in the Netherlands. Patients were included if they underwent arterial puncture with the intention to perform thrombectomy for acute ischaemic stroke. The choice of EVT technique and device was left to the treating interventionist. Radiological baseline and follow-up imaging were centrally adjudicated by an independent core lab of trained radiologists blinded to all clinical and treatment data except for symptom side. We used data of patients treated between 16 March 2014 and 31 December 2018, when the last patient was included in the registry. We included patients with a proximal occlusion in the anterior circulation on CTA, aged 18 years or older, who were treated in a MR CLEAN trial centre with a known baseline serum creatinine value. Data collection We collected data on patient demographics, previous disease and risk factors (previous stroke, myocardial infarction, peripheral artery disease, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, use of anticoagulants/antiplatelet therapy and smoking status), pre-stroke score on the mRS and NIHSS score at hospital admission. Process times including in-hospital to intravenous thrombolytic therapy (door to needle) and groyne puncture time, time from stroke onset to reperfusion and duration of the EVT procedure were collected. Since in the MR CLEAN Registry no eGFR values were collected, serum creatinine levels measured directly at hospital arrival and before intervention were used to calculate the eGFR using the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration formula. 16 Outcome measures The primary outcome measure was the full-scale non-dichotomis-ed ordinal mRS score at 90 days (±14 days) after stroke. In addition, the mRS score was dichotomised to obtain the proportion of functional independence (mRS 0–2), and excellent outcome (mRS 0–1) at 90 days after stroke. Safety outcomes were mortality and the occurrence of sICH within 90 days. Intracranial haemorrhage was considered to be symptomatic if patients died or deteriorated neurologically (a decline of at least 4 points on the NIHSS) and the haemorrhage was related to the clinical deterioration (according to the Heidelberg bleeding classification). 17 Statistical analysis Continuous variables were described using medians with IQR, and categorical and binary variables as numbers and percentages, unless indicated otherwise. The independent Student’s t -test or Mann–Whitney U test was used for continuous variables, χ 2 or Fisher’s exact test for dichotomous and categorical variables. For descriptive purposes, eGFR was trichotomised into categories representing different stages of renal failure according to the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration criteria : < 30 mL/1.73 kg/m 15 2 (low, stage 4 or worse), 30–60 mL/1.73 kg/m 2 (intermediate, stage 3A and 3B) and > 60 mL/1.73 kg/m 2 (high, stage 2 or better, no clinically relevant renal failure). We performed binary and ordinal logistic regression analyses to assess the association between eGFR and outcomes, analysing eGFR as a continuous variable. Missing data were imputed for the regression analyses only, using pooled data of 5 imputed data sets. Regression analyses were conducted in a hierarchical way using 3 blocks with possible confounders subdivided in demographic factors (age, sex), medical history/comorbidities (previous stroke, previous myocardial infarction, previous peripheral artery disease, diabetes, hypertension, atrial fibrillation and smoking status) and clinical factors/treatment-related factors (pre-stroke mRS score, NIHSS score at baseline, duration of EVT procedure, treatment with antiplatelet agents and the use of oral anticoagulants). Adjusted (common and crude) odds ratios (a(c)OR) were expressed with 95% CI, per 10-unit increments of eGFR. Analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 31.0 (IBM, New York, United States). Discussion In this study, we found that renal function measured by eGFR was not independently associated with functional outcome or sICH in stroke patients treated with EVT. Nevertheless, at 3 months post-stroke, mortality exceeded 50% among EVT-treated patients with renal dysfunction. Most previously published cohort studies have reported an independent association between renal function and functional outcome after stroke in patients treated with EVT. 7 , A limited number of studies have shown results similar to ours. 9-11 6 , 8 , Possible explanations for these discrepancies include differences in the study populations (eg, age distribution, cohort size or the use of adjuvant thrombolytic therapy), the extent of adjustment for potential confounders and the varying definition of renal dysfunction across different studies. 12 In line with other studies, patients with impaired renal function in our cohort were older and had significantly more vascular comorbidities. In such a population, controlling for known risk factors for poor outcome in ischaemic stroke is particularly challenging. As many comorbid conditions such as hypertension, diabetes and atherosclerotic disease are strongly correlated with impaired kidney function and often co-occur, the resulting collinearity limits the ability to discern the impact of renal dysfunction from that of other risk factors. 18 An additional explanation for the discrepancy between our findings and earlier EVT cohorts may be the unmeasured role of frailty. Frailty, although related to multimorbidity, captures a broader vulnerability to stressors due to sarcopenia, reduced physiological reserve, cognitive impairment and functional dependency. It is a robust independent predictor of post-stroke mortality and poor functional outcome, even after adjustment for age and comorbidities. Chronic kidney disease is strongly associated with frailty, with higher frailty scores observed as renal function declines and shared underlying mechanisms such as chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, anaemia and physical deconditioning. 19 20 In our cohort, patients with impaired renal function were older and more comorbid, but they were also likely relatively selected, as they were considered eligible for EVT and patients with extremely low eGFR or severe pre-stroke dependency may have been under-represented. It is plausible that reduced eGFR in this selected population only partially captured frailty, while a substantial part of frailty was already accounted for by age, comorbidity and other covariates in our models. Differences in outcomes following EVT depending on stroke aetiology have been reported in several cohort studies, particularly when comparing cardioembolic and atherosclerotic subtypes, with cardioembolic strokes associated with higher rates of successful reperfusion and better functional outcomes. 21 , Nonetheless, meta-analyses of the literature have not consistently demonstrated clear outcome differences between etiologies. 22 21 , In our cohort, both atrial fibrillation and peripheral artery disease were more prevalent in patients with renal dysfunction. The overlapping risk profiles for atherosclerosis and atrial fibrillation may explain the coexistence of these etiologies in patients with renal dysfunction, yet our results suggest these stroke subtypes did not independently influence EVT outcomes in this group. 22 Several studies have shown that renal dysfunction can impair platelet function and thereby might increase the risk of haemorrhagic complications after stroke leading to an elevated risk of sICH following thrombolytic therapy. 23 , Chronic renal dysfunction is thought to induce a persistent state of systemic inflammation, increasing vascular wall fragility and further increasing the risk of poor neurological outcome. 24 Interestingly, in our cohort there appeared to be no evident relationship between renal function and the occurrence of sICH, even without adjustment for possible confounding variables. A possible explanation is that clinically relevant reductions in platelet activity increasing the risk of sICH may predominantly occur at extremely low eGFR levels, which were underrepresented in our cohort. In this cohort, only patients treated with EVT were included. As such, a direct comparison of the outcome after EVT versus no EVT in patients with renal dysfunction could not be made. This study shows that after adjusting for different confounding factors, renal function itself does not appear to be related to outcome. Although eGFR can be used as a prognostic factor for disability and death after EVT-treated stroke, our results do not imply that EVT should be withheld from patients with renal dysfunction. 25 The strength of our study was the large sample size and controlling for confounding variables fitting demographic factors, different comorbidities and treatment-related factors. This study has several limitations. First, observational data are prone to an increased risk of bias and should be interpreted with caution. Patients in our registry were included at the time of groyne puncture, thus patients who were deemed ineligible for EVT by the treating physician (possibly because of renal dysfunction) were not registered. This approach may have introduced selection bias and consequently underestimated the adverse impact of renal dysfunction on clinical outcomes. Second, no pre-stroke data on renal function were available in our cohort. Estimates of renal function were based on a single measurement at hospital admission, directly after the index stroke. As the stroke itself may have acutely affected renal parameters, the measured eGFR might not reliably reflect the pre-stroke renal function. Ideally, longitudinal or pre-event data are used for better causal interpretation. 26 Conclusion In this large cohort of ischaemic stroke patients treated with EVT, renal function was not independently associated with worse functional outcome or sICH. However, renal function may serve as a predictor for disability and mortality. These results indicate that renal dysfunction should not preclude EVT in otherwise eligible patients with large-vessel occlusion stroke. Supplementary Material

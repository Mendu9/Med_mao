Introduction Stroke remains the second leading cause of death globally according to the WHO Global Health Observatory 2025, with approximately 15.3 million new cases annually and 62% classified as ischemic strokes caused by arterial occlusion . In sub-acute ischemic stroke, occurring 24 h to several weeks post-onset, accurate lesion identification is crucial for treatment planning, prognosis assessment, and rehabilitation strategies 1 , 2 . The volume and location of potentially salvageable tissue directly influence therapeutic decisions and patient outcomes 3 , 4 . 5 , 6 FLAIR MRI has emerged as the gold standard for sub-acute stroke imaging due to its superior contrast resolution and sensitivity to tissue changes compared to conventional T1 and T2-weighted sequences . FLAIR effectively suppresses cerebrospinal fluid signal while enhancing lesion conspicuity, making it particularly valuable for detecting cortical and juxtacortical infarcts 7 , 8 . However, manual lesion delineation by radiologists is labour-intensive, requiring approximately 25–35 min per case for complex stroke lesions 9 , and suffers from significant inter- and intra-observer variability, with reported concordance rates ranging from 60 to 85% 10 , 11 . This time burden is particularly challenging in acute care settings where rapid treatment decisions are critical for optimal patient outcomes. Current challenges in automated stroke lesion segmentation include intensity inhomogeneity across imaging sessions, artifacts mimicking pathology, irregular lesion morphology, and the need to distinguish acute changes from chronic findings . Traditional machine learning approaches using handcrafted features have shown limited success due to the complexity and variability of stroke lesions 13 , 14 . 15 – 17 Recent advances in deep learning, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have revolutionized medical image segmentation by automatically learning hierarchical feature representations . Comprehensive surveys of deep learning approaches for stroke lesion segmentation highlight the rapid evolution and diversity of techniques in this field 18 , 19 . However, standard U-Net implementations face limitations when segmenting small, irregular structures with varying intensity characteristics, which are typical of stroke lesions 20 . 21 , 22 This study addresses these limitations by proposing a novel multi-path residual U-Net architecture designed explicitly for sub-acute ischemic stroke lesion segmentation in FLAIR MRI. Our contributions include: (1) a multi-path convolutional block design that captures features at multiple scales simultaneously, (2) residual connections that preserve fine-grained spatial information, (3) comprehensive evaluation using proper patient-level validation to prevent data leakage, and (4) detailed computational analysis for clinical deployment feasibility. Related work Traditional approaches Early ischemic stroke lesion segmentation methods predominantly utilized classical image processing techniques such as thresholding, region growing, and atlas-based registration methods . These approaches often faced challenges in handling intensity heterogeneity and were sensitive to parameter tuning, limiting their clinical utility. Machine learning methods, including support vector machines (SVM), Random Forests, and fuzzy clustering algorithms, introduced a degree of automation and improved segmentation performance 23 . However, these models required extensive manual feature engineering and demonstrated limited generalizability across multi-institutional datasets with varying imaging protocols. 24 – 26 While multi-modal MRI approaches using statistical learning have been explored, traditional machine learning models consistently underperformed in segmenting sub-acute ischemic stroke lesions due to their inability to model complex spatial and contextual information . These limitations necessitated the transition towards data-driven deep learning techniques that could automatically learn hierarchical feature representations. 25 Deep learning approaches The emergence of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) significantly advanced stroke lesion segmentation performance. Zhang et al. proposed a 3D CNN model for segmenting acute ischemic stroke using Diffusion-Weighted. Imaging (DWI), which improved segmentation performance but incurred high computational costs . Chen et al.introduced VoxResNet, a 25-layer deep residual network incorporating voxel-wise residual connections for 3D segmentation, which demonstrates enhanced spatial learning capabilities but encounters substantial memory and computational demands 21 . In response to the computational inefficiencies of 3D networks, 2D CNN-based models. 22 have gained traction. Kumar et al. proposed Classifier-Segmenter Network (CSNet), an end-to-end architecture utilizing multi-modal MRI sequences, achieving a Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) of 0.84 on the Ischemic Stroke Lesion Segmentation (ISLES) 2015 dataset, although its robustness to intensity inhomogeneity remained limited . Liu et al. introduced DRANet, embedding attention mechanisms within the U-Net architecture, enhancing feature representation but achieving a moderate DSC of 0.76 24 . Additionally, Bousselham et al. explored the integration of the bioheat equation with U-Net for acute stroke segmentation 26 , while alternative U-Net variants have been investigated for various imaging modalities 27 . 28 Recent innovations have sought to tackle specific segmentation bottlenecks. Garcia-Salgado et al. incorporated generalised focal loss into an Attention U-Net to address class imbalance, achieving 0.70 DSC, but requiring extensive hyperparameter optimisation . Zhang et al. developed a Multi-Plane Fusion Network (MPFN), focusing on artefact suppression and false positive reduction, yielding a DSC of 0.62 29 . 30 Clinical studies such as those by Butcher et al. , Thomalla et al. 31 , and Cheng et al. 32 have highlighted the clinical relevance of accurate stroke lesion segmentation, emphasising the need for models that generalise well to variable imaging conditions. However, clinical deployment remains limited due to computational constraints and concerns regarding validation methodology. 33 Recent advances in 2024–2025 have further demonstrated the evolution of stroke lesion segmentation methodologies. Li et al. introduced SrSNet, a two-stage segmentation framework that leverages bilateral symmetric comparison of brain hemispheres to locate pathological abnormalities, achieving state-of-the-art performance on the ISLES’22 dataset with a recall score of 83.80% for stroke lesions with maximum cross-sectional diameter larger than 5 cm . The progression from traditional segmentation tasks to more complex clinical scenarios is exemplified by the ISLES’24 challenge, where the winning solution by Ren et al. emphasized that carefully designed preprocessing pipelines, including deep-learning-based skull stripping and custom intensity windowing, can be as critical as architectural innovations for clinical deployment 34 . Additionally, novel multi-stage cross-scale attention mechanisms continue to advance precise lesion boundary delineation capabilities 35 . These developments highlight both the maturation of established segmentation approaches and the emergence of new methodological paradigms in stroke imaging analysis. 36 Current limitations Despite these notable advancements, current stroke lesion segmentation methods exhibit significant limitations that impede clinical translation: (i) Inadequate intensity correction: Most approaches fail to adequately address intensity inhomogeneity prevalent in clinical FLAIR MRIs, leading to inconsistent segmentation performance across different imaging sessions and scanners. (ii) Suboptimal small lesion detection: Traditional pooling operations in standard CNN architectures result in spatial resolution loss, compromising the segmentation accuracy of small lesions that are clinically significant for early intervention. (iii) Methodological weaknesses: Many studies employ improper validation strategies, such as slice-level data splitting, which introduces data leakage and leads to overly optimistic performance estimates that do not reflect real-world generalizability. (iv) Limited computational analysis: The absence of detailed inference speed and hardware requirement assessments hinders the practical deployment of these methods in time-critical clinical environments. Our contribution In this study, we propose a multi-path, deeply supervised residual U-Net architecture that systematically addresses these critical gaps through several key innovations: Intensity normalization: Utilizing N4 bias field correction to effectively handle intensity inhomogeneity across different imaging protocols and scanner types. Multi-scale feature preservation: Incorporating specialized convolution blocks with residual connections and parallel pathways to mitigate spatial feature loss while capturing lesions at multiple scales simultaneously. Rigorous validation methodology: Implementing strict patient-level data splitting to prevent data leakage and provide realistic performance estimates for clinical deployment. Clinical deployment readiness: Providing comprehensive computational feasibility analysis including inference speed, memory requirements, and hardware specifications suitable for real-world clinical implementation. Methodology Data description We utilized the publicly available Sub-acute Ischemic Stroke lesion Segmentation (SISS) dataset from the ISLES 2015 challenge . The dataset comprises 28 patients with confirmed sub-acute ischemic stroke, imaged 24 h to 2 weeks post-onset. Each case includes four MRI modalities: FLAIR, DWI, T1-weighted, and T2-weighted images with corresponding expert-annotated ground truth segmentations. 37 Imaging parameters 3D volumes with 230 × 230 × 154 voxels, isotropic 1 × 1 × 1 mm resolution, acquired on 1.5T and 3T MRI scanners. We selected FLAIR sequences for analysis due to their superior sensitivity for sub-acute lesion detection . 7 Patient characteristics Mean age 67.3 ± 12.4 years, 64% male, median lesion volume 23.7 mL (range: 0.8-187.2 mL), representing diverse stroke subtypes and locations. Data preprocessing pipeline Bias field correction Intensity inhomogeneity was addressed using N4 Iterative Intensity Normalization (N4ITK) bias field correction . Alternative bias correction approaches have been explored 38 , but N4ITK remains the gold standard for FLAIR imaging due to its robust performance across different scanner types and imaging protocols. 39 Spatial preprocessing 3D FLAIR volumes were converted to 2D axial slices following neurological convention (patient right = image left). Images were resized to 128 × 128 pixels using bicubic interpolation to maintain anatomical detail while ensuring computational efficiency. The RGB format (128 × 128 × 3) was adopted for compatibility with standard CNN architectures. Data augmentation parameters and input size rationale To improve model generalization and mitigate overfitting, we applied a systematic data augmentation strategy during training. The augmentation parameters were selected based on common clinical variability in patient positioning, acquisition orientation, and intensity differences, ensuring plausible anatomical transformations. The exact augmentation settings included: Rotation: Random rotations within ± 20° to simulate mild head tilts. Horizontal flipping: Applied with a 50% probability to address lateralization invariance. Shearing: Applied random shear transformations within ± 0.2 shear factor to mimic minor acquisition plane misalignments. Zooming: Random zoom scaling in the range of 0.8× to 1.2× to account for scale variations. Brightness adjustment: Random brightness modification within ± 10% to simulate scanner intensity fluctuations. Rescaling: Pixel intensities were rescaled to the [0,1] range by dividing by 255. In total, these augmentation techniques generated approximately 20,340 2D slices, ensuring balanced representation of various lesion morphologies and spatial orientations. The input size of 128 × 128 × 3 was selected as a trade-off between computational efficiency and anatomical fidelity. The original axial slices were resampled from 230 × 230 resolution to 128 × 128 using bicubic interpolation, preserving sufficient spatial details for accurate lesion boundary detection while significantly reducing memory and computational load during training. The three-channel (×3) input was employed by replicating the single FLAIR slice across the RGB channels to maintain compatibility with standard convolutional neural network backbones optimized for 3-channel inputs, facilitating stable training dynamics and leveraging pre-trained model architectures where applicable. Data partitioning and validation strategy Primary data partitioning To ensure rigorous evaluation and prevent data leakage, we implemented a strict patient-level data partitioning strategy. The 28 patients from the ISLES 2015 SISS dataset were randomly divided into three mutually exclusive sets: training, validation, and test. Specifically, the training set consisted of 18 patients (64.3%), which were used for model development and hyperparameter optimization. The validation set included 5 patients (17.9%), used for final model selection and early stopping, while the test set comprised 5 patients (17.9%), which was reserved exclusively for the final performance evaluation. This partitioning ensures that no patient appears in more than one set, and the test set remains completely isolated from the model development process. Key design principles were adhered to during the partitioning process. Complete patient isolation was maintained, meaning no patient was included in both the training and validation sets. Stratified sampling was used to ensure balanced representation of different lesion volumes, scanner field strengths (1.5T vs. 3T), and demographic characteristics (e.g., age and sex). Additionally, temporal integrity was preserved by ensuring all imaging timepoints from a single patient remained within the same partition. Hyperparameter optimization strategy Hyperparameter optimization was performed exclusively within the training set using 5-fold cross-validation. This allowed for efficient exploration of the hyperparameter space while ensuring that the final evaluation on the test set remained unbiased. For each configuration, the training set (18 patients) was divided into 5 folds. In each fold, 4 folds were used for training, while the remaining fold served as the validation set for hyperparameter tuning. After evaluating all hyperparameter configurations, the model with the best performance on the validation folds was selected. The final model was trained on the entire training set and evaluated on the validation set for early stopping. The test set was kept completely isolated, used only for the final evaluation after model selection. Final model training and evaluation The final model training occurred after selecting the optimal hyperparameters using cross-validation. The model was trained on the full training set (18 patients) using the best-performing configuration from the hyperparameter optimization phase. Model selection was performed based on the performance on the validation set (5 patients) using early stopping criteria. Once the optimal model was selected, its final performance was evaluated on the test set (5 patients). Importantly, the test set was never used during any part of the model development process, ensuring an unbiased assessment of the model’s generalization performance. Temporal sequence handling Handling of temporal dependencies in patients with multiple imaging timepoints within the sub-acute stroke window was as follows: Single timepoint patients ( n = 24): Each patient contributed one FLAIR scan, which was assigned to the appropriate data partition (training, validation, or test). The standard processing and evaluation protocols were applied. Multiple timepoint patients ( n = 4): For these patients, all scans were assigned to the same partition. The most representative scan (typically taken 48–72 h post-onset) was used for training and evaluation, while additional timepoints were used to assess temporal consistency . Temporal consistency analysis involved evaluating the model’s performance across sequential scans and measuring volumetric changes and boundary stability using Hausdorff distance. Proposed SIS segmentation model Our proposed model extends the traditional U-Net framework with novel multi-path residual blocks designed to capture multi-scale features while preserving spatial resolution. The architecture of Fig. 2 consists of six encoding levels and six decoding levels, connected by skip connections to maintain spatial correspondence. Key specifications: Input dimensions: 128 × 128 × 3. Output dimensions: 128 × 128 × 1 (binary segmentation mask). Total parameters: 2,347,459 (all trainable). Network depth: 12 layers (6 encoder + 6 decoder). Skip connections: 6 levels with feature concatenation. Fig. 2 The architectural framework of the proposed SIS lesion segmentation model. Special convolution blocks The novelty of this architecture lies in the special convolution blocks employed in both the encoder and decoder sections, as illustrated in (Fig. 3 ). These convolution blocks differ from traditional convolutional blocks by integrating six parallel pathways in each block of input data, denoted as D0, D1, D2, D3, D4, and D5​, to enhance feature extraction and overcome the limitations of single-path networks. The design of Special Convolution Blocks illustrates that path D0 does not contain any convolution layers, thereby preserving raw spatial information through residual connections. Then the Paths from D1 to D5 progressively increase the number of convolutional layers, with D1 having one layer, D2 having two layers, and so forth, up to five layers in D5. This multi-path approach ensures that features at varying depths are captured simultaneously. Each path in special convolution blocks utilizes convolutional layers with a 3 × 3 kernel, zero padding, and filter size f, followed by batch normalization and ReLU activation. The outputs of all six paths are concatenated to create an accurate feature representation before being passed to the next block. The input shape of each block is x × y × k, while the output shape is anticipated to be x × y × (5f + k). So, the motivation behind the special convolution blocks is inspired by the need to overcome specific challenges like addressing information loss in traditional U-Net topology by introducing residual connections in D0, minimizing the loss of spatial information typically caused by pooling operations, and creating multi-scale feature encoding through parallel pathways. these parallel pathways facilitate feature extraction at multiple depths, enabling the model to handle both small and irregular lesion structures effectively. Additionally, this approach enhances the model’s flexibility by introducing the varying depths of convolutional layers in the pathways, offering adaptability to diverse feature complexities involved in ischemic stroke lesion segmentation tasks. Encoder-decoder mechanism The output from each encoding structure which utilized special convolution blocks undergoes scrutiny through two distinct approaches. Firstly, Each output is fed into the subsequent encoder block through a max pooling layer. Secondly, the same output is concatenated with the transposed output from the consecutive encoder block, acting as feedback to the corresponding decoder block to enhance features. The images at the lowest level undergo a max pooling operation five times, resulting in a six-fold reduction in the image size than the original. The decoder section mirrors the structure of the encoder, with a key difference: it uses a convolutional transpose operation with a 2 × 2 stride in place of max pooling to efficiently up-sample the extracted features. After each convolutional layer, batch normalization is applied to stabilize the learning process, followed by the ReLU activation function to introduce nonlinearity . To address intensity inconsistencies and other challenges inherent in clinical MRI imaging, the proposed model leverages the Adam optimization algorithm for hyperparameter tuning, ensuring optimal performance. During the investigation, various optimization techniques were evaluated. Incorporating Adagrad and momentum into the Adam algorithm produced significant training performance improvements compared to other methods 40 , 41 . 42 After hyperparameter tuning, which included a decay factor of 0.2 and a step size of 2. The model was trained with an initial learning rate of (1e -4 ) and a basic learning rate of 1e^(-6) demonstrated exceptional result outcomes in the proposed model. He_normal initializers were used to initialize the model’s parameters, offering an advantage over alternative initialization methods and the network weights were updated using the Adam optimizer with a batch size of 16. To enhance training efficiency and prevent overfitting, early stopping was applied by monitoring the validation dice loss. The novelty of the proposed architecture relies on the significant improvements over traditional U-Net through i) Parallel Pathways and ii)Residual Connections in the special convolution blocks. These design choices collectively address the limitations of U-Net in segmenting small or irregular structures and in handling intensity abnormalities present in clinical FLAIR MRI images and the proposed multi-path framework ensures robust encoding and decoding, resulting in enhanced segmentation performance in terms of accuracy and DSC. The search space included critical learning rate configurations, batch sizes, optimizer types, and architectural parameters. Key parameters for the optimization process were: Learning rate: Tested initial rates from 1e-3 to 1e-5, with different schedulers and decay factors. Training configuration: Batch sizes , and optimizers [Adam, AdamW, RMSprop]. 8 , 17 , 31 Architecture-specific: Number of parallel pathways in the multi-path block, filter sizes, and dropout rates. Regularization: Early stopping and data augmentation intensity. Optimization methodology The optimization process followed a three-stage approach. In Stage 1, we performed a coarse grid search on the most critical parameters—learning rate and batch size—using 3-fold cross-validation on the training set. After identifying the best combination of these parameters, we fine-tuned secondary parameters in Stage 2, optimizing the optimizer type, weight decay, and architecture-specific parameters. Stage 3 focused on optimizing regularization parameters such as dropout rate and early stopping patience. For each hyperparameter configuration, 5-fold cross-validation was used, where 4 folds were employed for training and 1 fold for validation. The model’s performance was averaged across the 5 folds. Selection criteria and evaluation The primary metric for selection was the Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC), with a threshold set to DSC > 0.80 for acceptance. Secondary metrics included Hausdorff Distance (HD), training time, and model size. During the selection process, we prioritized models with fewer parameters, better boundary precision (lower HD95), and faster convergence (fewer epochs to optimal performance). Optimal configuration discovery After evaluating 60 hyperparameter configurations across 300 training runs, the following optimal configuration was identified: Learning rate: 1e-4. Batch size: 16. Optimizer: Adam (β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999). Parallel pathways: 6. Filter sizes: 64, 128, 256, 512, 1024, 2048. Dropout rate: 0.1. Early stopping patience: 20 epochs. This configuration achieved the best validation performance, with a mean CV DSC of 0.847 ± 0.023 and a mean HD95 of 13.8 ± 2.1 mm. Hyperparameter sensitivity analysis We analyzed the sensitivity of the model’s performance to different hyperparameters. The learning rate, number of parallel pathways, and batch size had the most significant impact on performance. The sensitivity analysis showed that the learning rate and the number of parallel pathways contributed the most to performance variation. Computational considerations The hyperparameter search required approximately 156 GPU-hours, with peak memory usage of 10.2 GB. Training times for each configuration were approximately 89 ± 12 epochs per fold. We optimized computational efficiency by using early termination, which stopped training for configurations that failed to meet performance thresholds early on. Statistical analysis Confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated using bias-corrected bootstrap resampling with 10,000 iterations. Performance comparisons between models employed paired t-tests on per-patient metrics with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (family-wise error rate α = 0.05). Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d and interpreted as trivial (< 0.2), small (0.2–0.5), medium (0.5–0.8), or large (> 0.8). All analyses were performed using Python 3.8 with SciPy 1.7.3 and statsmodels 0.13.2. Performance metrics were calculated at the patient level to ensure clinical relevance. For hyperparameter optimization, we report mean ± standard deviation across 5 cross-validation folds. For final evaluation, confidence intervals were calculated using bias-corrected bootstrap resampling with 10,000 iterations on the test set results. Statistical comparisons between methods used paired t-tests on per-patient metrics with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Discussion Technical contributions Our multi-path residual U-Net architecture addresses key limitations in existing stroke segmentation approaches. The parallel pathway design enables simultaneous processing at multiple feature scales, improving detection of both small punctate lesions and large territorial infarcts. Residual connections preserve fine-grained spatial information typically lost in traditional encoder-decoder architectures, resulting in more accurate boundary delineation. The patient-level validation approach represents a significant methodological improvement over previous studies that employed slice-level splitting. Our results, while slightly lower than some reported values, provide more realistic estimates of clinical performance and generalizability. Clinical implications Automated lesion segmentation has the potential to significantly impact clinical workflow efficiency and treatment standardisation. Our model’s 45 ms inference time per slice translates to approximately 3 s per complete volume (assuming ~ 70 slices per volume), enabling real-time processing during imaging acquisition and supporting immediate treatment decisions in time-critical clinical scenarios. The high specificity (0.95) minimises false positives that could lead to unnecessary interventions, while adequate sensitivity (0.82) ensures that most lesions are detected. Volume correlation analysis shows strong agreement ( r = 0.89) between automated and manual measurements, supporting use for quantitative outcome assessment and longitudinal monitoring. However, careful validation in prospective clinical studies is essential before implementation. Limitations and future work Several limitations must be acknowledged. The single-dataset evaluation limits generalizability assessment across different imaging protocols, patient populations, and scanner manufacturers. The relatively small sample size (28 patients) restricts statistical power for subgroup analyses and rare lesion types. Future work should prioritize: Multi-site validation across diverse clinical environments. Prospective clinical trials measuring impact on diagnostic accuracy and workflow. Multi-modal integration incorporating DWI, perfusion, and angiographic data. Longitudinal analysis tracking lesion evolution over time. Uncertainty quantification providing confidence estimates for clinical decision-making. Comparison with literature Our results compare favourably with recent publications, particularly considering the rigorous validation methodology. The 0.85 DSC represents competitive performance while providing more conservative estimates than studies that use slice-level validation. The computational efficiency advantages over attention-based methods make our approach more suitable for clinical deployment. The multi-path design exhibits clear benefits in ablation studies, suggesting that this architectural innovation could also benefit other medical segmentation tasks. The residual connections prove particularly valuable for preserving small lesion details often missed by standard U-Net implementations. Conclusion This study introduces a novel multi-path residual U-Net architecture for automated sub-acute ischemic stroke lesion segmentation in FLAIR MRI. By incorporating six parallel pathways and residual connections, the model simultaneously extracts multi-scale features while maintaining fine-grained spatial information. The model achieved a Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) of 0.85 ± 0.12 (95% CI: 0.79–0.91) on the ISLES 2015 dataset, demonstrating competitive performance with state-of-the-art methods, while maintaining efficiency with 45 ms inference time per slice and a model size of 35.2 MB, making it suitable for clinical deployment. The study’s methodological contributions include a robust evaluation framework with strict patient-level data partitioning, systematic hyperparameter optimization, and statistical analysis using bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals. These innovations address the limitations of slice-level validation used in previous studies, providing more reliable and generalizable performance estimates. However, this single-dataset study, with 28 patients, limits the model’s generalizability across different imaging protocols and patient populations, so it serves as a foundational step rather than a fully deployable solution. Future work should focus on multi-site validation, prospective clinical trials, and regulatory approval. The integration of multi-modal imaging and longitudinal analysis of lesion evolution will also be crucial in improving the model’s clinical applicability. While there are still challenges ahead, particularly in ensuring robust validation across diverse environments, this study lays a strong methodological foundation for future stroke imaging research. Continued development and validation could lead to tools that enhance diagnostic accuracy, reduce interpretation variability, and improve clinical workflow efficiency, making automated stroke segmentation a valuable asset in clinical practice.

1. Introduction Urinary system diseases affect millions globally, substantially impacting patient quality of life and healthcare systems. [ Among these diseases, diabetic nephropathy, a major complication of diabetes mellitus, is particularly concerning. It affects 20% to 40% of diabetic individuals and is the leading cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) in developed countries, which has a poor 5-year survival rate, underscoring the need for effective interventions. 1 ] [ 2 , 3 ] Insomnia (difficulty initiating/maintaining sleep) affects up to 30% of the general population, [ and although it is linked to systemic conditions like cardiovascular disorders and metabolic syndrome, 4 ] [ emerging evidence also suggests its potential associations with urinary system diseases (e.g., nocturia, overactive bladder, and chronic kidney disease [CKD]). 5 ] [ However, the causal nature of these insomnia–urinary disease relationships remains unclear because of limitations of observational studies, such as confounding factors and reverse causation. 6 , 7 ] [ 8 ] Mendelian randomization (MR) has emerged as a powerful tool for investigating causal relationships in epidemiology. [ This method uses genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to mimic randomization in observational studies, thus reducing the impact of confounding factors and reverse causation. 9 ] [ MR analysis offers several advantages over traditional observational studies, including the ability to infer causality from cross-sectional data and prevent certain types of bias. 10 ] [ By leveraging the random assortment of alleles during meiosis, MR provides a natural experiment that can help elucidate the causal effects of modifiable risk factors on disease outcomes. 11 ] [ 12 ] In this study, we used a 2-sample MR approach to investigate insomnia’s causal relationship with urinary system diseases, with clear primary/secondary outcome distinction: diabetic nephropathy was the primary outcome (selected for its high clinical burden), and other urinary diseases were analyzed as exploratory outcomes. [ Using public genome-wide association study (GWAS) data, we aimed to clarify insomnia’s potential causal effects on these conditions. Our findings may inform clinical and public health strategies for urinary disease prevention/management. This study follows the MR-STROBE checklist to ensure methodological transparency and reproducibility. 13 ] [ 14 ] 2. Materials and methods 2.1. For the exposure variable, which was insomnia, we used 2 large-scale GWAS datasets derived from the UK Biobank. Both datasets included over 3,00,000 participants of European ancestry, providing robust genetic associations with insomnia. These studies were conducted by different research groups within the UK Biobank consortium, offering complementary perspectives on insomnia genetics. The outcome datasets for urinary system diseases, including diabetic nephropathy, CKD, urinary tract infections (UTIs), and kidney stones, were also sourced from the IEU GWAS database. These datasets represent a diverse range of cohorts and consortia, ensuring comprehensive coverage of urological conditions. To maintain consistency and facilitate analysis, all datasets were harmonized to ensure that effect alleles and effect sizes were correctly aligned across exposure and outcome data. We excluded variants with ambiguous strand alignment (A/T or C/G SNPs) to prevent potential bias in the analysis. The use of publicly available summary statistics from the IEU GWAS database allowed us to leverage large-scale genetic data without the need for individual-level information, thus allowing us to adhere to privacy regulations while maximizing statistical power. 2.3. Selection of instrumental variables Genetic instruments for insomnia were selected from the UK Biobank GWAS datasets based on a series of predefined criteria to satisfy the assumptions of MR. First, only single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) reaching genome-wide significance ( P < 5 × 10 −8 ) were considered. This stringent threshold minimizes the likelihood of weak instrument bias. Second, to ensure independence among selected SNPs, we applied a linkage disequilibrium clumping procedure ( r 2 < 0.001 within a 10,000-kb window) using the European ancestry reference panel from the 1000 Genomes Project. Finally, SNPs with a minor allele frequency below 1% were excluded to prevent any potential instability in the analysis. These selection criteria were designed to meet the 3 core assumptions of IVs in MR: Relevance : the selected SNPs are strongly associated with insomnia, as ensured by the genome-wide significance threshold. Independence : the SNPs are not associated with confounders of the insomnia–urological disease relationship. Although this assumption cannot be directly tested, the use of genetic variants as instruments reduces the likelihood of confounding. Exclusion restriction : the SNPs affect urological diseases only through their effect on insomnia and not through other pathways. This assumption is supported by the biological plausibility of the selected SNPs and their specific association with insomnia. By adhering to these criteria and assumptions, we aimed to create a robust set of genetic instruments for our MR analysis, which minimize potential biases and strengthen the validity of our causal inferences. To evaluate instrument strength, we calculated the F -statistic for each selected SNP. All F -statistics exceeded the threshold of 10 ( F > 10), indicating no evidence of weak instrument bias (Table 2 ). Mendelian randomization and sensitivity analyses We implemented 5 widely used MR methods to assess the causal relationship between insomnia and urinary system diseases, with the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method serving as our primary approach. The IVW method provides a weighted average of the causal estimates from each genetic variant, assuming all instruments are valid. For sensitivity analyses, we used the following methods: MR-Egger regression analysis: It allows for directional pleiotropy and provides a test for its presence. Cochran’s Q test: This test detects any heterogeneity in causal estimates across individual SNPs, which may indicate pleiotropy or invalid instruments. Leave-one-out analysis: This involved systematically removing one SNP at a time and recalculating the causal estimate to identify influential outliers and assess the robustness of our findings. These methods collectively provide a comprehensive evaluation of the causal relationship, while addressing potential violations of MR assumptions and ensuring the reliability of our results. Significant discrepancies between methods would prompt further investigation into the validity of the genetic instruments and the robustness of the causal inference. 2.5. [ For MR analyses and sensitivity tests, we used the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.5.6) 15 ] [ to detect and correct for horizontal pleiotropy. Data manipulation and harmonization were facilitated by the dplyr package (version 1.0.7). 16 , 17 ] [ For visualization of results, we used the ggplot2 package (version 3.3.5) 18 ] [ to generate forest plots, scatter plots, and funnel plots. Mendelian randomization results of insomnia and urological diseases Our MR analysis examined the causal relationships between insomnia and various urological diseases using genetic variants as IVs. The results are presented in Figure 2 . Figure 2. Forest plot of causal effects of insomnia on various urinary system diseases estimated by the inverse variance weighted method. CI = confidence interval, OR = odds ratio, SNP = single-nucleotide polymorphism. Two separate analyses for diabetic nephropathy using different insomnia datasets yielded consistent results. The first analysis (Diabetic nephropathy_1) showed an odds ratio of 7.73 (95% CI: 1.62–36.90, P = .010), while the second analysis (Diabetic nephropathy_2) demonstrated an odds ratio of 4.67 (95% CI: 1.03–21.19, P = .046). For UTIs, the analysis revealed an odds ratio of 1.01 (95% CI: 1.00–1.02, P = .022). The beta value of the MR-Egger method for UTIs showed inconsistent direction with the other 4 methods. Discussion This MR study investigated the causal relationship between insomnia and various urinary system diseases. Our findings suggest a potential causal link between genetic predisposition to insomnia and diabetic nephropathy. Interestingly, we did not observe significant causal relationships between insomnia and either type 1 or type 2 diabetes, suggesting that the association with diabetic nephropathy is independent of diabetes development. These results were consistent across multiple MR methods, including IVW, MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode approaches. [ Results of sensitivity analyses, such as leave-one-out tests and assessments of horizontal pleiotropy, further supported the robustness of our findings. 21 , 22 ] [ The substantial effect sizes (OR = 7.73 and 4.67 in 2 separate analyses) and the stability demonstrated through sensitivity analyses suggest a clinically meaningful association. Notably, our investigation into potential mediating pathways found no significant causal relationship between insomnia and either type 1 or type 2 diabetes, indicating that the influence of insomnia on diabetic nephropathy likely operates through mechanisms independent of diabetes development. While we observed a statistically significant association between insomnia and UTIs (OR = 1.01), the minimal effect size suggests limited clinical relevance. The elevated, albeit nonsignificant, odds ratios for conditions such as prostatitis and adrenal gland neoplasm may warrant further investigation in larger cohorts. However, the presence of significant heterogeneity in some analyses, particularly for chronic tubulo-interstitial nephritis and male infertility, underscores the need for cautious interpretation of these findings and highlights the complexity of the relationship between sleep disorders and urological conditions. 17 ] The observed causal relationship between insomnia and diabetic nephropathy, independent of diabetes, suggests alternative pathways through which sleep disturbances may impact renal function. Several mechanisms could explain this association. First, chronic sleep deprivation has been linked to increased sympathetic nervous system activity and elevated blood pressure, both known risk factors for kidney damage. [ Recent GWAS have identified genetic loci associated with both insomnia and hypertension, supporting a potential shared genetic architecture. 23 ] [ Second, insomnia may lead to chronic low-grade inflammation, which has been implicated in the pathogenesis of kidney disease. 24 ] [ Studies have shown that sleep disturbance and short sleep duration are associated with increased levels of inflammatory markers, such as C-reactive protein and interleukin-6. 5 ] [ Third, disrupted circadian rhythms resulting from insomnia may alter renal physiology, including the glomerular filtration rate and electrolyte homeostasis. 25 ] [ Recent research has highlighted the importance of circadian genes in regulating renal function, providing a potential mechanistic link between sleep disorders and kidney disease. 26 ] [ These findings have important clinical implications, suggesting that improved management of sleep disorders can reduce the risk of diabetic nephropathy. Healthcare providers should consider screening for and addressing sleep issues in patients at risk for or already diagnosed with diabetic nephropathy. Furthermore, these results underscore the need for interdisciplinary approaches that integrate sleep medicine with nephrology and endocrinology in managing complex chronic conditions. 27 ] [ 28 ] Several MR studies have investigated the causal relationships between insomnia and various health outcomes and between potential risk factors and diabetic nephropathy. These studies have provided valuable insights into the complex interplay between sleep disorders and various diseases, including urinary system disorders. Regarding insomnia and its causal effects, a landmark MR study by Lane et al [ examined the genetic architecture of insomnia and its relationship with various health outcomes. Consequently, they found evidence supporting the causal effects of insomnia on depression, anxiety, and coronary artery disease, highlighting the far-reaching impact of sleep disturbances on overall health. In the context of kidney-related outcomes, Park et al 24 ] [ conducted an MR study exploring the causal effects of sleep traits on CKD. Although they did not identify significant causal relationships for insomnia specifically, their findings suggested that genetically predicted short sleep duration was associated with an increased risk of CKD. This study underscores the importance of considering how various aspects of sleep health affect/are associated with kidney function. Turning to diabetic nephropathy, several MR studies have examined the potential causal risk factors. A comprehensive MR analysis by Xu et al 29 ] [ investigated the causal effects of various metabolic traits on diabetic kidney disease. They found evidence supporting causal roles for higher body mass index, higher systolic blood pressure, and lower HDL cholesterol in increasing the risk of diabetic kidney disease. These findings highlight the complex etiology of diabetic nephropathy and the potential for multiple intervention points. In addition, Hu et al 30 ] [ conducted an MR study examining the causal relationship between serum uric acid levels and diabetic kidney disease. Their results suggested a causal effect of elevated serum uric acid levels on increased risk of diabetic kidney disease, providing support for the exploration of uric acid as a potential therapeutic target. Although these studies have provided valuable insights, it is important to note that the field of MR research investigating the relationship between insomnia and urinary system diseases, particularly diabetic nephropathy, remains relatively unexplored. Most existing studies have focused on either insomnia and its broad health effects or on risk factors for diabetic nephropathy, with limited direct examination of the causal link between insomnia and urinary system disorders. Our study builds upon this existing body of research by specifically addressing the potential causal relationship between insomnia and various urinary system diseases, including diabetic nephropathy. By doing so, we aim to bridge the gap in current knowledge and provide new insights into the complex interplay between sleep disorders and urinary system health. 31 ] Notably, MR analysis revealed an odds ratio of minimal clinical significance for UTIs. Although this association reached statistical significance, the effect size was negligible. An increase of 1 standard deviation in insomnia liability is associated with only 1% higher odds of UTI. Given the minimal magnitude of this effect, it is not considered clinically meaningful for guiding UTI prevention or management in individuals with insomnia. Despite the strengths of our MR approach, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the genetic instruments used in this study were primarily derived from European populations, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups. [ Besides, due to limitations in the available datasets, the ethnic information for patients with chronic glomerulonephritis, CKD, and kidney stone was not uniformly restricted to individuals of European descent. Genetic architecture and allele frequencies can vary across populations, thus potentially affecting the validity of the IVs used by us in non-European cohorts. 32 ] [ Future studies should aim to include more diverse populations to enhance the robustness and applicability of the results. Second, although MR analysis helps minimize confounding and reverse causation, it cannot completely rule out the possibility of pleiotropic effects. 33 ] [ Although our sensitivity analyses did not indicate significant horizontal pleiotropy for most outcomes, the possibility of vertical pleiotropy or unmeasured confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Finally, our study relied on summary-level data, which precluded the investigation of potential nonlinear relationships between insomnia and urinary system diseases. Individual-level data would allow for more nuanced analyses and the exploration of potential threshold effects. 11 ] [ In addition, the use of self-reported insomnia symptoms as a proxy for clinically diagnosed insomnia may introduce some measurement error; however, recent studies have shown high concordance between self-reported and objectively measured sleep parameters. 34 ] [ It is important to note that many of these analyses, particularly for rarer conditions, have wide confidence intervals, reflecting the uncertainty in the estimates. This underscores the need for larger studies or meta-analyses to provide more precise estimates of these causal relationships. Specifically, the 2 diabetic nephropathy outcomes (Diabetic nephropathy_1 and Diabetic nephropathy_2) yielded wide 95% CIs (1.62–36.90 and 1.03–21.19, respectively). This broad interval range indicates high uncertainty in the magnitude of the potential causal association between insomnia and diabetic nephropathy, reducing the practical interpretability of the odds ratios. The low precision likely stems from 2 factors. First, the GWAS datasets for diabetic nephropathy had relatively smaller sample sizes than other urinary outcomes, limiting the statistical power to narrow effect estimate variability. Second, although the genetic instruments selected for insomnia met strict quality criteria, they explain only a fraction of insomnia’s heritability, which may further attenuate the precision of causal effect calculations. These limitations highlight the need for future studies to use larger, more powerful diabetic nephropathy GWAS datasets to validate and refine our current observations. 35 ] Moreover, the application of advanced statistical methods in MR studies, such as multivariable MR and factorial MR, could help disentangle the complex relationships among sleep disorders, metabolic traits, and urinary system diseases. [ These approaches could account for potential mediators and explore the joint effects of multiple exposures. In addition, the use of nonlinear MR methods could reveal potential threshold effects or U-shaped relationships between sleep duration and health outcomes. 36 ] [ As the field of genetic epidemiology continues to evolve, the integration of MR with other causal inference methods, such as negative control studies and cross-contextual comparisons, could provide more robust evidence for causal relationships. 11 ] [ Notably, we did not conduct formal multiple comparison correction for the following reasons: 36 ] Exploratory nature of the analyses : we did not apply formal correction as our multi-outcome analysis (six urinary conditions) was exploratory and focused on hypothesis generation rather than confirmatory testing. Strict correction here would be overly conservative, risking dismissal of biologically meaningful signals that may deserve more attention, consistent with conventions for exploratory MR studies. Robustness and biological plausibility of findings : the outcomes showed significant associations with consistent positive effect sizes, all robust to sensitivity tests (MR-Egger and weighted median). Their alignment with shared pathological links (e.g., inflammatory dysregulation) between insomnia and urinary diseases further supports plausibility. These findings should be interpreted as preliminary hypotheses for future validation. Building on the limitations and implications of our current findings, several avenues for future research emerge to advance our understanding of the insomnia–urinary system disease relationship. Given that our study relied solely on European population data, future work should first leverage multiethnic GWAS datasets to validate the causal links identified by us, which would enhance the generalizability of results across diverse demographic groups and account for potential population-specific genetic variations in disease susceptibility. Beyond validating causality, future studies should further explore the potential mediating mechanisms (e.g., inflammatory pathways, hormonal imbalances, and disrupted circadian regulation of renal function) that underpin the association between insomnia and urinary system diseases; uncovering these intermediary processes could yield novel targets for developing targeted interventions to mitigate urinary disorder risk in individuals with chronic insomnia. Besides, to strengthen the clinical relevance of such causal inferences, future research should consider integrating longitudinal cohort data with MR analyses, enabling investigations into how dynamic changes in insomnia severity over time influence the incidence, progression, and prognosis of urinary conditions, ultimately providing more actionable insights for clinical prevention and management strategies. Regarding the exploration of underlying mechanisms, future studies should apply formal mediation analysis or multivariable MR to dissect the potential mediating role of diabetes. By simultaneously including insomnia (exposure), diabetes (mediator), and urinary system diseases (outcome) in the analytical framework, direct and indirect effects can be quantified, and offering an insight into whether diabetes acts as a key intermediary in the insomnia–urinary disease association. 5. Conclusion Our MR study provides exploratory evidence suggestive of a potential causal relationship between insomnia and diabetic nephropathy, which requires confirmation in larger and more diverse datasets. These findings highlight the importance of sleep health in maintaining urinary system function and suggest that addressing sleep disorders could be a valuable strategy for preventing and managing the diabetic complication. As our understanding of the complex interplay between sleep and urinary system health continues to evolve, interdisciplinary collaborations involving sleep specialists, nephrologists, and epidemiologists will be crucial for translating these insights into improved patient care and public health strategies. Of course, the findings of this study are merely exploratory, and the practical clinical value of these results awaits further investigation and validation through more in-depth experiments (both in vivo/in vitro and in silico). Future research should focus on elucidating the underlying mechanisms, exploring potential interventions, and addressing the limitations of current studies to further advance our understanding of this important relationship.

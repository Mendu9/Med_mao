Key Points Treatment inertia and hesitancy, lack of efficacy in some individuals, side effects, poor adherence and other factors associated with high-intensity statins may lead to inadequate control of LDL-C. A moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe may be noninferior to a high-intensity statin in LDL-C reduction and CVD prevention with a lower risk of side effects and better treatment adherence. Moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe may be a promising upfront treatment for LDL-C lowering across several populations. Introduction Decades of research on statins have established the relationship between low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) lowering and proportional reduction in the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) [ 1 ]. The LDL-C goals have been set by treatment guidelines for individuals at the highest risk, and achievement of these LDL-C targets is crucial for prevention of CVD in these individuals [ 2 , 3 ]. The latest international guidelines, including the 2019 European Society of Cardiology (ESC)/European Atherosclerosis Society (EAS) guidelines for the management of dyslipidaemia and the 2021 ESC guidelines on cardiovascular disease prevention in clinical practice, endorse the use of high-intensity statins at the highest tolerated dose as the initial treatment to achieve the LDL-C goals [ 2 , 3 ]. This recommendation has not changed over several years, although new evidence for alternative treatment has been accumulating [ 4 – 7 ]. However, an alarming gap exists between the presumed treatment effects and real-world situations. Use of high-intensity statins and achievement of LDL-C goals are far from optimal in the real world; as such, a great proportion of patients continuously live with high LDL-C and are therefore at high risk for CVD [ 8 – 11 ]. Hesitancy to use high-intensity statins, treatment non-adherence, lack of effectiveness in certain individuals, side effects and other factors may have contributed to this situation [ 12 – 15 ]. It is therefore imperative to rethink whether there are other lines of treatment. Emerging evidence from clinical trials and real-world studies has shown a combination strategy of lipid-lowering, moderate-intensity statins plus ezetimibe (a cholesterol absorption inhibitor), presenting considerable efficacy/effectiveness, along with better safety and adherence versus a high-intensity statin [ 4 – 7 ]. Here we review the available clinical evidence, treatment guidelines and challenges in using high-intensity statins, and summarise the evidence on the combination therapy, moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe, as a possible initial treatment to achieve the LDL-C targets across several populations. Statins and Clinical Evidence After the discovery of cholesterol biosynthetic pathway, intensive research was conducted in the 20th century to look for cholesterol-lowering agents [ 16 ]. The rate-limiting enzyme in cholesterol synthesis—3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase—was sought after as a promising target. Statins are competitive inhibitors of HMG-CoA reductase that are isolated from the culture broths of fungi. The first statin, lovastatin, was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 1987. Since then, numerous studies of statins have been conducted, which have corroborated the efficacy of statins in lowering blood cholesterol and preventing CVD. The degree of LDL-C reduction varies among different statins and by dose. Doubling the dose of the same statin is estimated to result in a 6% greater reduction in LDL-C [ 17 ]. Based on the average percentage reduction of LDL-C that they produce, statins are classified as low intensity (e.g., fluvastatin 20–40 mg, lovastatin 20 mg, pravastatin 10–20 mg, simvastatin 10 mg), which result in < 30% reduction in LDL-C, moderate intensity (e.g. atorvastatin 10–20 mg, fluvastatin 80 mg, lovastatin 40–80 mg, pitavastatin 1–4 mg, pravastatin 40–80 mg, rosuvastatin 5–10 mg, simvastatin 20–40 mg), which result in a 30%–<50% reduction in LDL-C, and high intensity (e.g., atorvastatin 40–80 mg, rosuvastatin 20–40 mg), which can result in a 50% or greater reduction in LDL-C [ 18 ]. In addition, red yeast rice extract (i.e., xuezhikang) is a moderate-intensity statin with pleiotropic anti-CVD effects; it is widely used in Asia and recommended by Chinese guidelines [ 17 , 19 ]. The lipid-lowering and CVD prevention effects and safety of xuezhikang have been validated by Phase 2 trials and the large-scale China Coronary Secondary Prevention Study (CCSPS). It should be noted that CCSPS is the largest randomised controlled trial that evaluated a lipid-lowering agent for the prevention of cardiovascular events in China, which provided important evidence for a lipid-lowering treatment in the Chinese population [ 20 ]. Xuezhikang has a favourable safety profile, with low risk for muscle and liver toxicities, and therefore can be considered in individuals with statin intolerance [ 19 ]. A meta-analysis of 27 randomised trials, encompassing trials of statin versus control and those of more intensive statin versus less intensive statin, showed that each 1.0 mmol/L reduction in LDL-C was associated with a reduction in the risk of major vascular events by around one-fifth [ 21 ]. The absolute benefit in terms of CVD prevention is expected to be more substantial in individuals at a high risk compared with those at a low risk: a 2.0 mmol/L reduction in LDL-C with statins was estimated to prevent major vascular events in approximately 1000 (10%) higher risk patients per 10,000 and in approximately 500 (5%) lower-risk patients per 10,000 [ 1 ]. A meta-analysis found that more intensive statins produced a further reduction of LDL-C of 0.51 mmol/L per year compared with less intensive statins, and this was translated to a 15% further reduction in the risk of major vascular events [ 22 ]. It should be noted that in this meta-analysis, the extent of risk reduction calculated for every 1 mmol/L reduction in LDL-C was similar across comparisons of more versus less intensive statins (risk ratio [RR] 0.72, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.66–0.78) and statins versus control (RR 0.79, 95% CI 0.77–0.81), and therefore it is possible that the effect is independent of which drug was used but dependent on the level of LDL-C reduction [ 22 ]. This was validated by another meta-analysis showing that the reduction in cardiovascular risk per change in LDL-C was similar across statin and non-statin therapies that act via upregulating LDL receptor expression [ 23 ]. In addition, the benefit of LDL-C lowering seems without boundary and even below the targeted level, further reduction in LDL-C can still result in reduction in the risk of CVD [ 24 ]. These findings constitute the evidence base for the push for high-intensity statin therapy to achieve the LDL-C goals in specific risk groups [ 25 ]. Treatment goals and initial treatment recommended by guidelines. The 2019 ESC/EAS guidelines for the management of dyslipidaemia and the 2021 ESC guidelines on CVD prevention in clinical practice recommend an LDL-C goal of < 1.4 mmol/L (< 55 mg/dL) plus LDL-C reduction ≥ 50% from baseline for patients at very high risk; for individuals at high risk, the LDL-C goal is < 1.8 mmol/L (< 70 mg/dL), along with ≥ 50% reduction from baseline [ 2 , 3 ]. Goals of < 2.6 mmol/L (< 100 mg/dL) and < 3.0 mmol/L (< 116 mg/dL) are proposed for individuals at moderate risk and those at low risk, respectively, but more evidence is needed to ascertain the benefits [ 2 ]. The main criteria that factor into the classification of very-high-risk or high-risk categories include history of atherosclerotic CVD (ASCVD), presence of comorbidities, renal functions, calculated Systematic COronary Risk Evaluation (SCORE), blood cholesterol levels and familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH). To achieve the LDL-C goals, the European guidelines recommend starting with a high-intensity statin, prescribed up to the highest dose that can be tolerated [ 2 , 3 ]. If the LDL-C goal is not reached with statin monotherapy, ezetimibe can be added; if with subsequent treatment intensification the LDL-C goal is not reached with the combination therapy, a PCSK9 inhibitor can be further added. Treatment recommended for older people (aged ≥ 70 or ≥ 75 years) is the same as that for the younger people, unless renal impairment or drug interactions exist. The recommendation for high-intensity statins at the highest dose possible is based on the aforementioned large volume of evidence from clinical trials and meta-analysis of statins; the main conclusion is that the benefit of CVD prevention is proportional to the extent of LDL-C lowering, and high-intensity statins are expected to be able to achieve the target percentage of LDL-C reduction [ 1 , 22 , 26 ]. However, emerging evidence of alternative treatment has not been taken into consideration for the recommendation of initial treatment option, and the inter-individual difference is also not considered; this will be discussed in the next section. The 2023 Chinese guidelines for lipid management utilise a risk assessment approach that is tailored to the Chinese population [ 17 ]. Individuals with established ASCVD are categorised into very high risk and ultra-high risk based on the presence of risk factors and number of ASCVD events; high-risk, medium-risk and low-risk are designated to individuals without ASCVD according to blood cholesterol levels, comorbidities and other factors. The treatment goal for ultra-high-risk individuals is LDL-C < 1.4 mmol/L (< 55 mg/dL) plus > 50% reduction from baseline, and for very-high-risk individuals this is LDL-C < 1.8 mmol/L (< 70 mg/dL) plus > 50% reduction from baseline, which echoes the goals for very high risk and high risk, respectively, in the European guidelines [ 2 , 3 , 17 ]. The Chinese guidelines also recommend an LDL-C goal of < 2.6 mmol/L (< 100 mg/dL) for medium- and high-risk individuals and propose an LDL-C goal of < 3.4 mmol/L (< 131 mg/dL) for low-risk individuals. Unlike the European guidelines, the Chinese guidelines recommend upfront use of moderate-intensity statins to achieve the LDL-C target. The recommendation is based on benefit-risk evaluation of high-intensity statins and moderate-intensity statins in the Chinese population. The pharmacokinetics of statins in the Asian population are different from those in the Caucasian population, with a markedly higher systemic exposure in Asians than Caucasians at the same dose of the same statin [ 27 , 28 ]. In line with this, several studies have indicated that in the Asian population a lower dose of statins may be able to produce a similar magnitude of LDL-C reduction compared with the dose required for non-Asians. In a meta-analysis, rosuvastatin 14.1 mg used in Asian individuals was estimated to reduce LDL-C by a similar degree on average as that at 40.0 mg in individuals from Western countries, and atorvastatin 18.9 mg in Asians was almost equivalent to atorvastatin 80.0 mg in the Western population [ 29 ]. The Management of Elevated Cholesterol in the Primary Prevention Group of Adult Japanese (MEGA) study showed that pravastatin at a low dose reduced the risk of coronary heart disease in Japanese patients by an extent comparable to that seen with a higher dose in Europe and the USA [ 30 ]. Furthermore, the China Intensive Lipid Lowering with Statins in Acute Coronary Syndrome (CHILLAS) trial showed that a more intensive dose of statin (20 or 40 mg/d atorvastatin, or equivalent dose of other stains) did not provide additional reduction in the risk of CVD compared with a moderate dose of statin (10 mg/d atorvastatin, or equivalent dose of other stains) in Chinese patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) [ 31 ]. Similarly, high-intensity statins did not provide additional benefits compared with usual care before elective percutaneous coronary intervention in Asian patients with ACS [ 32 , 33 ]. On the other hand, Asian patients are more prone to experience side effects when receiving statins. This was shown in the Heart Protection Study 2-Treatment of HDL to Reduce the Incidence of Vascular Events (HPS2-THRIVE) trial, in which the incidence of myopathy was three times higher in China than in Europe in the placebo arm, and this difference was further increased to 10 times in the niacin plus laropiprant arm, with all patients receiving simvastatin 40 mg with or without ezetimibe 10 mg [ 34 ]. Weighing the relatively limited benefit against the increased risk for side effects, the Chinese guidelines recommend moderate-intensity statins as the primary choice of pharmacological intervention to lower LDL-C. Furthermore, the 2023 Chinese guidelines for lipid management recommend combining a moderate-intensity statin with a cholesterol absorption inhibitor as a core strategy to achieve LDL-C goals when a moderate-intensity statin does not yield the desired effect [ 17 ]. These recommendations represent a shift from the conventional high-intensity statin–based treatment approach. Gaps Between Guideline Recommendations and a Real-World Scenario Although there is solid evidence from clinical trials to support the “highest-dose, high-intensity statins” initial approach to achieve LDL-C goals, the real-world situation is dismal. Only a small proportion of patients receive high-intensity statins, and the treatment effect is far from optimal. A US retrospective cohort study showed that high-intensity statins were initiated in approximately half of patients who were hospitalised for myocardial infarction in 2014, with persistence to ever-intensity statins in 63.8% of this patient group [ 8 ]. An EU-wide observational study revealed that 22% of individuals who received lipid-lowering therapy used high-intensity statins in 2017–2018, while moderate-intensity statins were most commonly used across all risk groups; among those who used high-intensity statins, 29% of individuals at high risk and 22% of individuals at very high risk achieved their treatment goals according to the 2019 ESC/EAS guidelines [ 9 ]. In a Swedish nationwide cohort study that included patients admitted for myocardial infarction between 2006 and 2016, 27% had LDL-C reduced by ≥ 50% at the first cardiac rehabilitation visit compared with baseline; among the patients who had LDL-C lowered by < 50%, 33% were treated with high-intensity statins and 56% with low-/median-intensity statins [ 10 ]. A cross-sectional study in China also highlighted the problem of inadequate lipid control in China: only 44.8% of patients with dyslipidaemia who received treatment between 2014 and 2019 had LDL-C ≤ 1.8 mmol/L (70 mg/dL), and among those with established ASCVD, 42.9% had LDL-C ≤ 2.6 mmol/L (< 100 mg/dL) [ 11 ]. There are multifaceted reasons for not using high-intensity statins or not achieving the LDL-C goals. First, therapeutic inertia among physicians and reluctance of patients to receive high-intensity statins may prevent the widespread use [ 12 ]. Second, treatment adherence is alarmingly low in the real-world scenario. Based on a retrospective claims analysis, it was estimated that the probability of continuing treatment with statins was < 54% at 1 year and < 25% at 5 years after first statin claim across patients with and without baseline ASCVD [ 13 ]. Although this is under debate, patients who receive high-intensity statins are more likely to have poor adherence [ 8 , 35 , 36 ]. Third, side effects are one of the main reasons for non-initiation of and non-adherence to high-intensity statins [ 14 ]. Statin-related toxicity appears to be dose dependent and high-intensity statins are associated with greater risk for muscle and liver adverse events (AEs) compared with moderate-intensity statins [ 12 , 37 , 38 ]. Likewise, the risk for new-onset diabetes increases with statin intensity, with a high-intensity statin carrying a 36% proportional risk increase over placebo according to a meta-analysis [ 39 ], and the risk is higher with intensive-dose statins than with moderate-dose statins [ 40 ]. Fourth, individual variability in response to the same statin at the same dose is great. For example, in an analysis of data from the VOYAGER database, the percentage change in LDL-C ranged from an increase to a > 80% decrease with atorvastatin 80 mg, and the same was observed with rosuvastatin 40 mg, while both statin regimens are expected to reduce LDL-C by ≥ 50% [ 15 ]. Underlying this individual variability are genetic variants, comorbidities, diet, and lifestyle, among other factors [ 41 , 42 ]. Given the variability, it would be sufficient to use a moderate-intensity statin in the high responders while in the low responders even the highest-dose, high-intensity statin would not achieve the desired effect. For an Asian population, although a moderate-intensity statin is now recommended, the achievement rates of the LDL-C goals are not adequate, which was demonstrated by the under-achievement of LDL-C goals among the Chinese population in the real-world setting [ 11 , 43 ]. Moderate-Intensity Statin Plus Cholesterol Absorption Inhibitor: Mechanisms and Clinical Evidence Efficacy/Effectiveness The treatment strategy of combining a statin with a cholesterol absorption inhibitor is built on the fact that blood cholesterol is derived from two main origins: cholesterol absorption in the gastrointestinal tract and cholesterol synthesis in the liver [ 44 ]. Dual targeting of the two routes is thus hypothesised to confer a remarkable reduction in blood cholesterol. Ezetimibe is a selective inhibitor of cholesterol transport protein Nieman Pick C1 like 1 protein (NPC1L1) [ 44 ] that primarily inhibits the uptake of cholesterol in the small intestine and may also inhibit hepatic cholesterol absorption. Ezetimibe, when used alone, can produce an approximately 19% reduction in LDL-C [ 45 ]. Adding ezetimibe to a statin is estimated to result in a 15% further reduction in LDL-C compared with a statin alone according to a meta-analysis [ 46 ]. In addition to greater LDL-C lowering, adding ezetimibe to a statin may further reduce the atherosclerotic plaque burden, although this remains controversial. In the Plaque Regression with Cholesterol Absorption Inhibitor or Synthesis Inhibitor Evaluated by Intravascular Ultrasound (PRECISE-IVUS) trial, a greater reduction in percent atheroma volume (− 1.4% vs − 0.3%) and a greater percentage of patients having coronary plaque regression (78% vs 58%) were seen with atorvastatin plus ezetimibe compared with atorvastatin alone in patients who underwent percutaneous coronary intervention [ 47 ]; however, such effect on atherosclerotic plaque was not seen in patients with FH in the Ezetimibe and Simvastatin in Hypercholesterolemia Enhances Atherosclerosis Regression (ENHANCE) trial [ 48 ]. Several large-scale clinical trials provide evidence on primary and secondary prevention of CVD with the addition of ezetimibe to a moderate-intensity statin (Table 1 ). In the Study of Heart and Renal Protection (SHARP), which included 9270 patients with chronic kidney disease and no known history of myocardial infarction or coronary revascularisation, simvastatin 20 mg plus ezetimibe resulted in a 17% proportional reduction in major atherosclerotic events (RR 0.83) compared with placebo over a median follow-up of 4.9 years [ 5 ]. In the Improved Reduction of Outcomes: Vytorin Efficacy International (IMPROVE-IT) trial, among 18,144 patients who had been hospitalised for an ACS, simvastatin 40 mg plus ezetimibe significantly reduced cardiovascular events, with an absolute risk difference of 2.0% at 7 years compared with simvastatin 40 mg (hazard ratio [HR] 0.936) [ 6 ]. A larger magnitude of CVD risk reduction was seen in patients with diabetes (absolute risk difference 5.5%; HR 0.856) and in patients aged ≥ 75 years (absolute risk difference 8.7%; HR 0.797) [ 6 ]. The effect of the combination on the prevention of cardiovascular events is also supported by real-world evidence: an analysis based on the Polish Registry of Acute Coronary Syndromes (PL-ACS) registry data showed that upfront combination therapy with a statin plus ezetimibe significantly reduced all-cause mortality compared with statin monotherapy in 1536 patients who were hospitalised for ACS (odds ratio [OR] at 3 years 0.526) [ 49 ]. A number of studies, each having a relatively smaller sample size ( n < 300) and a relatively short follow-up duration, compared a low-/moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe with a statin alone [ 50 ]. Based on two meta-analyses, a low-/moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe produced a more pronounced reduction in LDL-C than high-intensity statin monotherapy [ 50 , 51 ]. Recently, the medium-intensity statins with ezetimibe and high-intensity statins in acute ischaemic cerebrovascular disease (MESIA) trial, which was conducted in 382 Chinese patients with acute ischaemic cerebrovascular disease, showed that a significantly greater proportion of patients who received a moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe achieved their LDL-C target at 3 months compared with those who received a high-intensity statin (89.86% vs 70.77%, OR 0.273, 95% CI 0.106–0.705, p = 0.005) [ 52 ]. The Randomized Comparison of Efficacy and Safety of Lipid-lowering with Statin Monotherapy Versus Statin/ezetimibe Combination for High-risk Cardiovascular Disease (RACING) trial is the first large-scale, head-to-head clinical trial that compared moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe with high-intensity statin in the reduction of cardiovascular events [ 7 ]. Among 3780 patients with atherosclerotic CVD, rosuvastatin 10 mg with ezetimibe 10 mg was non-inferior to rosuvastatin 20 mg in the risk for the 3-year composite endpoint of cardiovascular death, major cardiovascular events or non-fatal stroke (9.9% vs 9.1%, absolute difference − 0.78%, 90% CI − 2.39–0.83); significantly more patients in the combination group achieved the LDL-C goal of 1.8mmol/L (70 mg/dL) at 1, 2 and 3 years compared with the monotherapy group [ 7 ]. Subgroup analyses in very-high-risk patients, diabetic patients, those with metabolic syndrome, patients after percutaneous coronary intervention, and those aged ≥ 75 years generated consistent results [ 53 – 57 ]. However, it should be noted that in the RACING trial, patients with documented ASCVD were excluded and patients were required to achieve LDL-C < 1.8 mmol/L (70 mg/dL); thus, the trial did not include patients with acute diseases. Future head-to-head studies in patients with acute CVD are warranted to provide evidence for the use of moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe in the acute setting. ROSuvastatin plus Ezetimibe Treatment for Target LDL-C goal Achievement in patients with recent ischaemic Stroke (ROSETTA-Stroke) was another trial conducted in Korea that evaluated moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe versus high-intensity statin, with a focus on secondary prevention in patients with recent ischaemic stroke [ 58 ]. The primary endpoint, LDL-C reduction ≥ 50% from baseline at 90 days, was achieved in 72.5% of patients in the rosuvastatin 10 mg plus ezetimibe group and 57.6% in the rosuvastatin 20 mg group (OR 1.944, 95% CI 1.352–2.795, p = 0.0003); the incidence of major vascular events was also significantly lower in the combination group (0.4% vs 3.5%, OR 0.101, 95% CI 0.013–0.805, p = 0.0091). Furthermore, results from an observational study favoured upfront use of the combination therapy over a statin alone: among 72,050 patients after drug-eluting stent implantation identified in a Korean nationwide cohort database, rosuvastatin 10 mg plus ezetimibe was associated with a significantly lower risk of cardiovascular events than rosuvastatin 20 mg monotherapy (11.6% vs 15.2%, HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.70–0.79) [ 4 ]. Three other real-world studies based on the Korean National Health Insurance Service database found similar results: a moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe was associated with a reduced risk of composite CVD outcome compared with a high-intensity statin alone in either primary or secondary prevention setting [ 59 – 61 ]. Safety and Treatment Adherence Evidence to date supports a favourable safety profile for moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe, with no particular safety concern. A meta-analysis concluded that treatment with a low-/moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe is associated with a lower aspartate aminotransferase level and a lower increase in creatine phosphokinase than treatment with a high-intensity statin, although another meta-analysis found only a difference in creatine phosphokinase [ 50 , 51 ]. In the RACING study, the incidence rates of new-onset diabetes and muscle-related AEs were numerically lower with rosuvastatin 10 mg plus ezetimibe than with rosuvastatin 20 mg [ 7 ]. Real-world evidence has also consistently showed that rosuvastatin 10 mg plus ezetimibe is associated with a significantly lower incidence of new-onset diabetes that requires medication than rosuvastatin 20 mg (7.7% vs 9.6%, HR 0.80, 95% CI 0.72−0.88, p < 0.001) [ 4 ]. These findings indicate that treatment with a moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe has a reduced risk for muscle AEs and new-onset diabetes compared with a high-intensity statin. In IMPROVE-IT, the AEs, including muscle AEs, were similar between the moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe and moderate-intensity statin groups during 7 years of follow-up; therefore, it could be anticipated that a moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe has a safety profile very similar to that of moderate-intensity statin alone, if not better [ 6 ]. The safety findings over a median 4.9 years’ follow-up in SHARP suggest that the risks for muscle pain, increased creatine kinase and other muscle and hepatobiliary system AEs were comparable between the simvastatin 20 mg plus ezetimibe and the placebo groups; as such, a moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe may carry muscle and hepatobiliary toxicity risks no greater than those of placebo [ 5 ]. Notably, treatment discontinuation or dose reduction due to intolerance was significantly less common among patients receiving rosuvastatin 10 mg plus ezetimibe than among those receiving rosuvastatin 20 mg in the RACING trial over a median follow-up of 3 years (4.8% vs 8.2%, p < 0.0001) [ 7 ]. Improved treatment adherence with moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe was also observed in a real-world study, in which rosuvastatin 10 mg plus ezetimibe versus rosuvastatin 20 mg was associated with a significantly lower rate of statin discontinuation (6.5% vs 7.6%, HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.78–0.94, p < 0.001) [ 4 ]. Why Do We Need Moderate-Intensity Statin Plus Ezetimibe as the Upfront Treatment Option As mentioned previously, the gap between guideline recommendations and real-world practice in the control of hyperlipidaemia is staggering. The armamentarium of LDL-C–lowering therapies has been expanding and we are now ready to move on to a “post high-intensity statin” era. It is imperative to revisit the question of what the initial treatment option for LDL-C lowering should be—one which must be more effective and secure, and foster better treatment adherence than high-intensity statins. Mounting evidence points to a moderate-intensity statin plus a cholesterol absorption inhibitor as a promising upfront treatment strategy; this combination is now endorsed by the Chinese treatment guidelines and the recommendation can be extended to other populations. We propose that a moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe may be considered as the initial treatment option to achieve the LDL-C goals across several populations (Figs. 1 and 2 ), for several reasons. Although to date the reported head-to-head long-term trial of moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe versus high-intensity statin was conducted in only Asian patients, the benefits can be estimated to be generalisable to non-Asians [ 7 ]. The SHARP and IMPROVE-IT trials, although comparing moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe versus either placebo or moderate-intensity statin alone, included predominantly non-Asian individuals and supported the CVD prevention benefits of a moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe in those individuals; subgroup analysis of IMPROVE-IT demonstrated consistent benefits across different races and regions [ 5 , 6 ]. In IMPROVE-IT, 51% of patients in the moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe combination group achieved the dual goal of LDL-C < 1.8 mmol/L (70 mg/dL) and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein < 2.0 at 1 year, and in the RACING trial, this percentage was 73% [ 6 , 7 ]; therefore, it can be estimated that the majority of individuals across different populations can reach LDL-C < 1.8 mmol/L (70 mg/dL) with treatment with a moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe. Moreover, given the individual variability in the response to statins, individuals carrying specific gene variants, not only present in Asian people but also in non-Asians, may have inadequate LDL-C reduction when using high-intensity statins [ 62 ]; these patients require alternative lipid-lowering treatment. Second, moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe tends to present a favourable safety profile, which can obviate the safety concerns associated with high-intensity statins [ 4 – 7 , 50 , 51 ]. The above-mentioned evidence supports low risks of muscle and liver toxicities, new-onset diabetes and any other AEs with moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe. This is crucial for specific patient groups for whom use of high-intensity statins should be approached with caution, such as older individuals and those with impaired organ function [ 63 ]. However, the combination is not recommended for patients with moderate-to-severe hepatic impairment due to increased exposure to ezetimibe and the risk for liver toxicity in these patients [ 64 ]. In addition, in individuals who are likely to have high systemic exposure to statins, such as Asian people and those with specific gene variants, use of a moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe can avoid the safety risk associated with high-intensity statins. Third, treatment adherence may be improved with moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe compared with high-intensity statin, as shown by a clinical trial and a real-world study [ 7 , 56 ]. As patients with dyslipidaemia may require long-term interventions to reduce the risk of CVD, adherence to treatment is of paramount importance and can be lifesaving. The envisioned lower risk of toxicity with a moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe may also improve adoption of and adherence to the treatment. Fourth, adding ezetimibe to a moderate-intensity statin is more cost effective than using a high-intensity statin [ 65 , 66 ], although the combination may increase financial burden for patients in countries where the prices for the two medications are high and they are not covered by insurance. Using a moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe early to prevent CVD is meaningful not only at the individual level for preventing CVD-related morbidity and mortality and cost savings, but also at the societal level for reducing the burden of CVD on the healthcare system and government. Conclusion Both statin intensification and combination therapy are optional initial treatments aimed at achieving LDL-C goals and cardiovascular benefits. With the continuous emergence of research on the combination therapy, it is reasonable for treatment concepts to diversify and update. If choosing statin intensifications, the 6% effect and special populations or certain racial/ethnic/geographic characteristics are worth mentioning. We tended to put forward a phenomenological viewpoint observed from several clinical trials, meta-analyses and real-world data on the use of moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe rather than determining a certain conclusion against current guidelines based on evidence-based medicine practice to replace the recommendation on the use of high-intensity statin. It is time to make a call on the “appropriate or localised” strategy. The combination of moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe may be considered in Asian individuals or other populations where appropriate. In conclusion, we propose that moderate-intensity statin plus ezetimibe, and not just highest-dose statins, be considered as the potential primary lipid-lowering treatment across populations. The promising standard of care may help address the worrying real-world situation of treatment non-adherence, inadequate LDL-C control and global increase in CVD.

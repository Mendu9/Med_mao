Introduction Cancer is the second most common cause of death for women in the US and the most common cause of death among women aged 45 to 64 years. Together, breast, cervical, and colorectal cancer account for more than 70 000 deaths among women in the US each year. 1 Screening tests exist for these 3 cancers and are associated with reduced late-stage diagnosis and cancer-specific mortality. 2 Yet, uptake of these screening tests is suboptimal and screening disparities remain. 3 , 4 , 5 6 , 7 Disparities in up-to-date (UTD) use of individual cancer screening tests are shown to exist across age, race or ethnicity, 6 , 8 rurality, 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 and income. 11 Of particular importance is the role of health care access, including stark differences in the use of colorectal, breast, and cervical cancer screening for those without health insurance compared with those with health insurance. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 There are numerous evidence-based interventions to increase access to and use of screening, but these generally target a single screening test. 8 , 10 , 16 , 17 Yet, the ubiquity of access barriers suggests that interventions to address access barriers broadly may improve uptake across all 3 screening tests. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 It is a crucial time for addressing screening uptake, as changes to national screening guidelines have shifted the ages in which women should initiate screening. While women aged 50 to 65 years have traditionally needed screening for colorectal, breast, and cervical cancer, recent guidelines have changed the start age for colorectal cancer to 45 years and have increased the strength of the recommendation for starting breast cancer screening at age 40 or 45 years. 24 , 25 Data consistently show lower uptake of colorectal cancer screening compared with breast or cervical, as well as a relatively slow uptake for those in the 45- to 50-year age range. 26 , 27 Although use of individual tests is well documented, patterns of screening completion across multiple tests for this age range is limited. This study sought to describe patterns of screening use across 3 routine cancer screening tests and to identify whether there are factors, especially potentially modifiable factors, associated with fully UTD screening use among age-eligible women. 6 , 10 , 28 , 29 Methods Data Source This cross-sectional study used data from the 2024 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance Survey (BRFSS), a nationally representative survey of the noninstitutionalized US population, including 49 states and 4 territories, with an overall 2024 response rate of 43.9%. Tennessee was not included in the 2024 data due to issues with data collection. Data were collected from January to December 2024 and analyzed from October 2025 to March 2026. Data were deidentified and therefore the study was determined to be exempt from review and the requirement for informed consent by the institutional review board at the University of Texas at Austin. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology ( 30 STROBE ) reporting guidelines for cross-sectional studies. Primary Outcomes Primary outcomes were UTD use of multiple cancer screenings using the US Preventative Services Task Force recommended intervals and modalities. Full definitions of UTD screening for this age group using BRFSS variables are provided in eTable 1 in 25 , 27 , 31 Supplement 1 . To include the population due for all 3 tests, our primary analysis was restricted to women aged 45 to 64 years at the time of the survey. We excluded those who were missing data for each screening or who did not know whether or when they had last completed each screening (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1 ). BRFSS asks about hysterectomy but does not assess reasons for hysterectomy or distinguish between total and supracervical procedures. However, prior analyses have suggested that 85% to 90% of procedures in the US are total hysterectomies ; therefore, we include women reporting prior hysterectomies and consider them UTD on cervical cancer screening. We evaluated the number of screening tests which a person was UTD with (0, 1, 2, or 3), as well as type (breast, cervix, colorectal, and specific combinations of tests where applicable). 32 , 33 Covariates Covariates included both sociodemographic and health access variables. Sociodemographic variables included age in 5-year age groups, self-reported Hispanic ethnicity, census region, county rurality, educational attainment, and self-reported household income. Race was self-reported by respondents who could select multiple races if applicable. This was transformed into nonoverlapping categories of those reporting only 1 race (American Indian, Asian or Pacific Islander, Black or African American, or White), those reporting any combination of races (multiple races), and those reporting any other race not listed (other race). Data on race and ethnicity are included in this study to reflect the socioeconomic construct of race which may influence perceptions of and access to cancer screening. Health care access variables included source of insurance (public, private, or no insurance), having a person or a group of doctors that you think of as your personal health care practitioner, having a wellness visit in the past year, and being able to afford all needed health care in the past year. Missing data were retained as a separate level for all variables. Statistical Analysis Survey-weighted analyses, following procedures for complex survey design recommended by the National Center for Health Statistics, were used to produce weighted means for all outcomes, sociodemographic variables, and health care access variables. We compared demographic characteristics for those with missing vs complete screening data using a χ 34 2 test and an α = .05. We then evaluated the proportion of women across each of 4 screening groups (0, 1, 2, or 3 completed screening tests) by key sociodemographic characteristics. We performed a series of regression analyses to identify factors associated with UTD cancer screening use. We included all sociodemographic and health care variables as factors in both univariable and multivariable models using a survey-weighted log-linked Poisson generalized linear model to produce relative risks of UTD screening, along with robustly estimated 95% CIs. To understand factors associated with these key screening patterns, we compared (1) use of any screening test (1, 2, or 3 completed tests) vs no screening tests, (2) use of all screenings (3 tests) vs some (1 or 2 tests), and (3) the use of all screenings (3 tests) vs no screening tests. 35 , 36 Finally, using the survey-provided weights, we scaled the survey population to the full US population of women aged 45 to 64 years to estimate the total number of individuals overdue (ie, not UTD) for each combination of cancer screening tests. All analyses were performed in R statistical software version 4.3.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing) using the survey package to account for complex survey design. 37 Results Screening Patterns Our final analytic sample included 68 111 women, reflecting a weighted US population of 36.2 million. Included respondents were mean (SD) 54.8 (5.8) years old, and most were privately insured (42 950 respondents [weighted percentage, 61.5%]). With regard to race and ethnicity, 1457 (1.9%) were American Indian, 1700 (5.5%) were Asian or Pacific Islander, 7052 (14.3%) were Black or African American, 1595 (2.6%) self-identified as multiracial, 1222 (3.6%) self-identified as other race, and 53 729 (69.6%) were White. Of the total sample, 7155 (81.0%) were Hispanic, 60 538 (81.0%) were non-Hispanic. Analysis of the 8537 excluded respondents (11.9%) who were missing cancer screening variables suggests that this group was more likely to be missing other demographic variables, including race and income and was more likely to be uninsured (8.2% vs 6.3%) but was otherwise similar to those who were included in analysis (eTable 2 in Supplement 1 ). Among the 68 111 included women, only 35 047 (50.3%) were UTD with all 3 recommended screening tests ( Table 1 and Figure 1 ). This was notably different by age, with 5023 (34.8%) of those aged 45 to 49 years UTD with all 3 tests compared with 12 190 (58.6%) for those aged 60 to 64 years. Just over one-quarter of women (19 389 women [28.8%]) were UTD with 2 screening tests, most commonly breast and cervical cancer screening ( Figure 1 ), and 8776 (13.2%) were UTD with only 1 screening test, most commonly cervical cancer screening. Factors Associated With UTD Screening In regression analysis, we first examined factors associated with any screening compared with having no UTD screening tests ( Table 2 ). The largest univariable associations were seen for access variables, including reporting a regular source of care (risk ratio [RR], 1.34; 95% CI, 1.28-1.40), having a wellness visit in the past year (RR, 1.29; 95% CI, 1.26-1.33), and having private insurance (RR vs no insurance, 1.27; 95% CI, 1.22-1.33). In multivariable models simultaneously controlling for both sociodemographic and access variables, we found factors related to access maintained a large magnitude of association with the same factors holding the largest associations: reporting a past year wellness visit (adjusted RR [aRR], 1.22; 95% CI, 1.19-1.25), a regular health care practitioner (aRR, 1.18; 95% CI, 1.13-1.23), and having private insurance (aRR vs no insurance, 1.09; 95% CI, 1.05-1.13). In contrast, several sociodemographic factors had a statistically significant but modest association, including higher screening use among those of Black race (aRR compared with White only, 1.02; 95% CI, 1.01-1.04), older age (aRR, 60-64 vs 45-50 years, 1.03; 95% CI, 1.02-1.05), and higher educational attainment (aRR for college graduate vs less than high school, 1.05; 95% CI, 1.03-1.08). We also examined, among those reporting at least 1 UTD screening test, factors associated with being fully UTD with all 3 tests vs partially UTD (1 or 2 tests) ( Table 3 ). In univariable analyses, having private insurance (vs no insurance) was the variable with the highest RR (RR, 2.26; 95% CI, 1.94-2.62), followed by reporting a regular source of care (RR, 2.05; 95% CI, 1.78-2.37), and having a past-year check-up (RR, 1.96; 95% CI, 1.79-2.14). In multivariable analyses, having a routine health care visit in the past year had the largest magnitude (aRR, 1.71; 95% CI, 1.57-1.86) followed by reporting a regular health care practitioner (aRR, 1.43; 95% CI, 1.24-1.66) and having private insurance vs no insurance (aRR, 1.43; 95% CI, 1.24-1.63). Older age, higher education, and higher income were also independently associated with higher use of all recommended screenings and being American Indian (aRR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.74-1.00), Asian or Pacific Islander (aRR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.76-0.98), or living in a rural county (aRR, 0.94; 95% CI, 0.89-0.98), were negatively associated with screening, compared with being White or living in an urban county, respectively, after controlling for other differences. Finally, we compared factors associated with being UTD on all vs no recommended screening tests (eTable 3 in Supplement 1 ). Both univariable and multivariable analyses showed patterns similar to the analysis of being UTD on all vs some tests, although magnitudes were generally larger. Specifically, in multivariable analyses we found that having all vs no screenings was twice as likely among those with a wellness visit in the past year (aRR, 2.03; 95% CI, 1.85-2.23), as well as 1.65 (95% CI, 1.41-1.92) times as likely among those with a regular practitioner, and 1.54 (95% CI, 1.32-1.81) times as likely among those with private (vs no insurance). Other characteristics, including older age, higher education, higher income, and residence in a northeastern state or an urban county, were all significantly associated with being UTD with all vs no screening. Total Need for Screening in the US Population Projecting our findings to the US population, we estimated 17.9 million women were overdue for at least 1 screening test ( Figure 2 ). Of these, 10.3 million (57.5%) were overdue for a single test, with 6.0 million overdue for colorectal cancer only (including 2.4 million in the recently added age group of those aged 45-49 years), 2.3 million women overdue for breast cancer screening only, and 2.1 million women overdue for cervical cancer screening only. The remaining 7.6 million women (42.4%) were overdue for multiple tests, with 4.4 million overdue for a combination of 2 tests and 3.2 million women overdue for all 3 tests. Within the population overdue for each individual cancer (these populations are overlapping, and individuals can be counted in multiple groups), a higher proportion of women were overdue for multiple screenings than for each single cancer screening alone ( Figure 2 ). Specifically, of the 12.6 million women overdue for colorectal cancer screening, 6.6 million (52.4%) were also overdue for breast and/or cervical screening; of the 8.5 million women due for breast cancer screening, 6.2 million (72.9%) were also due for cervical and/or colorectal screening; and of the 7.2 million women due for cervical cancer screening, 5.1 million (70.8%) were also due for breast and/or colorectal screening. Discussion In this cross-sectional study, by use of national data on self-reported screening behavior, we identified important patterns, including that approximately one-half of age-eligible women (17.9 million women nationally) weredue for at least 1 cancer screening test. Women were most commonly due for colorectal cancer screening, although there was substantial need across all 3 cancers. Importantly, half of those due for colorectal screening were due for at least 1 other screening. This proportion was even larger among those due for breast and cervical cancer screening—with 73% of those due for breast and 71% of those due for cervical cancer screening needing at least 1 additional test. This underscores the potential for bundled interventions that address multiple screenings and policy interventions that address root causes of these screening gaps. We identify 2 important populations in our study and demonstrate that engaging each of these populations in screening may require different approaches. First, we estimated around 3.2 million women in the US who were overdue for all 3 recommended screenings for their age group. This group was disproportionately uninsured and had low utilization of health care across multiple measures; engaging this population may require identifying ways to reach the population outside a traditional health care setting, facilitating insurance or care access, or improving funding for resources targeted to low income and uninsured individuals like community health workers, patient navigators, and Federally Qualified Health Centers. While this population is smaller and may be harder to reach, the benefits of interventions for this population are high, as engagement may offer the opportunity to screen for multiple cancers, as well as address other potentially unmet preventive care needs in a population that may be chronically disengaged from health care. 14 , 20 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 16 The second important group identified in this research is the 15.2 million women who were UTD with some, but not all, recommended screening tests. This group has sufficient health care access and awareness to have engaged in some cancer screening but remained overdue for 1 or more tests. Strategies for outreach to this population may importantly vary from those who are universally due for screening. While we identifed a large and important role of insurance and access to care, we also note that more than 80% of those UTD with some but not all screenings report having had a wellness visit in the past year, suggesting there may be missed opportunities for screening engagement within the primary care setting. Furthermore, our regression analysis found age, education, and income were all independently associated with being fully UTD among those with at least 1 cancer screening, suggesting targeted outreach among those newly aging into eligibility for colorectal and breast cancer screening, as well as addressing barriers related to knowledge and the direct or indirect costs of screening. 42 We found the largest gap for colorectal cancer screening, mirroring the findings of many other national studies. 8 , 13 , 17 The reasons for lower screening for colorectal cancer may be multifactorial, including the perceived cost, time, or discomfort associated with colorectal cancer screening relative to other screening tests, the fact that programs to improve colorectal screening are generally newer than their breast or cervical analogues, and differences in overall awareness of colorectal cancer risk relative to cervical and breast cancer. 6 , 10 , 28 , 29 43 , 44 It is important to consider how strategies to improve access to care broadly may positively impact the use of all cancer screenings. Expansion of Medicaid programs and subsidies for commercial insurance following the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act have been associated with increased cancer screening, with some variation across states and specific populations. While narrower in scope, the National Breast and Cervical Cancer Early Detection Program has also been associated with higher screening uptake. 41 , 45 , 46 , 47 However, recent federal and state changes in the funding for Medicaid, for subsidies for private marketplace insurance, and for outreach programs such as National Breast and Cervical Cancer Early Detection Program may increase vulnerability and place additional strain on existing safety net systems. In the long run, identifying large-scale policies to increase access to care (improving insurance coverage or primary care capacity) may be the most impactful strategy to meaningfully improve cancer screening and reduce excess cancer mortality. 48 , 49 , 50 In the shorter term, strategies to support screening for those without insurance or without a regular practitioner may become even more essential to fill what is likely to be a growing gap. These strategies may include combining innovative and relatively low-cost outreach strategies like mailed testing for colorectal and cervical cancer 19 , 51 , 52 or mobile mammography. 18 , 38 , 53 However, these programs can effectively improve health only if they are connected to accessible and timely follow-up for additional diagnostics and treatment. 39 , 54 Bundling successful strategies together could potentially improve the impact of these programs, but it could also create challenges for efficient delivery and follow-up by creating more complicated pathways based on each participants’ screening status. Research is needed to determine key elements of recruitment and intervention design to ensure programs are effective, efficient, and equitable. 55 , 56 40 , 57 Limitations and Strengths Our study has several limitations. First, we relied on self-report data, which may both overreport and underreport screening usage. While the BRFSS uses thorough and rigorously validated survey approaches, response rates were less than 50%, and this may introduce bias beyond that corrected for in nonresponse weighting. Second, screening start ages, test modalities, and recommended intervals have changed for all of these screenings over the past few years, making consistent reporting of these screenings in national surveys even more challenging. The BRFSS survey altered its cervical cancer question in 2022, resulting in a reduced reporting of screening compared with prior surveys, 58 and retained this wording in 2024, making it hard to assess changes in cervical cancer screening coverage over time. 36 As screening recommendations and the national health care landscape continue to change, it will be important to monitor these patterns to ensure priority-setting is well informed. Our study is strengthened by high-quality national data that assesses a wide variety of both sociodemographic and health care factors. 8 Conclusions In a cross-sectional study of US survey data, we found that nearly 18 million women in the US aged 45 to 64 were overdue for 1 or more cancer screening test. Nearly half of those overdue for 1 test were overdue for multiple tests, suggesting that cancer-specific interventions may be missing crucial opportunities to improve cancer outcomes overall. Engaging this large and heterogeneous population fully in cancer screening may require multiple strategies addressing care access, missed opportunities for screening in a primary care setting, and knowledge of changing screening recommendations. Developing strategies to address screening across multiple cancer screening types may offer the most impactful approach to reducing cancer mortality among women in the US.

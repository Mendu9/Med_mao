STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY Single-arm longitudinal design with high follow-up retention enabled robust cluster analysis of self-management trajectories. Cluster analysis using silhouette-optimised k=2 solution objectively identified distinct respondent profiles. Standardised Health Education Impact Questionnaire instrument facilitated cross-country comparability in three African settings. Complete-case analysis of paired data may introduce selection bias despite comprehensive missing data. Absence of a control group precludes causal attribution of intervention effects on self-management changes. Introduction The management of type 2 diabetes is recognised as one of the most difficult chronic diseases, particularly because of the complex demands of self-management, which often lead to emotional distress, feelings of loneliness and increased anxiety and depression. 1 4 Self-management, which relies mainly on patients and their families, is crucial in diabetes care, but studies focus more on patients than on those at risk. 2 5 The International Diabetes Federation simulated that Africa will experience the greatest increase in the prevalence of diabetes, rising from 24 million in 2021 to 55 million in 2045, mainly due to lifestyle changes and increasing urbanisation. Globally, around 6.1% of the world’s population is affected by diabetes, with a large proportion being at risk due to factors such as a high body mass index (BMI), poor diet and lack of physical activity. 6 7 In sub-Saharan Africa, many countries experienced a sharp rise in diabetes-related complications due to lifestyle changes and urbanisation putting future additional pressure on healthcare systems. 7 Given the very critical context for the epidemiology of diabetes in sub-Saharan Africa, it was crucial to find strategies to help people at risk of diabetes to improve their health at an individual level. 7 Self-management for diabetics or people at risk includes activities such as meal planning, exercise, blood glucose monitoring and managing hypoglycaemic and hyperglycaemic episodes. According to some studies, self-management among people at risk could significantly reduce the prevalence of diabetes and reduce the burden on healthcare systems. 2 Self-management was found to depend on a sufficient level of behavioural adaptation to be effective. 7 Self-management was defined by an individual’s ability to access, understand and use health information to make informed decisions. Current literature suggests that low self-management was associated with negative health outcomes such as high mortality, frequent hospitalisations and a decreased use of preventive care. 8 Overall, self-management was a key indicator of the quality of care for chronic diseases, as it enabled individuals to better understand and manage their health in several series. 4 8 9 10 11 In sub-Saharan Africa, self-management played an important role in diabetes prevention, especially given the rapid nutritional transition and limited access to healthcare services increases the risks of diabetes. Educational and community-based interventions, notably through digital platforms such as WhatsApp groups and peer educator outreach, have been shown to have positive effects on chronic disease self-management, thereby contributing to reduced diabetes-related morbidity. 12 13 Despite these findings, data remain scarce on the effect of such interventions in French-speaking African countries, particularly Burkina Faso, Mali and the Union of the Comoros, underscoring a pressing research gap. 10 14 This study aimed to evaluate the effect of educational interventions on multiple self-management dimensions (assessed via Health Education Impact Questionnaire (heiQ) domains) among at-risk individuals in these countries. 15 Cluster analysis was employed to identify distinct respondent profiles and explore heterogeneity in intervention responses over 6 months, informing tailored prevention strategies. We hypothesised that diabetes prevention educational interventions would improve self-management capacities (primary outcome: heiQ total score change), with greater effects among participants with higher baseline self-management profiles. 11 Materials and methods Setting This study was conducted in urban and peri-urban areas of Ouagadougou (Burkina Faso), Moroni (Comoros) and Bamako (Mali), representing major urban centres with high prevalence of diabetes risk factors. Health centres and ‘Diabetes Prevention Centres’ were purposively selected based on Santé Diabète’s national diabetes prevention programme mapping, identifying high-burden areas in partnership with patient associations, health professionals and national Ministries of Health. These specialised centres provide therapeutic education, nutritional counselling, physical activity promotion and psychosocial support, ensuring representativeness of urban at-risk populations. Prenatal recruitment targeted public referral maternity hospitals equipped for gestational diabetes screening. Recruitment methods varied depending on the intervention. With regard to the dissemination of videos on social media, eligible individuals from the 2021 screening databases who had given their prior consent were contacted by telephone and volunteered to participate in diabetes prevention centres. For the community peer education sessions, all eligible individuals present were identified by the investigators prior to the session. Finally, for gestational diabetes awareness during prenatal consultations, all eligible women present were recruited consecutively during waiting periods at the clinic. Selection therefore combined voluntary recruitment by telephone (digital intervention) and comprehensive registration (face-to-face interventions) depending on the operational context of each modality. Study design and participants This single-arm longitudinal before-and-after study was conducted from December 2022 to June 2023 in Burkina Faso, Mali and the Comoros, among people identified as being at high risk of developing diabetes. Using a pre–post design with no control group, the research aimed to assess the impact of educational and preventive interventions over a 6-month period. Participants were recruited from community screening initiatives and antenatal clinics. Non-diabetic adults completed baseline heiQ questionnaires immediately after risk screening (December 2022) and were reassessed 6 months later (June 2023) using the same heiQ questionnaires validated in French, administered again by trained interviewers. Eligibility was based on Santé Diabète NGO standardised screening protocol requiring presence of at least one of the following metabolic risk factors: BMI ≥25 kg/m² (overweight), waist circumference ≥80 cm for women or ≥94 cm for men (abdominal obesity), impaired fasting glucose ≥1.10 g/L (6.1 mmol/L; 110 mg/dL; pre-diabetes) or first-degree family history of type 2 diabetes; antenatal recruitment additionally included pregnant women or women of childbearing age with gestational diabetes risk indicators. Exclusion criteria comprised diagnosed diabetes (fasting glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L), current antidiabetic medication use, severe comorbidities precluding study participation (eg, terminal illness, uncontrolled psychiatric disorders), documented cognitive impairment preventing questionnaire completion, inability to understand or comply with study procedures as determined by physician assessment and pregnancy beyond 32 weeks gestation for the antenatal arm. Adults screened in the community had to be over 18 years of age, while all participants gave explicit consent to receive intervention questionnaires and follow-up contacts. Peer educators trained in the communities and maternal health facilities carried out the recruitment using two main strategies: dissemination of educational videos adapted to the local context via digital messaging platforms and direct outreach. Educational content was translated into relevant local languages to maximise accessibility and engagement. In the community screening, we started by identifying eligible individuals from lists compiled during outreach activities. To make sure our sample was diverse and fair, we then randomly selected participants from these lists using a computer-generated sequence. This method helped us avoid bias and better represent those at risk. On the other hand, recruitment in antenatal clinics was more straightforward: every eligible woman who came to the clinic and agreed to participate was included right away. Using these two different approaches allowed us to adapt to the unique situations of both community and clinical settings, improving how well our study reflects the populations and making the process more practical. Previous studies have shown that a sample of 50 individuals per intervention was sufficient to detect significant effect sizes. Sampling was therefore carried out by simple random selection from the screening lists, including only participants who had consented to take part. In total, 540 people at risk of diabetes were recruited for the baseline survey and 528 for the follow-up survey 6 months later, due to minimal attrition ( 15 16 figure 1 ). Self-management programme This study implemented a multifaceted self-management programme in Burkina Faso, Mali and the Union of the Comoros, countries burdened by increasing diabetes prevalence and limited healthcare infrastructure. Addressing such challenges requires culturally sensitive, accessible and scalable interventions to foster sustainable behavioural change and health empowerment. 17 The first intervention component used mobile health (mHealth) technology by broadcasting educational videos via WhatsApp in local languages ( Bambara , Mooré , Dioula and Shikomori ). These brief videos (3–5 min), covering glycaemic control, balanced diet and diabetes prevention, were delivered bi-monthly over 6 months. This approach effectively overcame geographic, linguistic and economic barriers, aligning with evidence supporting digital platforms’ role in enhancing health literacy and self-management in low-resource contexts. Drawing on Bandura’s social learning theory, this visual modelling intervention promoted observational learning and behavioural adoption crucial for chronic disease self-management. 18 The second component comprised peer educator-led awareness sessions targeting community members with personal or familial diabetes experience. Monthly group sessions (1–2 hours) employed culturally tailored materials and interactive discussions in local languages to encourage preventive behaviours such as weight management and physical activity. This strategy embodied principles of community empowerment and has been shown benefits in motivation, social support and stress reduction, factors strongly associated with improved diabetes outcomes. 19 The third component focused on pregnant women during antenatal visits, wherein peer educators provided targeted education on gestational diabetes risks and prevention through brief talks and simplified informational leaflets. Conducted at each visit over 6 months, this was grounded in behavioural change communication theory, emphasising adaptation to cultural contexts to enhance message comprehension and impact. Empirical studies are consistent with the effectiveness of antenatal education in improving gestational diabetes knowledge and preventive behaviours particularly within underserved populations. 20 23 Measures All variables were measured at baseline and after 6 months of follow-up through interviews conducted by trained investigators using standardised questionnaires. Socio-demographic, clinical, psychosocial and behavioural variables The socio-demographic variables assessed included age, gender, marital status and level of formal education. Clinical variables included BMI, family history of diabetes, heart problems and musculoskeletal conditions such as back pain and osteoarthritis or arthritis. In addition, psychosocial variables relating to knowledge of one’s own weight and height were collected. Finally, whether the individual engaged in physical activity was considered a behavioural variable related to lifestyle. Self-management variables The heiQ constitutes a robust and comprehensive instrument for the evaluation of patient education programmes across diverse health contexts. Widely validated and available in multiple languages, 24 the heiQ was developed through rigorous stakeholder engagement encompassing nominal group techniques to ensure its face and content validity for patients, clinicians and policymakers alike. 25 The instrument comprises 40 items distributed across eight psychometrically independent scales: health-directed activity, positive and active engagement in life, self-monitoring and insight, constructive attitudes and approaches, skill and technique acquisition, social integration and support, health service navigation and emotional distress, this last scale being reverse-coded to reflect psychological burden. 24 26 Each scale consists of four to seven items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1=strongly disagree, 2=disagree, 3=agree, 4=strongly agree). Scale scores are computed by summing item responses and dividing by the number of items, producing a standardised score ranging from 1 to 4. Higher scores indicate superior self-management capacities, except for the Emotional Distress Scale, where scoring is inversely related to well-being. Extensive psychometric evaluations have demonstrated the heiQ’s reliability and validity across heterogeneous populations and settings. The French version employed in this study was developed following the cross-cultural adaptation guidelines articulated by Hawkins and Osborne. 27 Primary outcome Change in heiQ total score (eight domains, 1–4 scale) from baseline to 6-month follow-up among stable cluster members. Secondary outcomes: domain-specific heiQ changes, cluster membership stability. Statistical analyses Descriptive statistics summarised participant characteristics, with means (SD) for continuous variables and percentages for categorical variables. Due to violations of normality and variance homogeneity across heiQ data, Welch’s robust analysis of variance was used to compare baseline versus 6-month follow-up scores for all eight heiQ scales within participants remaining in the same cluster over time. This analysis excluded individuals who migrated between clusters. Games-Howell post hoc tests were not applicable as comparisons were strictly within-cluster (baseline vs follow-up time points only). 28 Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s 29 d based on Elsworth and Osborne’s methodology, where values are interpreted as 0.2=small, 0.5=medium and 0.8=large improvements in self-management capacities. Absolute Cohen’s 16 d values in subsequent results indicate the magnitude of change; specifically, gains in heiQ scores from baseline to follow-up within stable cluster members (eg, | d |=0.45 reflects moderate improvement where follow-up score systematically exceeds baseline scores). This standardised metric facilitates comparison with other health intervention studies while quantifying clinically meaningful self-management progression across profiles. Missing data handling A total of 540 participants were enrolled at baseline across 3 countries (60 per subintervention×3 interventions×3 countries). At the 6-month follow-up, 12 participants (2.2% overall) from Burkina Faso were lost to follow-up: 3 from video channel arm (5.0%), 5 from peer educator arm (8.3%) and 4 from prenatal arm (6.7%). Complete case analysis was performed on the remaining 528 participants (97.8% retention) with matched baseline and 6-month heiQ data. Final sample distribution by country was Burkina Faso (n=168, 96.7% retention), Comoros (n=180, 100%) and Mali (n=180, 100%). Item-level heiQ data showed 100% completion across all 40 items and eight scales at both time points, with socio-demographic variables exhibiting ≥98% completion rates. No data imputation was performed due to negligible missingness (<2.2% overall) and excellent follow-up retention. Cluster analysis K-means using Ward’s hierarchical clustering method was planned for standardised z-scores of 6-month heiQ domain scores to identify homogeneous self-management profiles, with silhouette analysis to determine the optimal number of clusters. Cluster membership was to be recalculated independently at follow-up assessment. All analyses were performed in R (V.4.1.1) using RStudio V.2024.09.1 (‘Cranberry Hibiscus’). 30 Results Socio-demographic characteristics of participants Table 2 shows that the study population was mainly young adults under 60 years old, with a balanced BMI distribution. Women comprised 69% of participants, most were married (79%), and a significant proportion had completed secondary (42%) or university education (36%), with 14% having no formal schooling. Most were not professionally active (87%), but 69% reported engaging in physical activity. Clinically, 30% had a family history of diabetes, and prevalence of cardiovascular issues, arthritis or back pain was low. Most participants knew their weight (73%) and height (74%). Overall, the group was young, educated and presented some metabolic and behavioural risk factors relevant to diabetes self-management. Cluster analysis of self-management profiles Identification of self-management profiles Figure 2 and online supplemental figure S1 confirm the reliability and coherence of the clustering observed into two distinct profiles. Silhouette plots ( online supplemental figure S1 ) consistently showed the highest index for k=2 at both assessment times (baseline and 6-month follow-up), thereby identifying two natural participant clusters based on their self-management score. The parameter k represents the number of groups sought among participants: silhouette analysis objectively compares k=2 groups, k=3 groups, k=4 groups, etc and selects k=2 as the optimal solution because it maximises separation between the two profiles while ensuring strong internal cohesion within each group, representing a natural and stable division. The paired dendrograms ( figure 2 ) visually confirm this hierarchical division into two main groups (highlighted in red and blue). These consistent results, combining quantitative validation (silhouette plots) and visual confirmation (dendrograms), demonstrate the existence of two stable profiles of self-management skills: one with high abilities and the other with more limited abilities, observed consistently over the two assessment periods. Figure 3 shows standardised z-scores across eight heiQ dimensions for each cluster at baseline and follow-up, illustrating how self-management profiles evolved post-intervention. Cluster 1 participants consistently scored below average with little change over time, indicating limited progress in self-management skills, except for a modest improvement in emotional distress. In contrast, cluster 2 started with above-average scores and showed further improvements in areas like self-monitoring, skill acquisition, social support and healthcare navigation, along with reduced emotional distress. These distinct patterns reveal two experiences of self-management: one group with minimal change and another with substantial benefits, highlighting the intervention’s potential while indicating where additional support may be needed. Discussion This study evaluated the effect of educational interventions among individuals at risk of diabetes in three Francophone African countries: Burkina Faso, Comoros and Mali, using the heiQ as the outcome measure. The sample was classified into two distinct clusters using Ward’s hierarchical clustering method at baseline and at 6-month follow-up. The high follow-up rate, with 528 out of the initial 540 participants (98%), reflects strong participant engagement and the feasibility of this approach in these settings. In total, across both time points, cluster 1 comprised 440 individuals (111 at baseline, 329 at follow-up), while cluster 2 totalled 616 observations (417 at baseline, 199 at follow-up) ( 30 31 online supplemental table S1 ). Among the 528 participants with complete data across both time points, 45% (n=240) transitioned between clusters, suggesting intervention-responsive behavioural flexibility, while 55% (n=288) remained stable (cluster 1: n=100; cluster 2: n=188). Longitudinal heiQ analyses focused exclusively on these stable members to assess uncontaminated profile-specific trajectories, revealing distinct improvement patterns consistent with baseline profile characteristics. The persistence of this clustering pattern increases confidence in the robustness of the findings and provides a basis for examining the evolution of cluster-specific behaviours and outcomes over time. Significant differences between clusters in socio-demographic and clinical variables, such as age, sex, physical activity and family history of diabetes, are consistent with the key role these factors play in individuals’ capacity for self-management. Cluster 2, characterised by a more advantaged profile often comprising more physically active individuals, predominantly male and with a family history of diabetes is associated with better adaptation to educational interventions, aligning with findings from previous studies. 32 33 It is important to note that the recruitment of at-risk individuals varied according to the specific subinterventions implemented (social media platform, peer educator or pregnant women strategies). Recruitment for the social media and peer educator outreach was conducted through random selection from lists compiled during community screening activities, ensuring a diverse and representative sample of those at risk. In contrast, recruitment for the gestational diabetes education component focused on pregnant women at antenatal clinics, where all eligible and consenting women were directly enrolled. These differing recruitment methods correspond to the unique contexts of community and clinical settings, respectively, and should be taken into account when interpreting variations in intervention responsiveness and generalisability. The descriptive analysis of the profiles showed that participants in cluster 1 consistently scored below average across most heiQ domains, both before and after the intervention, with only modest improvements in emotional distress. This pattern may indicate inertia or lower receptiveness to the intervention, consistent with the ‘limited engagement’ profiles described in the literature, emphasising the need for enhanced psychosocial support. In contrast, cluster 2, which was initially better positioned across most domains, experienced significant improvements in several areas, including self-monitoring, skill acquisition, social support and healthcare navigation, along with a notable reduction in emotional distress. This aligns with recent evidence on the benefits of combining educational programmes with community or digital support to foster resilience and empower patients who are more ‘prepared’ for self-management, although causality should be interpreted cautiously due to the study design and potential confounders. 32 34 33 35 The redistribution of participants between clusters, with some moving toward profiles with better self-management, may reflect behavioural flexibility induced by the intervention. Nonetheless, the fact that most remain in the less receptive group highlights enduring barriers to adopting preventive behaviours and underscores the need for more intensive targeted support. The analysis revealed significant differences in the distribution of self-management clusters across countries ( figure 4 , p<0.001). These disparities may be influenced by unmeasured cultural and economic factors unrelated to recruitment methods, such as local beliefs and perceptions about diabetes risk, culture-specific dietary habits, community and religious support networks and varying access to health resources and economic constraints. The absence of these contextual variables in the dataset limits our ability to fully explain the differences between countries and to identify potential confounders. Consequently, cross-country comparisons should be interpreted with caution, highlighting the complexity of self-management behaviours across diverse sociocultural and economic environments. Future research should aim to incorporate these factors to better understand the challenges of diabetes prevention and self-management among at-risk populations in different contexts. Inferential analysis of heiQ scores across eight domains allowed quantification of the differentiated effects of educational interventions on the two clusters. Cluster 2, initially characterised by higher scores, showed statistically significant improvements in seven of the eight domains, with effect sizes ranging from moderate to large. Notable gains included self-monitoring, skill acquisition, social support, healthcare navigation and reduction in emotional distress. These findings suggest that populations with certain existing resources or capabilities may fully benefit from interventions, consolidating their progress, although causality cannot be definitively established due to the study design. In contrast, cluster 1, which comprised the majority of participants, exhibited more limited progress, with significant improvements in only two domains (self-monitoring and skill acquisition) and smaller effect sizes. This divergent trend indicates more substantial barriers related to resource access, psychological and social challenges that limit adoption of self-management behaviours despite exposure to the same interventions. Similar studies support these disparities in health education effectiveness, where patients with more favourable profiles benefit more rapidly and substantially, while vulnerable populations need targeted and tailored support to overcome barriers. 34 36 While the results suggest an impact of the educational intervention, caution is advised when interpreting causality. Given the single-group pre–post design without a control, factors such as selection effects and biases likely contributed to the observed improvements. Thus, this evaluation mainly highlights aspects linked to responsiveness rather than definitive causal effects of education alone. Its contextualised nature is valuable for understanding the complexities of intervention effects in real-world settings. Strengths and limitations This study has several strengths, including its multicentre design across three French-speaking African countries, enhancing external validity and generalisability. The use of the heiQ, a multidimensional and internationally validated instrument, enabled a nuanced assessment of psychosocial and behavioural aspects of self-management. Although the 6-month longitudinal follow-up was relatively short, it provided valuable insights into the evolving dynamics of self-management behaviours, while recognising that longer follow-up is needed to fully capture sustained changes and causal effects. However, important limitations exist. The before-and-after design on a selected population limits direct attribution of improvements to the interventions, especially without a control group. The short follow-up restricts evaluation of long-term effects. Reliance on self-reported data introduces potential biases. The female predominance (69% of sample), driven by antenatal clinic recruitment and higher female participation in community screening, limits generalisability to male at-risk populations. Additionally, cultural adaptation of the heiQ and lack of data on contextual factors like cultural, economic and social determinants limit interpretation of geographical differences and suggest a need for future research incorporating these dimensions. Overall, while the results are promising and offer novel insights into self-management variability, they should be interpreted cautiously with an emphasis on ongoing improvements in health education programmes. Implications for public health This study highlights the importance of tailoring diabetes prevention interventions to diverse self-management profiles among at-risk populations. Personalised strategies are needed beyond a one-size-fits-all approach, with targeted efforts to strengthen psychosocial capacities and community support for groups with low engagement and high behavioural risk. Integrating digital technologies with traditional education can enhance accessibility and resilience, while addressing technology access disparities. Training local healthcare professionals in these approaches and developing culturally and socioeconomically adapted monitoring tools are essential to improve primary prevention. Conclusion This multicentre study in Francophone Africa revealed that educational interventions could effectively improve self-management among populations at risk for diabetes. The interventions were associated with significant gains in key areas such as active engagement, health behaviours, self-care skills and reduced emotional distress in some participants. The identification of distinct patient profiles highlights the importance of personalised approaches, especially for those with limited engagement who need enhanced psychosocial support. These findings underscore the vital role of health education in diabetes prevention in African contexts and support the development of tailored, multimodal interventions for at-risk populations. Overall, the study provides quantitative evidence of varied self-management progress, emphasising the need for differentiated and inclusive strategies to maximise educational programme impact in resource-limited settings.

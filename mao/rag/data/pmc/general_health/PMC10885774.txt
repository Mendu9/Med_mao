Introduction Hypertension impacts a substantial proportion of the people in Sub-Sharan Africa (SSA), afflicting approximately 130 million out of the 650 million residents. Projections for SSA in 2030 paint a graver picture, with a predicted surge to 217 million hypertensive individuals diagnosed. 1 Notably, women face a higher susceptibility to hypertension in SSA. Over the last four decades, the prevalence of hypertension among SSA women rose from 27.2% in 1987 to 33% in 2019. 2 Strategic screening initiatives incorporate routine blood pressure (BP) measurements, public health screening programs, and health education interventions; however, although well- developed screening programs exist in some nations, others are severely under-resourced. 2 3 – 6 Addressing the dual challenge of hypertension and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) presents a compelling opportunity to enhance screening, diagnosis, and effective treatment strategies, particularly for pregnant women, within the distinctive geopolitical and cultural landscape of SSA. Failure to adequately address hypertension (whether through undertreatment, underdiagnosis, or inadvertent oversight) amplifies the risk of outcomes such as heart disease, stroke, kidney disease, and peripheral artery disease (PAD). This review synthesizes existing literature on standard treatments and clinical studies in SSA. The overarching goal is to define current state and highlight the urgent need for innovative diagnostic, treatment, and prevention strategies in SSA to address this critical health challenge. ( Figure 1 ) Underdiagnosing Hypertension and HDP in SSA Several studies indicate that only 50% of individuals within SSA are aware of their hypertensive status. This phenomenon underscores a labyrinth of barriers, including limited access to healthcare 2 , 6 – 8 , lack of healthcare infrastructure 9 , lack of screening programs 10 , demographic factors 11 , low health literacy 12 , the specter of stigma 13 and the impact of cultural beliefs 14 ( 15 Figure 2 ). In SSA, the primary deterrent for women seeking medical assistance is the confluence of diminishing access to healthcare and high poverty rates. The lack of healthcare access includes a shortage of healthcare professionals and low education levels. 16 Unlike conventional associations where urbanization correlates to an increase in income, SSA grapples with extreme poverty rates regardless of increases in urbanization. 17 The consequences of this atypical urbanization are evident in cardiovascular health, according to a longitudinal study following migrants from rural to urban environments in Kenya over 24 months. 18 These urban migrant workers exhibit a notable increase in body weight, electrolyte levels, and BP in comparison to their rural worker cohorts. 18 Opie et al 18 , 19 postulate that the surge in hypertension of urban migrant workers stem from sodium irregularities and genetic alterations within the renin-aldosterone- angiotensin system (RAAS). These trends persist in Malawi, where urban-dwelling women face a high prevalence of obesity and hypertension. 20 21 SSA nations face challenges in establishing and sustaining healthcare facilities due to infrastructural inadequacies, thereby impeding the populace’s access to medical care and hindering the implementation of hypertension screenings. Even if individuals manage to locate a nearby healthcare facility, the facility may lack the resources for diagnosing hypertension and HDP. 22 , 23 For instance, screening for proteinuria is necessary for preeclampsia diagnosis and in developing countries the only readily available measurement is dipstick testing. 24 However, the accuracy and reproducibility of dipstick analysis raise significant concerns 25 compared to the cost- prohibitive urinary protein-to-creatinine levels utilized within higher-income regions. 26 27 In the following section we will address the long-term consequences of hypertension and HDP in women of SSA. Left Ventricular Hypertrophy (LVH) Physiology A hallmark feature of hypertensive heart disease is LVH, which refers to the thickening and enlargement of the walls in the left chamber of the heart to adapt to the hypertensive pressure overload. LVH serves as a clinical prognostic marker for hypertension, and its presence elevates the risk of developing CVD by 10-fold. 28 The heart undergoes physiological LVH due to natural volume overload and estrogen-mediated cardiac remodeling during pregnancy, indicating that pregnancy and HDP may heighten the risk of CVD and potentially worsen LVH. 28 29 Current Research Recent strides in LVH research build upon the 1998 Italian Campania Salute Network registry, tracking 12,329 patients over 50 months. Initially, this study explored the risk factors for LVH, which encompass hypertension, hormonal factors, older age, obesity, diabetes, and smoking. 30 The Italian study reveals a higher prevalence of hypertension with a LVH diagnosis in women, emphasizing graver consequences for this cohort of patients. 30 Interestingly, women with LVH exhibit differences in cardiac structure and geometry compared to men, potentially elevating CVD risks. 30 Cardiac structure categories include concentric and eccentric LVH and women are more likely to develop eccentric LVH—dilation and enlargement of the left ventricle. 31 31 Postpartum monitoring is vital for assessing whether LVH persists or regresses in women with HDP. This monitoring typically extends 3–12 months postpartum. Compared to normotensive pregnancies, women with HDP one year postpartum show geometric changes in LV, greater left arterial size, lower mitral inflow velocity, and comparable systolic function. 32 An 8-year postpartum surveillance study notes the likelihood of heart failure (HF) and arrhythmias in women with a history of HDP, indicating persistent geometrical and functional changes beyond the postpartum period. 33 , 34 35 What is Known in SSA? Research regarding LVH in SSA primarily focuses on risk assessment and cardiac geometry. A study assessing 420 diabetic individuals in Cameroon identified pulse pressure and systolic BP increases as the best predictors for LVH. Many risk factors are on the rise in SSA due to changing lifestyles including increases in sedentary behaviors, cigarette smoking, and alcohol consumption. In one study in the Uganda, 61.6% of individuals report sitting or standing for no more than 10 minutes at a time per day. 2 , 7 , 36 Phanzu et al 37 demonstrates that 98.9% of individuals with LVH are regular smokers, and 96.6% consume more than two glasses of alcohol per day. Epidemiological transitions, such as shifts in population patterns and its relationship to poverty and food security, may also contribute to risk of LVH, although there is a gap in knowledge regarding how these factors contribute to LVH in SSA. Studies outside Africa suggest a link between diet and left ventricular ECG parameters, emphasizing the need for more research in SSA. 38 39 Research in SSA reveals a higher incidence of abnormal geometrical patterns in hypertensive women (87.6%). However, there are variations among studies, with some showing less obvious chamber diameter and wall thickness differences. 40 Contrary to the Italian study 41 , 42 , where eccentric LVH was prevalent in the population, Hamel et al 41 observes that concentric LVH is more frequent in Tanzanian women. Thus, women in SSA may be prone to abnormal cardiac geometry during LVH, though the data has limitations, such as not exploring pregnancy history. 40 40 Arterial dysfunction Physiology Arterial dysfunction involves an impairment in endothelial function, reducing nitric oxide and potentially raising oxidative stress or inflammation. This dysfunction can reduce blood flow or oxygen supply through the macro- and micro-vascular, causing structural damage to the vascular network. 43 The underlying causes may include atherosclerosis, thrombosis, or an increase in arterial stiffness due to elastin fiber degradation and collagen replacement in the arterial wall. 43 43 , 44 Current Research Diverse co-morbidities as risk factors for arterial dysfunction in women, include chronic hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, obesity, HDP, and menopause. These factors contribute to arterial damage, accelerate atherosclerosis, and increase the risk of CVD. 45 , 46 Additionally, patients with obesity have elevated Lipopolysaccharide-Binding Protein-levels, potentially due to chronic inflammation altering intestinal flora and gut permeability to LPS, which can destroy endothelial cells. 47 Women with preeclampsia exhibit increases in arterial stiffness at 7 weeks postpartum, as shown by increases in augmentation index, carotid-femoral pulse wave analysis, and carotid-radial pulse wave velocity. 48 Hormonal fluctuations during the menstrual cycle influence arterial elasticity via increases in the augmentation index and heart rate-adjusted augmentation index during the luteal phase. 49 Interestingly, hormonal changes during menopause increase the risk of arterial dysfunction in older women. 49 Holistic approaches, like hydrotherapy, show promise in mitigating arterial stiffness in HDP, highlighting the potential of non- pharmacological interventions in addressing this condition. 49 50 What is Known in SSA? In SSA, the prevalence of infectious diseases, particularly HIV, has significant cardiovascular implications. HIV-positive individuals in Zambia show malformations in the vascular endothelial lining, contributing to arterial dysfunction and stiffness. 51 However, studies on HIV in SSA often exclude pregnant women, and specific examination of endothelial function in pregnant HIVpositive individuals is lacking. 51 Urbanization contributes to increases in sedentary habits and the adoption of westernized diets rich in saturated fats, sucrose, and salt. Westernized diets induce metabolic and arterial dysfunction in both mice and humans, fostering conditions like hypertension, obesity, and diabetes. 51 Studies in South Africa reveal that in a cross-sectional study of 556 subject, 82% of individuals on a westernized diet had hypertension, 81% had diabetes, and 83% had obesity. 52 , 53 Study in Ghanaians reveals a similar trend. 54 55 Some studies in SSA explore the relationship between HDP and arterial stiffness. In South Africa, comparisons between 36 post-partum preeclamptic women and 86 post-partum normotensive women highlight that the preeclamptic women have a significantly higher pulse wave velocity for up to one month after delivery, and elevations in their BP were seen for up to one year after delivery. This study is consistent with a longitudinal study in Norway 56 , where elevations in BP can persist for up to six months, and arterial disturbances lasts for up to three years postpartum. These findings underscore the long-term cardiovascular consequences of preeclampsia through arterial dysfunction. 57 Heart failure (HF) Physiology HF is the inability of the heart to supply sufficient blood flow to meet the metabolic needs of the body, which affects over 64 million people globally. HF stems from diverse causes such as cardiac ischemia, hypertension or diabetes resulting in diminished cardiac efficiency due to primarily, pressure overload or myocardial cell loss. 58 58 , 59 Current Research Common atypical HF symptoms in women often lead to a diagnoses delay. Post-menopause increases the risk of HF presenting recognizable symptoms, yet challenges persist in medication access, with a higher likelihood of adverse reactions and lower prescription adherence, leading to hospitalizations. 60 , 61 Angiotensin receptor blockers, rather than angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, appear to offer better survival rates for women who can tolerate them. 62 Treatment disparities extend to device therapies; cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) in women correlates with delaying HF progression, mortality, and hospitalizations but carries a higher risk of adverse events like pneumothoraxes. 63 These variations to drug responses and CRT underscore the importance of incorporating sex-specific considerations into HF therapy. 64 , 65 66 , 67 Although clinical studies often exclude pregnant women for ethical reasons, comprehending pregnancy’s impact on cardiovascular health is crucial. Pregnancy induces cardiac hemodynamic changes that can exacerbate existing cardiac conditions or trigger new diseases. Peripartum cardiomyopathy (PPMC), a type of HF with decreased ejection fraction (EF), is on the rise with a global incidence ranging from 1 in 102 to 1 in 20,000. Mortality rates range from 2% to 50%, with PPMC typically occurring in the last trimester, the first 6 months postpartum, or after an abortion. 68 Although the etiology of PPMC remains unclear, 68 a study in Scotland revealed that women with a history of HDP are prone to PPMC (53.8% of women experiencing PPMC had HDP). 68 Children born to women with PPMC have a fivefold increase in mortality, suggesting a potential role for genetic predisposition in PPMC susceptibility. 69 69 HF treatment strategies during pregnancy involve careful medication management. Digoxin, diuretics, or hydralazine may be taken with caution. Beta-blockers are an option for severe conditions but may pose a risk of IUGR or preterm birth. Most other common treatments for HF are contraindicated due to their teratogenic effects. 70 Ongoing research is exploring alternative treatment strategies for managing HF risks during pregnancy including diet, exercise, and stress management. Omega-3 fatty acids (n-3 FA) play a positive role in maternal-infant health, but maternal consumption of n-3 FA is often inadequate. 70 Diets rich in n-3 FA may reduce the risk of hypertension and HF by improving left ventricular ejection fraction, restoring arterial compliance, influencing autonomic tone, and reducing electrical excitability in the plasma membrane. 71 – 73 74 – 76 What is Known in SSA? HF is a growing public health challenge in SSA, constituting 47% of the region’s health burden from 1990 to 2017. Interestingly, HF is significantly more prevalence in SSA and other low- income countries as infectious diseases, urbanization, aging, and genetic predisposition contribute significantly. 77 77 – 79 HF in SSA can arise from infectious diseases such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis (TB), HIV, or Chagas disease, which can lead to rheumatic heart disease (RHD), cardiomyopathies, and pericarditis. Notably, RHD affects 59.7% of women and girls in SSA. The VALVAFRIC study shows that 40% of those with RHD will experience HF later in life. 80 , 81 80 The escalating prevalence of HF in SSA intricately relates to rapid urbanization and poverty in the region, driven by Westernized diets, smoking, and sedentary lifestyles, as previously discussed. Age also contributes to increasing HF rates, with older age being a common risk factor for HF during pregnancy in Kenya 77 and South Africa. 82 Genetic factors, like increases in myosin binding protein C mutations, may play a role in the susceptibility to HF, and the physical demands of pregnancy accentuate these influences. 83 The ongoing Partnerships in Congenital Heart Disease in Africa initiative is exploring the genetic diversity in SSA to better tailor healthcare approaches for HF and cardiovascular disease. 84 85 Kidney Disease Physiology Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is a prevalent condition affecting approximately 8–16% of the global population. The reduction in kidney function in CKD often occurs in association with various underlying conditions, including HF, hypertension, diabetes, nephritis, and immune system disorders. 86 The etiology of CKD, irrespective of its underlying cause, often involves a progressive and irreversible loss of functional nephrons. 87 , 88 Over time, this loss of nephrons contributes to a decline in overall renal function. 87 , 88 87 , 88 Current Research In clinical practice, early identification of CKD using biomarkers is vital for preventing further kidney function deterioration. Glomerular filtration rate (GFR), serum cystatin C, creatinine, and albuminuria are conventional indicators of kidney function. Recent research suggests possible new markers, including podocalyxin, podocin, and neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) offer additional insights into early CKD stages. 89 NGAL is a particularly valuable early indicator of CKD as it rises before creatinine levels and can be used to monitor kidney function in the setting of preeclampsia. 90 , 91 These advancements in biomarker identification not only improve CKD screening but also pave the way for timely intervention and management, ultimately enhancing outcomes for individuals at risk of kidney disease. 91 , 92 CKD significantly impacts pregnancy outcomes, but pregnancy can also unveil suboptimal kidney function. During normal pregnancy, elevations in maternal sodium retention and GFR by 50% act as a stress-test for the kidneys and may reveal CKD. 93 Diagnosing CKD amid pregnancy presents hurdles owing to physiological changes and common symptomatic patterns, such as edema and proteinuria, which are prevalent in both pregnancy and CKD. 94 Additionally, pregnancy or HDP may obscure CKD diagnostic assessments, such as serum creatinine levels and hypertension and this may delay management of this condition. 95 Furthermore, women with a history of pregnancy complications, like HDP, face higher risks of developing long-term CKD later in life, highlighting the interconnectedness of these conditions. 95 This interlinking relationship between the cardiac and renal abnormalities is aptly demonstrated in the cardiorenal syndrome. 96 97 What is Known in SSA? CKD is on the rise in SSA, approximately 13.9% of women in a cross-sectional study of 8,000 participants aged 40–60 have CKD. Pregnancy, according to the International Society of Nephrology, stands out as the most common cause of CKD in low- and middle-income countries, possibly due to inadequate perinatal care or inadequate management of pregnancy complications including HDP, obstetric hemorrhage, and sepsis. 98 For example, in Nigeria 57% of mothers with kidney complications do not receive adequate treatment as they are referred for treatment late. 99 – 101 Additionally, anemia is common among pregnant women in SSA, increasing the likelihood of severe kidney damage in women who experience sepsis or obstetric hemorrhage. 102 An Egyptian hospital-based study suggests a link between cesarean section and CKD, emphasizing the importance of comprehensive research and clinical understanding of CKD in pregnancy in SSA. 103 104 HIV-related kidney disease is another prominent cause of CKD in SSA. Prevalence rates of CKD among patients with HIV exhibit a remarkable spectrum of diversity among nations, with South Africa recording a relatively modest 6%, while Uganda reports an astonishing range of 25–48%. A study in Zimbabwe demonstrates that 44.7% of CKD patients have severe immunodeficiency upon admission, with a concerning 49.2% mortality rate in a one-year follow-up, highlighting the critical need for improving understanding and management. 105 106 Environmental toxins, including contaminated water, air pollution, and pesticides, contribute significantly to CKD in SSA. Genetic predisposition plays a role in CKD, with the Apolipoprotein L1 (APOL1) gene as a risk factor for CKD, with 13% of African Americans carry the APOL1 gene in the United States. 107 In Cameroon, the frequencies of APOL1 coding alleles G1 and G2 are 8.7% and 8.9%, respectively. 108 However, the small study population in this context did not provide sufficient power to assess CKD’s association with APOL1. 109 The prevalence limitations of the APOL1 gene and its link to CKD in SSA emphasize the necessity for further investigation to target effective treatment strategies. 109 Peripheral Arterial Disease (PAD) Physiology Hypertension is a risk factor for PAD, which affects 236 million adults worldwide and refers to the narrowing branches of the aorta affecting the lower limbs, upper limbs, and renal circulation. PAD may develop from atherosclerotic, aneurysmal, or inflammatory blockage of the arteries. 110 , 111 111 , 112 Current Research Diagnosis of PAD involves assessing ankle-brachial index (ABI) by comparing the ankle BP to the arm BP using BP cuffs and a doppler ultrasound. Debate surrounds the appropriate ABI cutoffs for diagnosing PAD in women, as women may have higher ABI values than men due to relatively smaller arteries, which may delay diagnosis. 113 Women with PAD may experience worse functional impairment and quality of life, as they may have significant pain with walking. 113 – 115 Data from the Jackson Heart Study shows a significant association between smoking and PAD particularly in African Americans. 116 , 117 Other studies suggest eating saturated fats, sodium, and sugars can increase the risk of PAD 118 while exercising can reduce risks for PAD. 119 Although PAD is not common during pregnancy, HDP is a risk factor for acquiring PAD later in life. 120 121 , 122 What is Known in SSA? Compared to the Western world, PAD is often diagnosed in younger women in SSA. A study in northern Nigeria and Uganda shows that diabetes could be an important risk factor for PAD in young SSA women. 121 In Uganda, 63.7% of women with diabetes have PAD, and obesity, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia are common co-morbidities. 123 Additionally, smoking appears to increase the risk of PAD in the women of Uganda. 124 Taken together, the risk factors for PAD in SSA are known, but there is a fairly large gap in the literature about treatments and preventative strategies, underscoring the need for additional investigations. 124 Stroke Physiology Stroke, a significant complication of hypertension, is an infarction or lesion in the brain resulting in cell death due to hypoxia. In 2019, 6.6 million people died from stroke, including 3.3 million deaths are from ischemic stroke, 2.9 million from intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), and 0.4 million from subarachnoid hemorrhage. 125 125 Current Research Traditional risk factors for stroke include hypertension, diabetes, smoking, hypercholesterolemia, and older age. Additionally, the physiological changes in pregnancy increase the risk of stroke, driven by factors like hormonal fluctuations, blood volume increases, and cardiovascular adaptations. 126 , 127 The risk extends from pregnancy into the postpartum period, surpassing that of non-pregnant individuals of similar ages. 128 Later in life, women with a history of preeclampsia have a 4–5-fold increase in stroke risk. 129 130 Stroke management centers using medical interventions attempt to restore blood flow and minimize brain damage. The sole FDA-approved drug to treat strokes is tissue plasminogen activator, which can dissolve clots if administered within 4.5 hours of symptom onset. 131 Beyond this window, thrombectomy may be performed to physically remove the clot, but treatment options are limited, emphasizing a need for further research. 132 132 What is Known in SSA? Insufficient research exists to comprehensively grasp the epidemiology and outcomes of stroke in SSA. Nigeria reports a prevalence rate of 1,460 per 100,000 individuals. A Durban hospital study notes a high mortality rate of 58.6% for stroke patients. 133 Intriguingly, stroke, particularly ICH 134 , may disproportionately affect SSA women, but data quality issues undermine reliability. 125 , 135 Noteworthy is the younger age of stroke onset in SSA, with a mean age of 57 136 in comparison to the global average of 66. 137 Genetic factors and differing disease profiles, including HIV, and TB are potential contributors to the early onset of stroke in SSA. 138 Additionally, “The Southern African Stroke Prevention Initiative” reports a stark awareness gap about the increased risk of stroke for people with hypertension. 139 – 141 Moreover, little is known about the relationship between HDP and stroke in SSA, necessitating further research. 142 HDP Negatively Impact Women and Babies Impacting 1 out of every 10 pregnancies, HDP represent multisystem disorders encompassing chronic or gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, and eclampsia. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) defines chronic hypertension as a diagnosis of hypertension prior to or in the first 20 weeks of pregnancy whereas gestational hypertension, preeclampsia and eclampsia can present after 20 weeks of gestation ( 143 Figure 3 ). Preeclampsia symptoms often include one or more of the following categories: (1) proteinuria, or (2) other features of end-organ failure such as acute kidney injury, visual impairment, epigastric abdominal pain, liver dysfunction, headaches, or decreases in platelet counts, or (3) uteroplacental dysfunction such as stillbirth or fetal growth restriction. 144 145 HDP significantly affect both pregnant women and babies. Maternal risks include liver, kidney, and cardiovascular complications, with studies indicating long-term implications such as increased risk of cardiovascular disease later in life. For infants, risks encompass preterm birth, IUGR, placental abruption, and stillbirth, often requiring intensive care and prolonging hospitalization. 146 , 147 The diagnosis of HDP amplifies the likelihood of adverse metabolic outcomes, improper growth, neurological issues, cardiovascular problems, and congenital heart defects in offspring. Long-term effects on children exposed to maternal HDP involve lower cognitive abilities, mental disorders, neurodevelopmental problems, and increased risks of CVD and metabolic disorders. 148 – 150 151 , 152 The diagnosis of HDP amplifies the risk of offspring developing adverse metabolic outcomes, improper growth and development, neurological, and cardiovascular outcomes. Notably, neurological issues are at higher rates of developmental delays in babies born to HDP mothers. 153 – 156 Additionally, maternal HDP elevates the likelihood of offspring developing conditions like autism spectrum disorder by 1.4-fold and attention-deficit-hyperactivity disorder by 1.3-fold, signifying a profound and far-reaching impact on their health and development. 151 151 Hypertensive Disorders of Pregnancy (HDP) Physiology Pregnancy presents a major stress test for the cardiovascular system, constituting a dynamic process necessitating diverse maternal adaptations to establish successful uteroplacental circulation with escalating metabolic demands for fetal growth and development. These physiological changes in the maternal cardiovascular system manifest rapidly within the initial six weeks of gestation, encompassing a decrease in mean arterial BP and an augmentation in arterial vasodilation, glomerular filtration rate, stroke volume, cardiac output, and arterial vasodilation. 157 Patel et al 157 propose that a persistent renal nerve tone contributes to the increase in renal sodium reabsorption and stroke volume, perpetuating a vicious cycle that over activates the RAAS. The escalation in cardiac output may emanate from increases in stroke volume or the production of hormones in the placenta or uteroplacental circulation, acting as an arteriovenous shunt. 158 Undoubtedly, the hemodynamics of pregnancy are intricate, and any failure in these adaptive mechanisms during pregnancy significantly contributes to cardiovascular complications, including HDP. 159 159 Placental dysfunction is pivotal in the pathophysiology of HDP. Shallow trophoblast invasion into the decidua results in abnormal blood vessel development, reducing blood flow to the placenta. This triggers a cascade of events, exacerbating the disorder and contributing to oxidative stress, pro-inflammatory cytokines, mitochondrial dysfunction. 160 Increasing oxidative stress and inflammation in HDPs contributes to an imbalance in mitochondrial mechanisms attempting to counteract ROS. 161 , 162 This imbalance actively fuels inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and organ damage. Endothelial dysfunction causes clotting abnormalities and increases blood vessel permeability, which may lead to edema or organ damage. 161 , 162 However, the exact series of events and mechanism of pathophysiology of HDP remains elusive. 160 Current Research The imbalance in angiogenic factors within HDP disrupts proangiogenic (e.g., soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1, sFlt-1) and antiangiogenic (e.g., vascular endothelial growth factor, VEGF) elements. Elevations in sFlt-1 inhibits VEGF, impeding blood vessel growth and vascular function. 163 This imbalance, altering nitric oxide levels, leads to heightened BP, a potential contributor to HDP. 163 Pavastatin treatment in HDP rats increase nitric oxide levels, effectively preventing hypertension and modestly improving BP levels, which may be a promising treatment for humans. 163 An HDP study shows the upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNFα, IL- 1B, INFy), revealing that cytokine inhibitors or blockers could contribute to decreasing the severity of HDP. 164 165 , 166 Family history and DNA methylation at specific sites (CpG site 43 and CpG site 26) play roles in HDP. Specifically, DNA methylation at CpG site 26 may play a role in the development of preeclampsia. Addressing histone modification, ascorbic acid has the potential to reverse alterations in the histone modification of the angiotensin-converting enzyme 1 gene, offering a preventive avenue against hypertension. 167 168 Evaluations using shear-wave elastography indicate placental stiffness in preeclamptic women, possibly attributing to elevations in fibrosis via excessive collagen I deposition. The precise link between fibrosis and preeclampsia remains elusive; however, it may hinder trophoblast proliferation and invasion, exacerbating HDP. 169 , 170 Additionally, scanning electron microscopy observations highlight distinct differences: placental tissue from normotensive mothers displays a uniform surface texture, while those with HDP exhibit tissue surface swelling. 170 Suggesting, that the placenta’s structure is highly relevant to HDP states. 171 Managing HDP involves antihypertensive medications and prophylactic measures like magnesium sulfate for seizure prevention, particularly in cases of eclampsia. Low-dose aspirin reduces preeclampsia risk, in certain demographics of non-Hispanic white women, according to a study involving 3,135 participants. 172 However, understanding its effects across diverse ethnicities remains enigmatic. 173 What is Known in SSA?: Maternal Outcomes Maternal mortality rates are unacceptably high in SSA in comparison to peer countries. Particularly of interest is the country of Chad, which reports 1,140 deaths per 100,000 live births in 2017, the highest maternal mortality rate in SSA. In comparison, the maternal mortality rate across SSA is 542 deaths per 100,000 live births, while peer countries like Egypt is 52 deaths per 100,000 and South Africa is 88 per 100,000. 174 The maternal mortality rate in the United States, which is among the worst in the Western world, is 28 deaths per 100,000 live births. 175 , 176 177 However, the maternal mortality rate in SSA may be higher than reports indicate, as maternal deaths commonly occur after discharge from a healthcare facility and the cause of death is often missing. 178 179 Gestational hypertension affects nearly half (49.8%) of pregnancies in Africa, based on a study involving 854,304 women from 1997 to 2017. Africa has the highest prevalence of gestational hypertension among adults aged 25 and above. 180 Preeclampsia prevalence in SSA varies between 1.8% and 16.7% and significantly contributes to maternal mortality rates. 180 A comprehensive meta-analysis across Nigeria, Ethiopia, South Africa, and Sudan, covering 25,789 pregnancies, underscores that women in SSA with prior preeclampsia or eclampsia face a heightened risk of recurrence in subsequent pregnancies. 181 182 HDP is the second leading cause of maternal mortality in SSA (22.1% with a 95% CI of 19.9%- 24.2%). Anemia is the most common pregnancy complication in SSA, which may escalate the risk for both preeclampsia and eclampsia. 183 Multiple studies from Ethiopia and Sudan consistently confirm the correlation between anemia and the development of preeclampsia oreclampsia. 183 184 – 186 Key risk factors for HDP encompass underutilization of antenatal care, premature membrane rupture, extremes in maternal age (below 20 or over 35 years), malaria, HIV, and HDP history itself. Within SSA, ambient temperature emerges as an intriguing factor influencing HDP likelihood. 187 A study involving 7,986 women in South Africa reveals that higher early pregnancy temperatures (23°C compared to 18°C) increase the risk of preeclampsia, eclampsia, and HELLP syndrome. 188 Interestingly, an elevation in temperatures during later stages of pregnancy has a protective effect against these conditions. 188 188 What is Known in SSA?: Fetal Outcomes Babies born to mothers with HDP in SSA face various risks, such as preterm birth, IUGR, placental abruption, and stillbirth. These complications often necessitate intensive care, prolong hospitalization, and can lead to adverse metabolic, neurological, and cardiovascular outcomes, along with congenital heart defects. 148 – 150 The prevalence of preterm birth in low- to middle-income countries in SSA exhibits a wide range, spanning from 2.4% to 49.4%. 153 – 156 One study in Ghana involving 388 women with HDP emphasizing concerning statistics: 6.8% of babies were stillborn, 3.8% faced early neonatal death, and perinatal death affected 10.6% of cases. Additionally, IUGR occurred in 6.3% of babies, while 24.7% had low birth weight. 187 Furthermore, Ghana faces challenges in the availability of crucial medications for managing HDP. Shortages of anti- hypertensive drugs and magnesium sulfate, the primary choice for managing HDP, persist in health facilities, with only 55% of surveyed facilities in possession of magnesium sulfate. 189 In some cases, the only available treatment strategy for HDP is early delivery, which places premature babies at a risk for respiratory distress syndrome due to their underdeveloped lungs, leading to breathing difficulties. 189 Underscoring the limitations to medications directly impact HDP in SSA. 190 Summary and Conclusion Our knowledge of hypertension in women’s health has substantially advanced over the past decade. However, within SSA, the translation of this knowledge into clinical practice for therapeutic and preventative strategies has been notably lacking. Significant knowledge gaps persist regarding the repercussions of hypertension in SSA, largely due to barriers including limited access to healthcare , lack of healthcare infrastructure 9 , lack of screening programs 10 , demographic factors 11 , low health literacy 12 , the specter of stigma 13 and the impact of cultural beliefs. 14 These barriers may contribute to the exacerbation and progression of hypertension and HDP, ultimately leading to high incidence of CVD, organ damage, or death. To further advance the field, forthcoming investigations should delve into public health, clinical and basic science research to elucidate underlying mechanisms, pinpoint risk factors, and identify treatment targets in hypertension, with the aim of bolstering CVD prevention in SSA. 15

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS We examined multidimensional preferences of health promotion programmes. Stakeholders prioritised different attributes of health promotion programmes. However, cost and physical health were given high priority across all stakeholders. HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY These findings highlight the importance of incorporating stakeholder-specific preferences when designing and implementing health promotion programmes. Policymakers and programme developers can use these findings to design health promotion programmes that reflect stakeholder preferences and promote health and equity. Introduction The global burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), such as diabetes, cardiovascular diseases and chronic respiratory diseases, is rising, primarily attributed to modifiable risk factors including unhealthy diets, physical inactivity and smoking. Health promotion programmes are becoming invaluable in preventing NCDs. Health promotion programmes encompass a range of approaches to improving health, such as weight loss through dietary control and exercise, 1 2 improving physical 3 and cognitive functions 4 through physical activity, enhancing mental health through mindfulness 5 and facilitating smoking cessation. 6 7 In addition to physical and mental health, promoting social health through social interactions is also important. 8 Although the importance of health promotion programmes is widely acknowledged, their implementation remains limited for several reasons. Preventive care is often perceived as a lower priority than treatment. 9 10 There is also an expectation that such interventions should both improve health and reduce costs, despite evidence that many are not cost-saving. 11 12 In addition, sustaining motivation is challenging when benefits are realised only after a delay. 13 14 The efficacy of health promotion programmes varies across individuals and contexts, making it difficult to determine the most appropriate approach. Individual preferences and contextual factors often influence participation decisions. Therefore, promoting healthy behaviours should incorporate behavioural science approaches that account for these individual-level considerations. Beyond individual participation alone, decisions regarding the selection and implementation of health promotion programmes are often made by multiple stakeholders. However, empirical evidence on stakeholder preferences remains limited across stakeholder groups, including members of the general public, company representatives, local government officials and healthcare professionals. Although several discrete choice experiment (DCE) studies have explored preferences for specific health promotion programmes, such as weight management 15 16 and smoking cessation, 17 few have comprehensively addressed preferences for health promotion as a whole. Existing studies have primarily focused on individual consumers and health-related outcomes, often neglecting non-health benefits such as social participation, productivity or equity. Moreover, the preferences of organisational stakeholders, such as employers and local governments, have rarely been examined, in spite of their critical role in implementing and supporting health promotion programmes. Understanding how various stakeholders value these diverse attributes will support more informed and inclusive decision-making by local governments and companies. 18 Therefore, this study aims to clarify the preferences and relative importance (RI) that each stakeholder group in Japan places on these multidimensional attributes when selecting health promotion programmes. These findings have the potential to inform the development of more acceptable and effective health promotion policies and services. Materials and methods Discrete choice experiment We employed a DCE study design, a type of stated preference method. The DCE method is based on the assumption that individuals make decisions by evaluating and trading off the attributes of available options to select the one that offers the highest perceived benefit or utility. Stated preference methods are particularly useful in fields such as healthcare, where actual behavioural data are often unavailable or difficult to collect. They enable researchers to capture individual preferences through hypothetical choice scenarios. As a result, stated preference methods like DCE are widely used in health economics research, and there is an increasing trend in the number of studies using DCE in healthcare. 19 The study was reported in accordance with good research practices for conjoint analysis ( 20 online supplemental table 3 ). 21 Discrete choice experiment design Candidate attributes and levels were identified through multiple sources, including qualitative input from members of the general public, a survey of local governments, consultations with private-sector companies, expert review and a review of previous DCEs and studies evaluating prevention and health promotion programmes. 921 23 The initial candidate attributes and levels are shown in online supplemental table 2 , which summarises evidence from previous studies. 46 7 9 10 21 25 Specifically, perspectives on factors considered important when selecting health promotion programmes were collected from approximately 100 members of the general public during a public engagement event. In addition, 94 municipalities were surveyed regarding their health promotion initiatives and prioritised considerations. Input was also obtained from two private-sector companies. Feedback on the candidate attributes and levels was provided by experts in health economics, public health, medicine and nursing. Attributes and levels were refined through iterative discussion and feasibility considerations related to experimental design efficiency. Ultimately, seven attributes were selected, each with two or three levels ( table 1 ). Physical health, mental health, social health and productivity were each assigned two levels. Health disparity was specified with three levels. Social health refers to the quality of interpersonal relationships and social interactions and is an important dimension of health alongside physical and mental health. Cost and time attributes were assigned three levels, based on realistic estimates derived from actual health promotion programmes. Respondents were asked to choose between two hypothetical programmes in each choice set; an opt-out option was not included. A pilot test was conducted with 40 participants from the general public to assess the appropriateness of the selected attributes and levels. In addition, respondents’ understanding of the questionnaire was evaluated, and the descriptions were modified accordingly. Questionnaire design We employed a full-profile design, where all attributes were presented in each choice task. This approach is generally regarded as best practice in healthcare DCE studies. A fractional factorial design was used to construct the choice sets, applying a D-efficient design approach. The parameter estimates obtained from the pilot test were used to inform the design. A total of 40 choice scenarios were generated and divided into four blocks, each containing 10 scenarios. Choice tasks were presented in fixed order within each block; however, respondents were randomly assigned to one of four blocks to reduce systematic order effects. To help respondents familiarise themselves with the task, one additional practice scenario was presented at the beginning, resulting in a total of 11 scenarios answered by each respondent. The response to the practice scenario was not included in the analysis. Although health disparity was specified with three levels, the experimental design did not generate choice sets contrasting ‘no change’ with either widening or reduction. As a result, estimation of this attribute was based on the contrast between widening and reduction. 26 The questionnaire consisted of four sections: (1) basic demographic and health information, including age, sex and current medical conditions; (2) current engagement in health promotion activities (eg, exercise, smoking cessation and mindfulness); (3) 11 discrete choice scenarios and (4) a comprehension check. To assess comprehension of the choice scenarios, respondents were asked to rate their understanding using a 5-point Likert scale with the following options: ‘I fully understood the scenarios’, ‘I mostly understood the scenarios’, ‘I partially understood the scenarios’, ‘I hardly understood the scenarios’ or ‘I did not understand the scenarios at all’. Responses to this question were intended to assess respondents’ comprehension of the choice tasks and were used to inform exclusion criteria in the statistical analysis. Respondents were instructed to evaluate the alternatives from the perspective of their stakeholder role. Members of the general public were asked to choose the programme they would prefer to participate in. Company representatives were asked to choose the programme they would implement in their workplace for employees. Local government officials were asked to choose the programme they would introduce at the municipal level for residents. Healthcare professionals were asked to choose the programme they would recommend to their patients. The questionnaire is presented in the Appendix. Sample size consideration Sample size targets were determined in accordance with established methodological guidance for DCEs. Methodological guidance suggests that approximately 200 respondents per analytic group represents a practical benchmark for stable estimation in applied health research. 27 To ensure sufficient precision for both mean preference estimates and heterogeneity parameters and to account for potential exclusions due to inadequate task comprehension, we set a target of approximately 500 respondents per stakeholder group. 26 For the general public, sampling targets were further stratified by age (20–39, 40–64, ≥65 years) and income (<5 million yen, ≥5 million yen, not currently employed) to enhance demographic representativeness. Respondents Respondents were recruited through an online survey company (Rakuten Insight, Inc.). Individuals aged 18 years or older were eligible to participate. Four stakeholder groups were recruited using screening questions administered before the main survey: the general public, company representatives, local government officials and healthcare professionals. Company representatives were limited to individuals in management positions or human resources departments, local government officials were primarily recruited from departments responsible for health promotion and healthcare professionals were defined as respondents working in healthcare occupations. The general public group was recruited separately without occupational restrictions and was stratified by age (20–39, 40–64, ≥65 years) and income (<5 million yen, ≥5 million yen, not currently employed) to enhance demographic representativeness. Respondents were assigned to only one analytic group, and no individual participated in more than one group. Data analysis A mixed logit model was used to estimate respondents’ preferences. In the model, physical health, mental health, social health, productivity and health disparity were included as random parameters and treated as categorical variables. Time and cost were included as continuous variables using numerical values corresponding to the attribute levels (cost levels of 1000, 4000 and 8000 yen coded as 1, 4 and 8; time levels of 1, 3 and 6 hours coded as 1, 3 and 6). The RI of each attribute was calculated by dividing the utility range of each attribute by the total utility range across all attributes. For categorical attributes, the utility range was defined as the difference between the highest and lowest estimated utility coefficients. For continuous attributes (time and cost), the utility range was calculated as the coefficient multiplied by the difference between the highest and lowest attribute levels. The main analysis excluded respondents who answered ‘I hardly understood the scenarios’, or ‘I did not understand the scenarios at all’, in the comprehension check question. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis including these respondents. Informed consent was obtained electronically at the beginning of the survey. Participants were required to indicate their agreement before proceeding and were free to discontinue participation at any time. The research team received only anonymised data from the survey company, and no personally identifiable information was accessible to the investigators. The experimental design was developed using Ngene (V.1.4.0), and the collected data were analysed using STATA (V.17.0). This study was approved by the ethics committee of Kyoto University Graduate School of Medicine (approval number R4604-2). Results Respondents A total of 3870 respondents completed the DCE survey. Of these, 627 respondents (16.2%) reported low comprehension of the choice scenarios. Consequently, 3243 respondents were included in the main analysis ( table 2 ). The median age of respondents was 53 years, and 65.5% were male. Among company representatives, the proportion of male respondents was high (90.8%), and only 35.8% were not currently seeing a doctor. In contrast, among healthcare professionals, the proportion of male respondents was lower (33.9%), and 52.3% were not currently under medical treatment. Relative importance among stakeholders The RI of each stakeholder is shown in figure 1 and online supplemental table 1 . Across all stakeholder groups, cost and physical health were consistently the top two attributes (RI ranges: cost [0.257–0.336], physical health [0.221–0.270]). In addition, their RI values were notably higher than those of the third ranked attributes, such as time (RI: 0.130–0.154) or mental health (RI: 0.143–0.156). Bars indicate the estimated relative importance of each multidimensional attribute by stakeholder group. Attributes are ordered (top to bottom) based on general public ratings. Differences within each stakeholder group suggested variation in prioritisation patterns ( figure 1 ). In descriptive terms, company representatives showed slightly higher RI for physical health (RI: 0.270) than for cost (RI: 0.257), a pattern not observed in the other stakeholder groups. They also appeared to place relatively more importance on time (RI: 0.152) than on mental health (RI: 0.142). Similarly, healthcare professionals showed a pattern in which social health (RI: 0.092) ranked above productivity (RI: 0.070). Discussion Previous DCE studies on health promotion have typically focused on specific programmes or outcomes, such as weight loss, smoking cessation 17 or incentives for engaging children in obesity treatment. 28 Some have explored preferences for health versus non-health goals 29 or health and non-health outcomes. 21 However, to our knowledge, no prior study has comprehensively examined preferences across a broad set of multidimensional attributes, including physical, mental and social health, productivity and health disparities, as done in the present study. 9 The estimated preference model revealed that response options with greater cost or time were less likely to be chosen, consistent with theoretical expectations. The sensitivity analysis yielded similar trends to those observed in the main analysis. However, the absolute values of the coefficients in the sensitivity analysis were smaller than those in the main analysis, suggesting that the responses from respondents excluded in the main analysis may have been less consistent, possibly due to their lower comprehension of the questions. Physical health and cost were consistently among the most important attributes across all stakeholder groups, with substantially higher RI than the remaining attributes. This suggests that tangible health benefits and financial considerations represent core requirements when stakeholders select health promotion programmes. The prominence of physical health is consistent with previous research showing that improvements in physical health are often a central focus of health promotion programmes. Cost was also an important determinant of programme choice across stakeholder groups. Previous studies have shown that financial barriers influence participation in preventive programmes, 30 and even modest cost-sharing can reduce uptake of preventive services. 17 31 Designing programmes with reduced out-of-pocket payments, employer coverage or clear communication of cost–benefit information may therefore facilitate implementation and sustained engagement. 32 33 Despite these shared priorities, the patterns of attribute prioritisation differed across stakeholder groups. Company representatives prioritised time over mental health, while healthcare professionals valued social health more than productivity. These findings highlight the presence of stakeholder-specific preferences and trade-offs, underscoring the importance of incorporating such differences into the design of health promotion programmes to enhance participation and effectiveness. Company representatives showed a distinct prioritisation pattern in the RI of programme attributes compared with other stakeholder groups. In this group, physical health was prioritised over cost. Previous research on workplace health promotion suggests that employers are more likely to support programmes when benefits are visible, relevant to workforce performance and aligned with organisational goals. This pattern indicates that workplace-based implementation strategies may benefit from emphasising measurable health outcomes and demonstrating clear improvements in employees’ physical condition. In addition, time was ranked higher than mental health only among company representatives. Time constraints have been identified as an important barrier to participation in workplace health promotion programmes. 34 35 From the perspective of working populations, limited discretionary time may increase sensitivity to programme duration and scheduling. Short, flexible or on-site interventions that integrate into existing work routines may therefore be particularly suitable for corporate settings. 36 Healthcare professionals uniquely ranked social health above productivity. This pattern may reflect the broader conceptualisation of health commonly used in healthcare settings. The WHO defines health as a state of physical, mental and social well-being, highlighting the importance of social dimensions of health in healthcare practice. In clinical practice, health outcomes are often understood not only in terms of disease control but also in terms of patients’ ability to maintain social participation and interpersonal relationships. Health promotion programmes that incorporate social participation or community engagement may therefore be more acceptable to healthcare professionals when implemented in healthcare or clinical settings. 37 Notably, health disparity ranked lowest across all stakeholder groups. This suggests that considerations related to health disparities may play a less prominent role when stakeholders select health promotion programmes within the choice context. While reducing health inequalities is widely recognised as an important public health objective, the present findings indicate that such considerations may not be strongly reflected in stakeholders’ programme preferences within the context of this choice experiment. This highlights the importance of carefully considering equity implications when designing and implementing health promotion programmes, particularly to ensure that interventions do not unintentionally widen existing health disparities. Strengths and limitations This study has several strengths. First, it adopted a multidimensional approach by incorporating not only physical health but also mental health, social health, productivity, health disparities, cost and time into the DCE. Second, it is one of the few DCE studies to compare the preferences of multiple stakeholder groups: namely, the general public, company representatives, local government officials and healthcare professionals. Third, the large sample size and the exclusion of responses with low comprehension of the scenarios enhanced the validity of the findings by ensuring that the analysis reflected well-understood choices. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the scope of preventive and health promotion programmes is broad, and respondents may have envisioned different types of programmes when responding to the choice scenarios, depending on their background or prior experiences. In addition, some attributes were conceptually broad and may have been interpreted differently across respondents, potentially influencing respondents’ choices. Second, although the attributes were developed based on prior literature and stakeholder input, some potentially relevant aspects of programme participation, such as experiential features (eg, enjoyment or perceived accessibility), were not explicitly included in the choice design, as only a limited number of attributes can be included in a DCE. Third, participants were asked about their current participation in health-related activities before completing the DCE tasks. This may have introduced a priming effect, potentially influencing respondents’ choices. Conclusion This study demonstrated stakeholder preferences and the RI of multidimensional attributes of health promotion programmes comprising physical health, mental health, social health, productivity, health disparities, time and cost. Differences in the prioritisation of these programme attributes were observed across four stakeholder groups: the general public, local government officials, company representatives and healthcare professionals. While cost and physical health consistently showed the highest RI, the RI of other attributes varied by stakeholder group, reflecting multidimensional values and considerations that should be incorporated into the design of health promotion programmes.
